The Forrest Gump Way of Investing: Execution — A Straight Road Walked for Ten Years. Why Do Most People Fail to Finish It?

From "don't watch the market, don't pick stocks, don't time your buys" to "switching on the excess-return engine" — Chueh Yu-shang splits ten years of Forrest Gump investing into a planning volume and an execution volume. This deep read does not repeat the method; it asks three questions: is the straight road really straight? What does "earning a little extra" cost? And which numbers in the marketing can be checked, and which cannot?

PrologueA Straight Road, Walked for Ten Years

On June 4, 2026, Wan Hua Enterprise published a 408-page book of 156,669 words, priced at NT$580. Its title is long: The Forrest Gump Way of Investing: Execution. The words on its cover are short: "Don't watch the market. Don't pick stocks. Don't time your buys. Your tactical handbook for getting rich."[2] Three refusals, plus one promise. This is Chueh Yu-shang's third telling of the same method: Ten Minutes a Year to Make Your Salary Work in 2015, The Forrest Gump Way of Investing in 2020, and now, in 2026, a second revised edition split into two volumes — Planning and Execution — released on the same day.[2][7] One method, ten years of writing, growing thicker each time. This deep read will not ask what the book teaches, but something more basic: why does a road advertised as perfectly straight need 408 pages to describe?

The answer is in the title. "Execution" marks the book's position: it does not repeat the reasoning, but deals with what comes after it — the money is in, and then what? A crash arrives, and then what? Someone else is making money faster than you, and then what? Its reader is not meeting the Forrest Gump method for the first time, but someone who already believes in it and has got stuck putting it into practice.

Chapter OneWho Is Forrest Gump, and Why Him

Forrest Gump is the Forrest Gump of the film Forrest Gump. A man of modest intelligence who ran his way to the Olympics and shrimped his way to a fortune — not because he was clever, but because he never ran off course and never kept changing direction. Chueh borrows the name to describe an investing personality: admit you are not clever, and refuse to play clever people's games.

The author himself is no Forrest Gump. He went to the United States in 1985 and has managed money for 40 years as the manager of the Yu-shang Growth Fund.[2] His self-reported record is handsome: over the eight years to the end of 2016, a compound annual return of 17%; in the first half of 2017, his fund returned 28.86% while the S&P 500 returned 9.2%.[2] Reuters once called him "the unknown kid who beat Wall Street". The publisher's copy goes further, claiming that by buying S&P 500 ETFs he "beat Warren Buffett for 17 consecutive years on cumulative performance".[2]

Three kinds of statement need separating here. The first is the author's self-reported performance: it has dates and a benchmark, but no independent third-party verification, so the reader can only choose to believe it or not. The second is a media label: "the unknown kid who beat Wall Street" is a phrase from Reuters, an evaluation in a sentence, not an audit report. The third is publisher's copy: "beat Buffett for 17 consecutive years" sounds precise, but the copy never says how Buffett's performance was measured, over which period, or net of what fees. Mixed together, the three become the confidence of a bestseller. This deep read takes the position of pulling them apart: method to one side, marketing to the other.

Chapter TwoThree Lines of Defence, Four Life Plans, Five Tools

The book has two parts. Part One covers the underlying logic: Chapter 1 sees through the nature of investing, Chapter 2 presents the Forrest Gump method itself, and Chapter 3 gives a reliable method for operating Taiwan's 0050.[3] Part Two covers advanced practice: Chapter 4 switches on the three key tools for excess returns, Chapter 5 examines the method's weak points and shortcomings, and Chapter 6 says that managing money is also managing the mind.[3] The author sums up the book's skeleton in one line: three psychological lines of defence, four life plans, five sets of practical tools.[2]

The psychological defences come first. The hardest part of the Forrest Gump method was never what to buy, but not moving once you have bought. What do you do when the market falls 50%? The book puts that question first because it knows readers do not lose on the method; they lose to their own hands. The three lines of defence are really one thing at three levels: decide beforehand how much loss you can bear, refuse to be dragged around by the numbers on the screen while it happens, and refuse to throw out the whole method afterwards because of one crash.

Then the life plans. The book ties investing to the stages of a life: when you will need the money decides how much risk you should take now. The order matters — most investing books start with returns; this one starts with life. Money is not the goal; it is the tool that keeps every stage of a life fed.

Finally the practical tools. The most watched items in the Execution volume's toolbox are three: the business-cycle signal, the pairing of QQQ against SPY, and a "third country market-cap ETF".[3] The first two are operations; the third is a direction — the author asks a question that makes every reader sit up: over the next 20 years, where is the next national gold mine, the next "Taiwan of 20 years ago"?[2] There is no standard answer, but the question turns an operating manual into a book about judgement.

Chapter ThreeFrom "Steady Gains" to "A Little Extra"

If the Planning volume is about "how not to lose", the Execution volume is about "how to win a little more". The publisher's copy says it plainly: "The Forrest Gump that is simple, effective and elegant has not changed; what has changed is that the gains have grown." "From steady gains to a little extra." "Switch on the excess-return engine."[2]

The turning point is Chapter 4. The book presents three key tools for switching on excess returns: first, AI meets the business-cycle signal — using artificial intelligence to help read the cycle; second, QQQ against SPY — pairing the aggressive Nasdaq 100 with the steady S&P 500; third, a third country market-cap ETF — looking toward the next emerging market.[3] The book also publishes, for the first time, a verbatim record of the author's "soul-searching interrogation" with AI — a man who has managed money for 40 years sitting down to ask a machine: where does my method still hold, and where is it out of date?[2]

There is a change worth noticing here. The 2020 edition's spirit was "don't watch the market, don't pick stocks, don't time your buys". The 2026 Execution volume keeps the spirit but adds tools — and a business-cycle signal exists precisely to judge when to buy and sell, while AI exists to help with the judging. The more tools there are, the less the method resembles Forrest Gump. That is the most interesting, and the most suspicious, thing about the Execution volume, and Chapter 5 will come back to it.

Chapter 3 also covers how to operate Taiwan's 0050, including the "second-place investment philosophy" and a comparison between the 0050 and the S&P 500.[3] For Taiwanese readers, this is the most practical chapter in the book: it translates an American method into language anyone can use by opening a brokerage app.

Chapter FourWhy It Is Popular: Taiwan's Buy-and-Hold Anxiety

The numbers first. In mid-November 2026, the book reached number 15 on Books.com.tw's site-wide bestseller list.[5] Looking back a year, the 2020 edition of The Forrest Gump Way of Investing took number 10 in Books.com.tw's 2025 business and management category; the same author's The Forrest Gump Way of Saving Tax took number 5 on Eslite's annual list; and the 2020 edition placed in the top 34 of Readmoo's annual top 100 in business and finance.[2] The author's name has appeared on Books.com.tw's annual top 100 bestseller list more than once.[2]

Why this book, and why now? The first reason is Taiwan's buy-and-hold era. Regular investing in the 0050 has become a national habit, yet more and more people hold ETFs and grow anxious: is buying only the 0050 enough? Is it true that QQQ makes more? This book stands exactly at the centre of that anxiety — first it confirms that "steady gains" is right, then it tells you how to earn "a little extra".

The second reason is the author's own structure of credibility. Forty years as a fund manager, a Reuters label, self-reported performance figures, and a memorable name — "Forrest Gump" — combine into an image that is hard to knock down with a single sentence. Readers are not only buying a method; they are buying a person: someone who managed money on Wall Street for 40 years and came back to tell you that simple is enough.

The third reason is timing. The market in 2026 has not been quiet — technology stocks swing sharply, and AI stories change price by the day. Investors have never needed the words "don't move around" more. The book arrived exactly in time to catch that mood.

Chapter FiveThe Cracks in the Execution Volume: Three Things That Don't Add Up

But the more popular a book, the more fairly it deserves to be taken apart. There are three places in the Execution volume that do not add up, or at least are not explained clearly.

The first crack is the contradiction between "don't time your buys" and the business-cycle signal. The Forrest Gump method's trademark is not watching the market, not picking stocks, not timing buys. Yet the whole point of a business-cycle signal is to tell you when to buy and when to sell — "buy at the bottom, sell at the top".[3] The two statements cannot both be true: if the signal really can help you buy at the bottom, then you are timing your buys; if timing buys is wrong, then the signal is meaningless. The book never confronts this contradiction head-on, so the reader has to choose a side alone.

The second crack is the price of QQQ. QQQ's aggression is not free. In 2022, QQQ fell 32.6% for the year while the S&P 500 fell 18.2%.[9] Measured from the peak at the end of 2021, QQQ's maximum drawdown was 35.1%, against 24.5% for the S&P 500.[10] Further back, during the dot-com bust, QQQ fell 83% from its March 2000 peak and did not return to that peak until February 2015 — nearly 15 years in between.[8] "Earning a little extra" only works if you can survive "losing a little extra". The book discusses QQQ's aggression at length, but says far less about drawdowns than about returns. A 50-year-old office worker and a 30-year-old engineer facing the same 35% drawdown are enduring two completely different things.

The third crack is where the numbers come from. The author's self-reported fund performance and the claim of "beating Buffett for 17 consecutive years" come with no public, independently checkable source data. Readers can trust the author, but they cannot verify him. For a book that teaches people to manage money, that is a structural weakness — it asks readers to substitute trust for verification, and trust is the most expensive thing in investing.

To be fair, the book also gets things right. Chapter 5, "The Method's Weak Points and Shortcomings", is the most honest chapter in the book: the author lays out the method's weaknesses himself instead of waiting for readers to find them.[3] The verbatim dialogue with AI is also a rare attempt — letting a 40-year-old method be questioned by a new tool. Those two points make this more than a well-marketed bestseller.

Questions Without Answers Yet

To be honest, the following three things still have no reliable answers, and this article will not pretend otherwise:

  • Actual sales and print runs of the Traditional Chinese edition in Taiwan: number 15 on Books.com.tw is a ranking on a live bestseller list; the publisher has not disclosed actual sales, and they cannot be checked.
  • The source data behind "beating Buffett for 17 consecutive years": the publisher's copy does not state the comparison period, the benchmark, or the fees, so it cannot be independently checked, and this article does not vouch for it.
  • Which ETF the "third country market-cap ETF" actually is, and which country the author has in mind as "the next Taiwan of 20 years ago": the book leaves these to the reader's own judgement, and this article will not guess on its behalf.

EpilogueThe End of the Straight Road Is Not the End

When the book is closed, three distinctions should remain. First, method and marketing must be separated: as a method — regular investing, buying the broad market, not moving around — the Forrest Gump method holds up; but claims like "beat Buffett for 17 consecutive years" and "earn an extra Mercedes" are copy, not evidence.

Second, steadiness and aggression must be separated: "steady gains" and "a little extra" are two different kinds of risk, not two stages of the same road. QQQ is not an upgraded 0050; it is an asset with a different personality. Before deciding whether to go on the attack, ask yourself first: when a 35% drawdown arrives, will I still sleep at night?

Third, straight roads and winding roads must be separated. The most moving thing about this book is that it describes investing as a straight road; the most dangerous thing is in the same place — a road only looks straight because someone has hidden the bends from you. The business-cycle signal, QQQ, the third ETF: these tools are themselves entrances to the bends. A person walking a straight road does not need no map; they need to know who drew every bend on the map, and why.

When Forrest Gump ran, he never looked at a map. He just ran, and kept running. That is, in the end, what this book wants to teach: watch the market less, run more. As for how fast to run and where to run — that is the homework the Execution volume leaves you.

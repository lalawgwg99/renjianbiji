The Alishan Forest Railway: One Railway, One Mountain's Fate

From cypress forests to a tourist railway — how 762 millimetres of narrow gauge rewrote a century in Taiwan's mountains

PrologueBefore the Train Entered the Clouds

Alishan is not a mountain. It is a range, a logging ground, a railway, and a place the Taiwanese spent more than a century learning how to name. In 1906, a Japanese technician saw a red cypress in the fog, 53 metres tall and said to be 3,000 years old[8]; six years later, a train climbed the rails laid for it — and carried away the entire forest around it. This is the story of Alishan, and the most honest chapter of Taiwan's modernisation: we want something, so first we build a road for it; once the road is finished, we discover that what we wanted is no longer there.

This article is about that road. Rails 762 millimetres wide, climbing from Chiayi at 30 metres above sea level all the way to Alishan Station, 2,216 metres above sea level[6]. It was born to carry timber down the mountain, yet it survived the end of logging and now carries people up to look at trees. Sometimes the fate of a place hangs on a track this narrow.

Chapter OneTreasure in the Mountains

In 1899, the Japanese discovered that Alishan held abundant forests[1]. It was an age when an empire was taking inventory of its spoils: Taiwan had been ceded to Japan only four years earlier, and the Governor-General's office was eager to know what on this island was worth money. The answer hid in the clouds — red cypress and Taiwan cypress, fine-grained, rot-resistant timber, prized for shipbuilding, shrines, and government offices alike.

In 1900, railway technician Iida Toyoji went up the mountain to survey whether a railway could be laid, while Ogasawara Fujiro surveyed the forest resources[1]. A survey means: send people first to see how many trees there are, how the road should run, whether the whole thing is worth doing. In 1903, the Governor-General appointed forestry scholar Kawai Tsutaro to plan the development of the Alishan forest and the railway's route[1]. When an empire sets out to cut down a mountain's trees, it does not begin with axes, but with measuring instruments, budgets, and doctoral titles.

One thing must be said clearly first: Alishan was not unclaimed land. These forests were the hunting grounds where the Tsou people had lived for generations, and the very name "Alishan" comes from the Tsou language. But on the developers' maps, a hunting ground was only woodland waiting to be priced. The first step of modernisation is often to translate someone else's home into your own balance sheet.

In November 1906, Ogasawara Fujiro found in the forest the red cypress that would become known as the Sacred Tree: 53 metres tall, 4.66 metres across, 23 metres around, 500 cubic metres of timber, and an estimated age of 3,000 years[8]. The first thing its discoverers did was not to cut it down, but to leave it standing — a tree that had lived 3,000 years was worth more upright than felled: a landmark, a wonder, living proof that this forest was worth a great undertaking. The Japanese deemed it divine, and so the whole forest shared its sacred glow — and none of it escaped the axe.

That same year, the project formally began. The Osaka firm Fujita-gumi took on the works, and in July 1906 construction started between Chiayi and Jhuci[1]. For a trading company to take on laying railway on a cliff, it needed faith in the price of timber — and in 1906, on Alishan, that faith was harder than steel rail.

Chapter TwoNailing a Railway to the Cliff

Laying railway on flat ground is engineering; laying it on Alishan was a gamble with lives. From Chiayi to Alishan, a train must climb more than 2,200 metres. The track cannot go straight; it can only wind, fold back, and circle upward.

The engineers' solution reads today like a poem written in steel. At Dulishan, the line spirals around the peak three times — about 5 kilometres long, climbing 200 metres — and from the carriage you can see the same Jhangnaoliao Station below three times over[6]. Further up, between Pingjhena and the First Switchback, the line turns into a zigzag: the train advances, reverses, and switches back through three sidings, pushed and pulled in turn — the origin of the saying that the Alishan train "hits the wall"[6].

To climb such grades, the railway had to be narrow gauge — 762 millimetres, a maximum gradient of 6.25%, and a minimum curve radius of only 40 metres[6]. The locomotives had to be special too: the Shay steam engines brought from the United States in 1907, with bevel gears and vertical cylinders, were slow and immensely strong, built precisely for steep slopes[1]. The shape of a railway is the temper of its mountain.

But money was harder than the mountain. In 1908, Fujita-gumi, short of funds, abandoned the project[1]. Private capital did its sums and walked away, leaving half a railway drying on the mountainside. In 1910, the Governor-General secured funding from the Japanese Diet, took the works back into public hands, and that October the Chiayi–Jhuci section opened[1]. A colonial government willing to keep burning money for a forest had a simple reason: while the trees still stood on the mountain, the accounts still added up.

In May 1912, logging formally began; on 25 December that year, the Chiayi–Erwanping section opened, 66.6 kilometres long, and the next day the first cypress logs were sent down to Chiayi for display[2][4][7]. On 14 March 1914, the line was extended to Zhaoping, completing the 71.9-kilometre main line[4]. From Chiayi to Alishan, the train passes through tropical, warm-temperate, and temperate forest zones, driving on into a sea of clouds[4].

Chiayi below the mountain changed with it. In 1913, the Chiayi Timber Factory was built; in December 1914, its log pond and sawmill were completed, and logs from Alishan were sawn into timber here and shipped by rail across Taiwan and to Japan[2]. Chiayi turned from a plain town into the "Timber Capital": its log pond, the "Cedar Pond," was so large it was counted among the Eight Views of Chiayi, and timber merchants, sawyers, and log haulers gathered around the station into a complete industrial settlement[7]. A railway rewrites not only the scenery on the mountain, but the identity of a city below it.

Chapter ThreeThe Saw and the City

Once the railway opened, Alishan entered its busiest and cruellest age. Between 1912 and 1960, it was Taiwan's largest logging ground[3]. Every day, trains carried logs down the mountain and carried workers, food, and tools up. The logging camps worked like a factory that never stopped, the saws sounding from dawn to dusk. In 1915, the Tashan Line was completed, pushing the cutting toward Mount Tashan[1].

The men cutting the trees knew in their hearts. When the cutting grew too fierce, accidents followed: workers fell strangely ill, and some said the mountain god was angry. In 1935, the logging ground raised a Tree Spirit Pagoda in the forest, to honour the spirits of the felled trees[8]. A pagoda honours not a god, but a debt people could not speak aloud.

A man will climb a mountain to cut a tree for its price — and still raise a pagoda for it, so that he can sleep at night.

The railway was built to carry timber, but people found their own way aboard. From 1918, at the request of residents along the line, freight cars were converted to carry passengers; in 1920, the colonial government began building proper passenger cars and running mixed passenger-freight trains[4]. The logging railway grew a passenger service: mountain people rode down to see doctors, buy rice, and marry; people from the plains rode up to see the Sacred Tree, the sunrise, and the sea of clouds. A road for timber slowly became a road for people.

In 1945, Japan was defeated, and the Alishan logging ground passed to the Forestry Bureau[1]. The regime changed; the saws did not. The Nationalist government kept cutting until 1963, when the Alishan ground stopped direct logging, and the Chiayi Timber Factory stopped sawing in 1964[7]. It was not a sudden awakening of conscience, but the fact that the old-growth cypress was nearly gone[3]. A forest that took a mountain 3,000 years to grow, humanity cut down in 50. In the year the cutting stopped, Alishan did not celebrate; it only fell quiet — quiet as a factory with its lights switched off.

One detail is worth remembering: the more trees cut around the Sacred Tree, the likelier it was to be struck by lightning. On 7 June 1956, the 3,000-year-old red cypress was hit, and lightning fire hollowed out its heart[8]. It did not fall at once. But from that day on, it was already dead.

Chapter FourWhen the Sacred Tree Fell

A tree can be dead and still stand for 40 years. After the lightning strike of 1956, the Forestry Bureau planted young red cypresses on top of the Sacred Tree, so that it still looked lush and alive[8]. Visitors came, took photographs, marvelled, and left, never knowing that the "living" Sacred Tree before them had long been hollow inside. It was Alishan's gentlest and most honest lie: we could not bear to let the symbol fall, so we dressed it up and let it keep standing in the scenery.

Meanwhile, Alishan was changing its trade. The trees were gone, but the railway remained. In 1984, the "Alishan Express" began running, covering the journey in just 3 hours and 15 minutes; in 1986, the Jhushan Line opened — 6.25 kilometres long, the first high-mountain railway built by the Taiwanese themselves, carrying visitors up to watch the sunrise[6]. Sunrise, sea of clouds, sunset, Sacred Tree, and railway came to be known as the "Five Wonders" of Alishan[3]. In 2001, the Alishan area was designated a national scenic area[11]. A place that cut trees had gone into the business of selling scenery.

Fenqihu is the most telling footnote of this transformation. This mountain town at 1,403 metres began as only a midway station, but because trains stopped there for a long time around midday, crew and passengers all ate there — and over time a distinctive railway lunchbox culture grew up[10]. A lunchbox becomes famous not because the lunchbox itself is extraordinary, but because a train stops there on time every day, giving a whole village its livelihood. That is the plainest magic of infrastructure: where the train stops, people's lives take root.

On 1 July 1997, at 10:05 in the morning, after days of heavy rain, one-third of the Sacred Tree snapped and toppled onto the forest railway[9]. That day, Hong Kong had just returned to China, and the world's headlines were all about another city's fate; but on Alishan, what truly fell was a tree that had stood for 3,000 years. The Forestry Bureau deliberated for a year, and on 29 June 1998 decided to lay the Sacred Tree to rest where it lay[3]. On the day it was laid down, no one celebrated and no one protested — everyone understood that this was not a tree's funeral, but the full stop of an era.

In 2006, the Sianglin Sacred Tree was chosen by public vote as the second-generation Sacred Tree, 45 metres tall and 12.3 metres around[3]. People need a new Sacred Tree not because the old one was not good enough, but because a place cannot be without a symbol. Symbols fall; the human need for symbols does not.

Chapter FiveFifteen Years of Waiting

The Sacred Tree had fallen, but the railway still ran. Then, on 8 August 2009, Typhoon Morakot struck Alishan, inflicting 421 points of damage along the line and bringing the whole railway to a halt[5]. A railway that had run for 97 years was broken into pieces overnight.

Rebuilding proved harder than building. In 2015, with full reopening in sight, Typhoon Dujuan collapsed Tunnel No. 42, leaving the main line running only between Chiayi and Shihzihlu[5]. For years, the fate of the Alishan railway hung in the balance: to repair or not to repair? Was a railway that no longer carried timber worth so much money to fix? The objections were easy to imagine — the highway had long since opened, and there were faster, cheaper ways up the mountain.

But those who wanted it repaired were not thinking about transport; they were thinking about identity. In 2003, the Alishan Forest Railway was selected by the Council for Cultural Affairs as a first-category potential World Heritage site[4]; on 9 July 2019, the "Alishan Forestry and Railway Cultural Landscape" was designated by the Ministry of Culture as Taiwan's first Important Cultural Landscape[7]. Once a railway is recognised as a cultural landscape, it is no longer merely transport, but a vessel of collective memory. To repair it is to repair who we are.

There was also an interlude: in 2006, the Forestry Bureau contracted part of the railway and recreation area to the private Hungtu company, which took over in 2008; but the privatisation failed within two years, and in 2010 the Bureau ended the contract and took the railway back[1][4]. A century-old railway can be handed to the market, but the market counts money while the railway carries memory — two ledgers that never balance. On 1 July 2018, the dedicated Alishan Forest Railway and Cultural Heritage Office was inaugurated, giving the railway, for the first time, an institution to match its standing[5].

On 28 August 2019, the new Tunnel No. 42 passed its environmental review; on 17 May 2024, the tunnel was completed; on 6 July 2024, the Alishan Forest Railway reopened along its full length, after 15 years[5]. That day, the first scheduled train left Chiayi for Alishan carrying a full load of 85 passengers[12]. Fifteen years, 421 points of damage, one new tunnel — a whole society spent 15 years so that a small train could climb the mountain again. By any accounting it is a loss, except by one other kind of arithmetic: some things are worth keeping even at a loss.

EpilogueThe Mountain Remains, and the Train Still Runs

Today, the trains of Alishan no longer carry logs, but people who have come to see the mountain. Among the carriages is the "Formosensis," a sightseeing train built of cypress and praised as a moving art gallery; and Locomotive No. 21, retired for more than 40 years, has been restored and whistles again, hauling the "Hinoki to Chiayi" service between Chiayi and Beimen[5][12]. A tree cut down and made into a carriage, carrying people back up the mountain to look at trees — a reconciliation 100 years late.

The story of Alishan is, in miniature, the story of Taiwan's modernisation: first an empire built a road for resources, then a state cut trees for development, then a society repaired a railway for memory. At each stage, people believed they were doing something entirely rational — developing, building, preserving — yet looking back, every step carried the blind spot of its age. It took us 100 years to learn: a mountain is not a warehouse, a railway is not a pipe, and a Sacred Tree is not scenery. They are part of who we are.

The train that opened in 1912 carried cypress down the mountain; the train that reopened in 2024 carries people up it. The direction has reversed, and so has the meaning. Once we went up the mountain to carry the mountain away; now we go up to find ourselves again. As long as the train still runs, this story is not yet finished.

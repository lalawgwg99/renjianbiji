Atomic Habits: 1% Better Every Day, 37 Times Better in a Year — Is That Really True?

A 2018 book on habits sold 25 million copies on the strength of one equation. It shrank change into a two-minute daily act — and dressed arithmetic up as science. This deep read takes its most famous formula apart.

PrologueA Baseball Accident That Produced a Habit Bible

In his second year of high school, James Clear was struck in the face by a baseball bat during a game, so badly injured that he was placed in a medically induced coma.[6] When he woke, he did not return to the field at once. He rebuilt himself from the smallest things: a little rehab each day, a little reading each day, a little more sleep each day. Years later he captained his university baseball team, and later still became the world's best-known writer on habits.[5] On October 16, 2018, his first book, Atomic Habits, was published by Avery in the United States;[3] eight years later it had sold 25 million copies worldwide, licensed in more than 60 languages, the bestselling habit book of the decade.[6]

The book's success is itself a demonstration of its theory: no miracle, only 1% compounding daily. But that sentence is also where the book most deserves a close read — "1% better every day, 37 times better in a year" sounds like science, but it is really a maths problem. This deep read asks: what is this book genuinely good at, and where does it overpromise?

Chapter OneThe Mathematics of 1% Better

The most famous sentence in the book is an equation: 1.01 to the power of 365 is roughly 37.78. Improve by 1% every day and you will be 37 times better in a year; decline by 1% every day and you will fade to near zero.[4] The power of the equation is not mathematical but psychological: it turns "change" from a distant, enormous project into a small thing you can do today. Readers need not believe they can become a different person, only that tomorrow can be 1% better than today.

But honestly, the equation is a metaphor, not an experiment. No study shows that a person's abilities can grow at a steady 1% a day like compound interest, and none shows that after 365 days you land exactly on 37 times. Life is not a bank account: progress plateaus, reverses, and gets interrupted. The author knows this, which is why the book spends so much time on the "plateau of latent potential": results often appear suddenly after a long delay, and before that moment you feel as if you are standing still.[9]

Even so, the equation remains the book's most successful device, because it defeats change's greatest enemy — not laziness, but giving up when no results are visible. Thirty-seven times gives readers a reason to wait through the long stretch when nothing seems to happen. Whether the number is literally true matters less than that it gets people moving — and movement itself is where habits begin.

Chapter TwoThe Four Laws: Make Good Habits Obvious

The core of the book is a set of "four laws of behaviour change," built on the four steps of the habit loop: cue, craving, response, reward.[6] Each step gets a design rule: make the cue obvious, make the craving attractive, make the response easy, make the reward satisfying. To break a bad habit, invert the laws: make it invisible, unattractive, hard, and unsatisfying.

The laws work because they turn "discipline" from a moral problem into a design problem. To read more, do not rely on willpower — put the book beside your pillow. To scroll less, do not swear an oath — put the phone in another room. The author's slogan is: you do not need to become more disciplined, you need to design an environment in which good habits happen more easily. That idea is the most liberating sentence in the book.

Beyond the laws, the book offers a string of small tools: habit stacking — attach a new habit to an old one, "after breakfast, I will meditate for one minute"; the two-minute rule — shrink any new habit to under two minutes, aim to start, not to be perfect; never miss twice — missing once is fine, never miss two days in a row.[9] What these tools share is that none of them asks you to become a better person; they only ask you to lower the bar until failure is nearly impossible.

Chapter ThreeSystems Beat Goals

The most quoted line in the book is: "You do not rise to the level of your goals. You fall to the level of your systems." A goal is "lose 10 kilos"; a system is "walk 30 minutes a day, skip starch at dinner." A goal is "write a book"; a system is "write 500 words every morning." Winners and losers share the same goals; only their systems differ. That distinction is the book's weightiest insight.

Paired with systems is the second core idea: identity. The author writes that every action is a vote for the type of person you wish to become: each time you choose to exercise, you vote for "I am a person who exercises"; each time you write a page, you vote for "I am a writer." Habits are not a means to a goal but proof of who you are. That framing lifts habit from technique to identity, and gives persistence an emotional anchor.

Together, these two chapters explain why the book outlasts ordinary motivational writing: it offers not only methods but a way of seeing yourself. Readers are not just buying four laws; they are buying a new self-understanding — I am not a person who lacks willpower, only a person who has not yet designed a good system.

Chapter FourWhy It Sold 25 Million Copies

The numbers first. The English edition appeared in October 2018, 320 pages;[3] the Traditional Chinese edition followed on June 1, 2019, from Fine Press, translated by Tsai Shih-wei;[4] by 2025 global sales had passed 25 million copies, licensed in more than 60 languages, with combined print and ebook sales in Taiwan past 800,000 copies and more than 237 printings.[6] On Books.com.tw's annual reading chart it topped the overall list four years running (2020 to 2023);[6] in 2026 it was still number 6 on Eslite's annual bestseller list.[10]

Why has it sold for so long? The Fine Press editor gave two reasons: first, the methods genuinely work, so readers recommend it by word of mouth; second, it is "versatile" — it applies to every area of life.[5] Behind both reasons lies one structure: the book is choosy about no one. Students use it to study, salespeople to win clients, parents to raise children, and even an Olympic marathon champion said it taught him the value of tiny habits and discipline.[5] A book that serves so many people at once can do so because it addresses a difficulty everyone shares: wanting to change, and always giving up halfway.

There is one more reason: the author himself. Clear has written a weekly newsletter since 2012, building hundreds of thousands of subscribers, so the book launched with an audience already waiting. His style also fits the moment: short sentences, lists, concrete steps, no long essays. The book reads less like a book than an operating manual — and an operating manual is exactly what busy modern readers are most willing to buy.

Chapter FiveWhat the 37 Times Leaves Out

But the more successful the book, the fairer it is to ask: how much of it is actually new? The honest answer is: not much. The habit loop was already written about in Charles Duhigg's The Power of Habit in 2012 — cue, routine, reward, in three steps; Clear himself says he picked up where Duhigg left off, expanding three steps into four and adding the four laws.[7] Many of the book's ideas are a synthesis and repackaging of existing behavioural science, not the author's own discoveries.

A deeper criticism is that the book leans too hard on the individual and too lightly on structure. Telling someone who works 12 hours a day and commutes for two more to "improve 1% daily" is cruel; telling someone who lives in a neighbourhood full of fast-food outlets to "design your environment" is a luxury. Habits matter, but attributing the whole of a life's outcomes to habits turns structural problems into personal ones — poverty is not caused by a lack of good habits, and exhaustion is not caused by a lack of discipline. The book barely touches this layer.

Finally, the author's identity is worth knowing: Clear is a writer and speaker, not an academic researcher.[2] That is not a flaw — his strength has always been translation and synthesis — but readers should know they are reading a practical manual, not a scientific monograph. Thirty-seven times is arithmetic, not a conclusion; the four laws are tools, not theorems. Knowing that does not make the book useless — it lets you use it more wisely.

Questions Without Answers Yet

To be honest, the following two things still have no reliable answers, and this article will not pretend otherwise:

  • Exact sales and printings in Taiwan: 800,000 copies and 237 printings are figures reported in 2025; whether they have kept growing since then has not been disclosed by the publisher, so the latest sales cannot be checked.
  • The precise global sales figure: the publisher's wording is past 25 million copies, while the author's own website claims past 30 million; the two counts come from different dates, so this article follows the publisher's figure and does not reconcile them.

EpilogueHabits Are Not Magic, They Are Votes

When the book is closed, what should remain is not the number 37 but a quieter idea: every small action is a ballot cast for the person you want to become. One vote changes nothing; a thousand votes change everything. The book's real value is not what it invented but that it shrank "change" from a project requiring courage into a task requiring two minutes.

As for the 37 times, leave it on the cover. It is a slogan, not a promise. The real compounding is not in the equation but in the small thing you are willing to do every day — today, tomorrow, and the day after. Habits are not magic. Habits are votes, and the result of a vote is only known on counting day.

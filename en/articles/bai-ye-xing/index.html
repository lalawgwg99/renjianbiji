Journey Under the Midnight Sun: Nineteen Years in a Night Without a Sun

Keigo Higashino's 1999 mystery novel, published by Shueisha on August 5, 1999. After the author's death on July 23, 2026, it returned to the bestseller lists — No. 3 on Books.com.tw's e-book chart and a place on Readmoo's 2026 annual bestseller list. A 1973 pawnshop murder in Osaka binds Ryoji and Yukiho together for 19 years. This deep read asks: why does a novel that refuses to explain its protagonists' inner lives still hold readers 27 years later?

The Same Era · 1999

  • September 21, 1999A little over a month after this book was published, Taiwan was struck by the 921 earthquake: at 1:47 a.m., a magnitude 7.3 quake shook the whole island, killing 2,415 people and destroying 51,711 buildings outright. While Japanese readers were reading a novel about a 19-year manhunt, Taiwanese readers were living through 102 seconds that moved mountains.
  • December 20, 1999At the end of the same year this book came out, sovereignty over Macao passed from Portugal to China, and the Macao Special Administrative Region was established. In the year a novel about a sun that never rises was born, the world was handing over sovereignty and tidying up the end of the century.

PrologueThe Author Is Gone; the Book Is Back

In the early hours of July 23, 2026, Keigo Higashino died of colorectal cancer. He was 68. On July 27, Kodansha announced his death. That day, sales of his books in Chinese at Books.com.tw exceeded their total for the entire month of July up to that point, and e-book sales ran 16.5 times the previous day's. Journey Under the Midnight Sun climbed to third on the e-book bestseller list, The Devotion of Suspect X to fourth, and five of the top ten were Higashino's.[8][9] A novel published in 1999 returned to the most visible shelf in the bookstore on the day its author left. Readmoo's preview of its 2026 annual bestseller list put Journey Under the Midnight Sun and The Devotion of Suspect X in the same segment, titled "The Suddenly Departed Higashino."[6][7]

This deep read will not ask who the murderer is. That is the puzzle on the surface of a mystery novel, and it is clear from the first page that someone is dead. The question here is different: why can a novel from 27 years ago still be read in one sitting, all 672 pages of it? And why has its coldness still not worn off?

Chapter OneIt Went Viral Because It Explains Nothing

The numbers first. Byakuyakō was serialised in Shueisha's Subaru magazine from January 1997 to January 1999, and published as a single volume on August 5, 1999, 506 pages.[1][2] By November 2005 it had sold about 550,000 copies in Japan; around the television adaptation in January 2006 it passed 1 million; by December 2010 it had passed 2 million.[1] It became famous slowly, and stayed famous for a long time — long enough to still be selling after its author's death. On Kingstone's autumn 2026 literary bestseller chart, the single-volume classic edition ranked 58th, and The Devotion of Suspect X 22nd.[13]

The story starts simply. In 1973, in an abandoned building in Osaka, a pawnshop owner is murdered. The victim's son, Ryoji Kirihara, and the suspect's daughter, Yukiho Nishimoto, go their separate ways for the next 19 years.[1] Detective Sasagaki Junzo follows the case for 19 years, past his retirement and beyond. The reader follows him — not chasing the murderer, but chasing a question: what, exactly, is between these two people?

The first reason it caught on is the way it is written. Nowhere in the book is there a single passage of the protagonists' inner thoughts. What Ryoji is thinking, what Yukiho is thinking — Higashino does not write a word of it. Like Sasagaki, the reader has to piece it together from what bystanders saw: what a neighbour noticed, what a classmate remembered, what a business partner sensed.[3] A mystery novel usually hands the reader clues. This book hands the reader the shadows of clues. You think you understand, and in the next chapter someone else's account knocks down the judgement you just built.

The second reason is that it flatters nobody. This is not a book you finish feeling warm, nor one you finish feeling that justice has been done. You finish it sitting still, unsure whom to pity and unsure whom to blame. For 27 years, readers have been arguing about this book — and a book that can keep people arguing for 27 years needs no further proof that it is famous.

Chapter TwoThe Sun and the Shadow

The title deserves a careful look first. "Byakuya," the white night, is a natural phenomenon: the long twilight before sunrise and after sunset.[1] The sun never truly rises, and never truly sets. The whole book lives inside that hour: Ryoji walks in the shadow, Yukiho walks in the light. For one of them to succeed, the other must first disappear; the brighter one lives, the deeper the other must hide.

This is the book's most brilliant device, and its cruellest. Ryoji and Yukiho are not lovers, not siblings, not simply accomplices. They are symbiotic: like the sun and its shadow, neither can exist without the other, and neither can stand in the light. From the first page to the last, Higashino never writes a scene of the two of them alone together, never writes a single line of dialogue between them — their relationship exists only where nobody can see it. Only at the end does the reader understand that the real protagonist of this book is neither Ryoji nor Yukiho, but the invisible thread between them.

Sasagaki Junzo is the conscience of this book, and the reader's stand-in. He has no genius for solving cases, only obsession: 19 years on one case, past retirement, chasing a truth long after the people involved have moved on. He stands for a belief that is nearly extinct — that the truth is worth chasing, even when catching it changes nothing. The reader walks those 19 years with him, not to watch him solve the case, but to watch an ordinary man spend 19 years fighting against forgetting.

To be honest about the limits: this deep read will not lay out the ending. How to read the final scene is still argued over by critics, and Higashino himself never gave a definitive interpretation. Half of this book's power lives in what it leaves unsaid; spelling out the ending as a selling point would spend the most valuable thing it owns on the reader's behalf.

Chapter ThreeWhere the Idea Came From: A Mystery Writer Who Was an Engineer

Keigo Higashino was born in Osaka on February 4, 1958. He studied electrical engineering at Osaka Prefecture University, then worked as an engineer at the auto-parts supplier Nippon Denso, writing fiction after work. In 1985, After School won the 31st Edogawa Rampo Prize; he was 27, and he quit his job the following year to write full time.[12] In 1999 he won the 52nd Mystery Writers of Japan Award for Naoko — the same year Journey Under the Midnight Sun was published.[12] The Devotion of Suspect X came out in 2005 and won the 134th Naoki Prize and the Honkaku Mystery Award the following year.[12]

The engineering background shaped how this book is written. Higashino is not a writer who works by inspiration; he works by structure. Journey Under the Midnight Sun was serialised as linked short stories and then rebuilt as a novel — which explains why the book reads like a row of slices: each chapter changes the bystander, each chapter jumps forward a few years, and time slips past between chapters for the reader to join up.[1] The strength of this method is that nobody ever stands in a position of knowing everything; the truth only ever shows one corner. The cost is that the reader must do the joining themselves, assembling 19 years into one whole person.

The book also marks Higashino's turn from classic whodunits toward social mystery. His early work was campus mysteries built on tightly constructed puzzles; from Journey Under the Midnight Sun onward, he began writing about people and society: desire, class, poverty, education — what kind of environment a child grows up in decides what kind of adult they become. The story runs from 1973 to 1992, from Japan's economic rise to the eve of the bubble's collapse, and the pawnshops, cram schools, property deals, and luxury boutiques in the book are all slices of that era. The mystery is the shell. The era is what is underneath.

Chapter FourThree Adaptations, Three White Nights

Journey Under the Midnight Sun has been adapted many times; the two largest were both in Japan. From January 12 to March 23, 2006, TBS aired an 11-episode drama, with Takayuki Yamada as Ryoji and Haruka Ayase as Yukiho. It averaged a 12.28% rating — 14.2% for the first episode, 14.1% for the finale — and won four prizes at the 48th Television Drama Academy Awards: best drama, best actor, best supporting actress, and best supporting actor.[10] The novel's sales passed 1 million copies around the same broadcast.[1] On January 29, 2011, the film version opened in Japan, directed by Yoshihiro Fukagawa, starring Maki Horikita and Kengo Kora, 149 minutes long, selected for the Panorama section of the 61st Berlin International Film Festival.[11]

Every adaptation faces the same problem: the novel's power comes from what it does not write, while film and television must show everything. A protagonist whose inner life has been deliberately omitted has to be played through an actor's eyes and movements. The drama chose the more emotional route; the film chose a colder one. Both choices are right, and both are incomplete — because the original never offered a standard answer. Genya, published in 2004 and regarded as a sister work to Journey Under the Midnight Sun, was adapted by WOWOW in 2010; the two works together complete the world Higashino built.[11]

The adaptations also explain why this book has stayed popular in Taiwan for so long. Readers arrived in waves: watch the drama first, then find the book; read the book first, then find the drama. The Traditional Chinese edition was published by Solo Press (Unitas) on October 8, 2007, translated by Liu Zi-jun — 392 pages for the first volume, 352 for the second. A classic-return set followed in January 2018, 744 pages; a single-volume classic edition followed on January 4, 2024, 672 pages.[3][4][5] One book, three packagings, selling for 19 years — not on marketing, but on a loop in which the adaptations and the original keep feeding each other.

Chapter FiveWhat It Gets Right, What It Misses

Three things are right. First, the structure is ruthless. A 19-year span, told in slices, each chapter revealing only one corner — and only at the end does the reader see that every apparently unrelated event was connected to the same thread. Second, the restraint is ruthless. Leaving the inner life unwritten makes the reader want it more; leaving the dialogue unwritten makes the silence louder than speech. Third, the sense of era is exact. From 1973 to 1992, the changes in Japanese desire and class are all hidden in the supporting characters' lives — someone rises on property, someone is drained by cram schools, someone finds their place under the lights of a luxury boutique. The mystery is the shell. The era is what is underneath.

Three things should also be said plainly. First, the moral discomfort. This book asks the reader to sympathise with criminals — long-term, systematic crime — and Yukiho's "success" is built on other people's ruin. Halfway through, the reader has to ask: who exactly am I rooting for? There is no standard answer, and not every reader wants to be asked. Second, the portrait of Yukiho risks objectification. Her beauty, her methods, her climb upward read too easily as a "femme fatale" template; defenders call it deliberate social criticism, critics call it writing a woman's success as a sin. Third, the length. Inside 672 pages, some side plots exist only to move time forward, and they feel repetitive; readers who like a fast pace will find the middle slow. Fourth, and most important: there is no redemption in this book. From the first page to the last, nobody is forgiven and nobody is set free. That is its strength and its weakness — it is honest, but it offers no comfort.

The contrast with Higashino's later work makes the point sharper. The Miracles of the Namiya General Store is about warmth, about people saving each other; Journey Under the Midnight Sun is about symbiosis, about two people dragging each other down. One lets you believe the world can get better; the other lets you believe the world was always like this. Higashino grew warmer and warmer afterwards, but he never wrote a second book this cold. Cold enough that, 27 years later, readers are still arguing about it.

Questions Without Answers Yet

To be honest, four things still have no reliable answer, and this piece will not pretend otherwise:

  • Actual sales of the Traditional Chinese edition: the Taiwan publisher has not released figures. The Japanese "over 2 million copies" comes from publisher copy and the Japanese Wikipedia, and the original statistics behind it have not been checked.
  • The exact rank of Journey Under the Midnight Sun on Readmoo's 2026 annual bestseller list: the list page gives no ranks, and the preview video names the book without ranking it. This piece only confirms that it is on the list.
  • Box-office takings for the 2011 film in Japan: overseas tallies put the worldwide gross at about US$4.59 million, but no reliable figure for the Japanese domestic gross could be found, so none is quoted here.
  • How to read the novel's final scene: critics still disagree, and Higashino himself never gave a definitive interpretation. This piece will not decide for them.

EpilogueThe End of the White Night

A white night is not a night. Night always passes, and the sun always rises; a white night does neither. The sun never rises and never sets, and the world stays in a long twilight. Ryoji and Yukiho walked inside it for 19 years, until even they could no longer tell whether it was day or night.

In July 2026, the author left, and the book came back. This is not nostalgia. It is the book finally meeting readers who can understand it — readers in Taiwan in 2026 who have lived through a market crash and a turn of the era, and who are beginning to understand what it means when the sun will not rise. Close the book, and the question comes back to the reader: if you could only walk inside a white night, would you choose to be the sun, or the shadow? Higashino never answered. He wrote 19 years, and left the choice to you. That is enough.

Nordic Time: Happiness Is Not Faster, It Is Just Right

PrologueA Book That Starts with a "Useless Office Worker"

On October 29, 2025, Happiness Culture published a picture book of 224 pages. Its author, Inko Higurashi, describes herself as a former "useless office lady": a graduate of Waseda University's School of Science and Engineering who landed a job at a major Tokyo corporation, only to find herself exhausted — introverted, unsure of herself, always overthinking. Then she left, moved to Denmark, stayed for 7 years, and wrote this book, Nordic Time: What the World's Happiest Country Taught Me.[1][2] A little more than a year later, it reached No. 4 on Eslite's 2026 annual bestseller chart, behind Taiwan Travelogue, The Almanack of Naval Ravikant, and Rewire.[5] This deep read asks three questions: what exactly is it selling, that readers in Taiwan are buying? Is the Nordic time in its pages a real life, or a carefully chosen postcard? If we bring that way of time back to Taiwan, who pays the bill?

The facts first. The Japanese original was published by Daiwa Publishing in October 2023, 185 pages.[3] The Traditional Chinese edition, translated by Lin Mei-chi, followed on October 29, 2025, from Happiness Culture: 224 pages, priced at NT$398, the e-book running to 26,772 characters.[1][2] The photographs are by Saori Kojima, a Japanese photographer living in Northern Europe,[4] and the book is built around "five kinds of Nordic time": self-care, living by your own compass, single-tasking, creative exploration, and hygge.[4][11] Each chapter ends with an "everyday proposal": light a candle, drink a coffee, watch a sunset, go for a walk.[4] This is not a book about how to succeed, but about how to stop — and the question is what stopping actually costs.

Chapter OneSeven Years from Fukuoka to Denmark

Inko Higurashi was born in Fukuoka Prefecture, Japan, and graduated from Waseda University's School of Science and Engineering. After graduation she was "lucky" enough to join a large Tokyo corporation, but introverted, unsure of herself, and always overthinking, she lived for years as a "useless office lady."[4] That self-portrait is the foundation of the whole book: the author does not teach happiness from the winner's podium, but records, as an escapee, how another way of living caught her.

Exhausted in body and mind, she decided to leave her job and her old life behind and move to Northern Europe. After studying at a Nordic business school and a design school, she now works as a freelance creator.[4] There is a turn worth noticing here: she writes not about conquering Tokyo, but about leaving it. The book begins not with discipline, but with a plane ticket.

After moving, she met a book: Meik Wiking's The Little Book of Hygge: Danish Secrets to Happy Living. It taught her how Danes cherish time that is "comfortable and calm."[4][11] From then on, what she was seeing had a name: hygge. There is no exact Japanese equivalent, she writes; the closest word is "hokkori" — that warm, soft, relaxed feeling inside.[12]

One more thing should not be missed: the author writes as a freelancer,[4] not as a Danish office worker. The Nordic time she observes is seen through a freelancer's eyes. That perspective makes the life in the book look especially unhurried — her own time was already more flexible than most people's. Keep that in mind when the book talks about "copying it in Taiwan," or the picture will come out distorted.

Chapter TwoFive Kinds of Time, One Day Cut into Three

The skeleton of the book is simple. The author sorts the time Nordic people deliberately set aside in everyday life into five kinds: self-care, living by your own compass, single-tasking, creative exploration, and hygge.[11] Behind each kind sits one small action: open the day with a personal ritual, work out what really matters, do one thing at a time, keep a little playfulness in the day, light a candle and sit with the people close to you.[4][11]

The prologue hands out the book's catchiest numbers first: the 8-8-8 rule — cut the 24 hours of a day into three equal parts — 8 hours of work, 8 hours of sleep, 8 hours of your own time.[12] The book also says that in the Nordic countries, leaving work at 4 p.m. is normal, and that people even take a whole month off in July and August, like a summer holiday.[12] These numbers are the book's trademark, and also the part most easily misread.

Of the five kinds of time, the weightiest is the third: single-tasking. Her colleague told her: "Because we work seriously during that time, of course we finish."[12] That sentence states the book's whole position: slowness is not an excuse for laziness, but the result of focus. Doing one thing at a time sounds like a platitude, yet in an age of 10 open browser tabs, it has become almost a luxury.

The other two kinds of time are the book's vocabulary lesson. Sweden's fika is the precious time of sitting with friends or colleagues over coffee or tea and something sweet, sharing what is on your mind and the small events of the day;[12] Denmark's hygge is lighting a candle, watching the sunset, and sitting at ease with someone close, feeling a deep calm even in silence.[11] In Chapter Five, the author adds Iceland's Jólabókaflóð, literally the "Christmas book flood": in the months before Christmas, new books flood out,[12] and on December 24 people unwrap books as gifts and read them late into the night with hot chocolate. The tradition traces back to 1944, when the Journal of Books (Bókatíðindi) was first published; import restrictions after the Second World War made paper easier to get than other gifts, and books became the default Christmas present.[10]

Chapter ThreeIt Sold Because Taiwan Is Tired

First, the record. On Eslite's 2026 annual bestseller chart, Nordic Time ranked No. 4. The top three were Taiwan Travelogue, The Almanack of Naval Ravikant, and Rewire; No. 5 was Counselling for Toads, No. 6 Atomic Habits, No. 7 The Let Them Theory.[5] Nine of the top 10 are about self-adjustment: how to live with yourself, how to let yourself off the hook, how to stop burning out inside.[5] This book is not an exception. It is one slice of readers' mood in Taiwan in 2026.

The timing was right. The Traditional Chinese edition came out on October 29, 2025,[2] just in time for the year-end gift season and the following year's annual chart. And its tone — "you don't have to keep holding on," "happiness is not about being faster, but about being just right"[4] — caught exactly the sentence readers most wanted to hear that year. Huang Shan-liao's "getting better, slowly" line won Eslite's annual No. 1 for four straight years; by 2026 it had turned into the looser feeling of "no need to force it."[5] Nordic Time stands on that turning point, and what it sells is not a method, but permission.

There is another reason that is often missed: this book is easy to buy. At 224 pages, full of photographs,[2][4] with one proposal per chapter, a reader can open it for 10 minutes and walk away with a sentence and one thing to do tonight. Its real competitors are not other self-help books, but the endless short videos on the reader's phone — answered with the slowness of paper.

Honesty requires a note: how many copies it actually sold in Taiwan cannot be verified. The publisher has not released a sales figure, and the bookstore charts give ranks, not volumes. No number is estimated here. That gap is listed at the end, under "Questions Without Answers Yet."

Chapter FourHappiness Has a Price: Taxes, Hours, and Holidays

The title promises "the world's happiest country," and the claim has official backing — but what the backing actually says must be stated first. In the World Happiness Report 2025, published on March 20, 2025, Finland ranked No. 1 for the eighth year in a row, scoring 7.736 out of 10; Denmark was No. 2, Iceland No. 3, Sweden No. 4, and Norway No. 7.[6] The ranking rests on the 2022–2024 three-year average of how people in the Gallup World Poll rate their own lives overall.[6] In other words, the report does not measure smiles; it measures the score people give their own lives.

But the other side of the score is the bill. In 2024, Denmark collected tax revenue equal to 45.2% of GDP, the highest among the OECD's 38 member countries, against an OECD average of 34.1%.[7] Leaving work at 4 p.m., five weeks of paid holiday, free healthcare and education — behind all of it sits a contract that reads "pay almost half first, enjoy the services after." The author writes of "just-right comfortable time that can also be copied in Taiwan,"[4] but what can be copied is only the candlelight and the walk. The system that pays the bill cannot be copied.

The hours are worth laying out, too. In OECD statistics, the average Danish worker worked 1,372 hours in 2024;[8] the comparable figure for Taiwan is about 2,010 hours (2022).[13] The gap is more than 600 hours a year. Leaving at 4 p.m. is not proof that Danes understand life better; it is the rhythm that grows naturally in a society where working hours were already shorter and holidays already longer. The book presents the result as the cause.

Holidays work the same way. Under Denmark's Holiday Act, employees get five weeks — 25 days — of paid holiday a year, accruing 2.08 days a month.[9] Taking a whole month off in July and August[12] is possible under that system — but it is the product of a paid-holiday system, not of personal willpower. Taiwanese workers do not start from the same line on leave or on the culture of taking it, and carrying the book's one-month holiday into a conversation with your boss is more likely to produce a misunderstanding than a holiday.

One small check is also worth making: the 8-8-8 figures are not a Nordic invention. Their roots lie in the labour movement slogan of the late 19th century — 8 hours of work, 8 hours of rest, 8 hours of free time — an international demand that began in places like Australia.[14] Dressing up a century-old labour slogan as "the Nordic secret of time" is this book's cleverest piece of marketing, and also the place where it most needs a footnote: part of Nordic time is not Nordic at all. Workers everywhere fought for it together.

Chapter FiveWhat It Gets Right, What It Misses

Three things are right. First, the author's voice is honest. She does not pretend to be a winner in life; she starts from the low ground of the "useless office lady" and writes about how another way of living caught her.[4] A story told by someone who escaped is far more believable than success taught by the successful. Second, the book offers actions, not slogans. Light a candle, go for a walk, do one thing at a time — each can be done tonight, with no need to quit your job, save, or move house first. Third, the case for single-tasking holds up. Multitasking breeds anxiety; focus gets work finished. That needs no Nordic endorsement.

What is missing is also considerable. First, the book is thin. Of its 224 pages, many are photographs, and the e-book text runs to only 26,772 characters;[2] the ideas are five kinds of time plus one set of numbers. The first reading feels good; the second yields visibly less. Second, the five kinds of time overlap: self-care and enjoying hygge, living by your own compass and creative exploration — the boundaries are unclear. It is less five kinds of time than one attitude under five names. Third, the Nordic world in the book is a collage: Danish hygge, Swedish fika, Iceland's Jólabókaflóð,[12] plus Finland's happiness ranking,[6] four countries stitched into one "Nordic." But the book never deals with the differences between the four countries. Readers can easily add up their best into a utopia that does not exist.

The biggest gap is the question the book barely touches: who can afford to slow down? A freelance creator can finish at 4 p.m. and go for a walk; a shift worker cannot. Someone with five weeks of paid holiday can take a month off;[9] someone paid by the piece cannot. Slowness is a way of life that needs economic conditions underneath it. The book's "everyday proposals" look doable by anyone because the proposals themselves are cheap — candles, walks, and sunsets cost nothing. But the life behind the proposals — no overtime, no extra gigs, no fear of being replaced — is not cheap at all. Presenting the cheap part as the whole is the book's gentlest, least honest move toward its readers.

One more caution: many details in the book are the author's memories and observations — a colleague saying "because we work seriously, of course we finish," for instance[12] — and those conversations come from the author alone and cannot be independently verified. Read them as an honest person telling her own story, not as fieldwork.

Questions Without Answers Yet

To be honest, a few things still have no reliable answer, and this piece will not pretend otherwise:

  • Actual sales in Taiwan: the publisher has not released a sales figure, and bookstore charts give ranks, not volumes; no reliable number could be found here, so none is estimated.
  • Whether "leaving at 4 p.m." and "a month off in July and August" are typical in Denmark: the book describes the author's own surroundings; five weeks of paid holiday is the law, but actual hours vary widely by industry and job, and this piece did not verify them sector by sector.
  • Details of the memoir: colleagues' conversations and the author's inner turning points come from the author's own recollection and cannot be independently verified.
  • Whether all the photographs were taken in Northern Europe: the book says they are by a photographer based in Northern Europe, but this piece could not verify the location of each photograph.

EpilogueBefore You Take Your Time Back

Close the book, and what remains is not five kinds of time, but one question: of the time you spent today, how much was really yours? What the author learned in 7 years[4] can be compressed into a single sentence: first know what this moment's time is for, then decide how to spend it.[11]

But the answer is not in candlelight alone. Danes leave work at 4 p.m. on the back of 1,372 working hours a year,[8] 25 days of paid holiday,[9] and a tax take of 45.2% of GDP;[7] Finland has been No. 1 in happiness for eight years running[6] on the back of a whole social system, not a single candle. Bringing the candle home is right. Leaving the bill in the Nordic countries and bringing home only the postcard is not.

So the best way to read this book is as an invitation card, not a guarantee. It invites you to light a candle tonight, do one thing at a time tomorrow, and go for a walk this weekend. But if you finish the book believing that happiness will come to you automatically once you slow down, then what you read was not the book, but its advertisement. Real Nordic time was never in the book. It lives in the taxes Danes pay, the holidays they take, and the boundary around 8 hours of work a day — and those things cannot move to Taiwan. What can be moved is only the question: this moment's time — what is it for?

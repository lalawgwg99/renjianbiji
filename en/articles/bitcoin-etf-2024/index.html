The Bitcoin ETF: Wall Street Finally Lets Crypto In

Fifteen years ago it promised to route around every middleman; in 2024 it came home to them, dressed as an ETF.

PrologueAn Invitation Fifteen Years Late

On January 10, 2024, the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission approved 11 spot Bitcoin ETFs, and trading began the next day.[1] A thing born fifteen years earlier with the declared aim of routing around every middleman had finally been ushered into the great hall by the middlemen themselves — dressed in a suit, listed on an exchange, and admitted into retirement accounts.

This is not an article about how to buy an ETF. It is a story about absorption: how an invention built to defy the system was absorbed by it, step by step; how Wall Street went from laughing at Bitcoin to selling it as its own product; and what the rest of us were actually cheering for when we cheered for a financial revolution.

To understand why that day mattered, you have to go back to the beginning, back to those nine pages.

Chapter OneMoney Without Middlemen

On October 31, 2008, a person calling himself Satoshi Nakamoto sent a nine-page white paper to a cryptography mailing list. Its title was Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System.[2] The first sentence of the abstract stated the whole program: online payments sent directly from one party to another, without going through a financial institution.[2]

That year, the global financial system was living through a once-in-a-century crisis. Banks were failing, governments were printing money to rescue them, and ordinary people's savings and mortgages had become entries on someone else's balance sheet. Nakamoto's proposal was less a piece of technology than an accusation: the problem with money was not mathematics, it was trust — and the institutions we were forced to trust kept betraying that trust.

Bitcoin was designed to take trust out of the hands of institutions and hand it to code. Total supply capped at 21 million coins, written into the software where no one could change it; every 210,000 blocks, the reward paid to miners cut in half, so issuance would only ever slow down.[6] No central bank, no board of directors, no one who could meet at midnight and decide to print a little more.

Bitcoin's early believers thought this was the future of money: decentralized, permissionless, equal for all. Wall Street found it funny. A thing with no issuer, no cash flow and no office — what right did it have to call itself an asset? For a long time, mainstream finance had only one label for Bitcoin: a digital version of a Ponzi scheme.

Chapter TwoTen Years of No, One Court Ruling

More than 10 years had passed since the first application for a spot Bitcoin ETF was filed.[11] From 2018 through March 2023, the SEC rejected more than 20 applications, always for the same reasons: the market was too easy to manipulate, investor protection was insufficient.[11]

During those years, Bitcoin lived through a full cycle of boom and bust. In October 2021, the first Bitcoin futures ETF began trading — but it held no Bitcoin at all, only futures contracts;[9] a year later, the FTX exchange filed for bankruptcy, its founder was later convicted of fraud, and the whole industry fell into a deep winter.[10] The skeptics felt vindicated: see, it was a scam all along.

The turning point came from a courtroom. On August 29, 2023, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit ruled that the SEC's denial of Grayscale's application to convert its Bitcoin trust into a spot ETF was "arbitrary and capricious" — having already approved futures ETFs that tracked the very same price, the Commission could offer no coherent reason to refuse a spot product.[3] The court did not order the SEC to approve anything; it simply vacated the denial and sent it back for reconsideration.[3] That was enough. The legitimacy of refusal had been dismantled.

The day before approval, a farce played out. On January 9, 2024, the SEC's official X account was hacked and posted a fake approval announcement; Bitcoin's price jumped, and the SEC rushed to clarify that the message was false.[11] One day later, the real approval arrived. History sometimes works that way: first a rehearsal as farce, then the performance itself.

Chapter ThreeThe Day: January 10

On January 10, 2024, the SEC approved the listing of 11 spot Bitcoin ETFs by a 3–2 vote.[14] Chair Gary Gensler drew a careful line in his statement: "While we approved the listing and trading of certain spot bitcoin ETP shares today, we did not approve or endorse bitcoin."[1]

"While we approved the listing and trading of certain spot bitcoin ETP shares today, we did not approve or endorse bitcoin." — Gary Gensler, Chair of the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, January 10, 2024[1]

The next day, January 11, all 11 funds opened for trading at once. First-day volume reached $4.6 billion, with Grayscale's GBTC accounting for about $2.3 billion and BlackRock's IBIT for more than $1 billion.[5] Bitcoin itself touched $49,000 during the session and closed at about $46,070.[5]

What is interesting is that the price did not immediately soar. Those who expected approval to mean instant liftoff were disappointed; Bitcoin drifted lower over the following weeks. That was the market's most honest lesson: an ETF is not a guarantee of overnight riches. It is a new pipe. Water takes time to flow through it — and a pipe runs both ways. Money can flow in, and it can flow out.

But once a pipe is connected, it is very hard to disconnect. From that day on, anyone with a brokerage account could buy exposure to the price of Bitcoin at 9:30 in the morning, as easily as buying a share of stock. The significance of that was not the price. It was the threshold: for the first time, Bitcoin held a stamp of legitimacy from Wall Street itself.

Chapter FourWall Street Changes Its Face

No one changed face more completely than Larry Fink, chief executive of BlackRock. In 2017, he said Bitcoin was little more than "an index of money laundering." On July 5, 2023, in an interview on Fox Business, he called it "digitizing gold" and an "international asset."[8]

What happened in between? Bitcoin did not change. The clients did. In June 2023, BlackRock filed to launch a spot Bitcoin ETF, and within weeks Fidelity and other traditional asset-management giants followed.[8] When the world's largest asset manager decides to enter a market, the voices of opposition suddenly sound very lonely. Wall Street's moral judgments have always followed its clients' money.

The numbers tell the rest. Within seven weeks of launch, IBIT's assets passed $10 billion.[12] For the whole of 2024, U.S. spot Bitcoin ETFs took in more than $35 billion in net inflows; IBIT alone drew in more than $37 billion, Fidelity's FBTC nearly $12 billion, while Grayscale's GBTC lost more than $20 billion.[4] On November 21, 2024, total assets across U.S. Bitcoin ETFs passed $100 billion for the first time.[4]

But behind the numbers lies a structural reversal. People buying the ETF were not buying Bitcoin's ideals. They were buying Bitcoin without having to guard a private key. Investors hold fund shares; the actual Bitcoin sits in a custodian's cold wallet, looked after by someone else. That is precisely the thing Satoshi Nakamoto set out to destroy: the middleman.

Here, crowd psychology shows its truest face. People praise freedom with their mouths and choose convenience with their bodies. Guarding your own private key means freedom — and it means that if you lose it, it is gone forever. Handing it to BlackRock means giving up sovereignty in exchange for a certificate you can sell at any moment. Most people chose the second option, without a moment's hesitation.

Chapter FiveThe Halving and a Hundred Thousand Dollars

Three months after the ETFs launched, Bitcoin reached its fourth halving. On the evening of April 19, 2024, Eastern Time, block 840,000 was mined and the block reward fell from 6.25 BTC to 3.125 BTC; by Coordinated Universal Time, the moment fell at 00:09 on April 20.[6] From then on, the number of new Bitcoin created each day dropped from 900 to 450.

A halving is monetary policy written in code. It needs no meeting, no vote, no one's approval. It happens every four years, as punctual as the tide. What made the 2024 halving unusual was that it was the first to take place in a world where ETFs existed: on one side, supply was destined to halve; on the other, demand had a brand-new pipe.

It took the market most of the year to work out that arithmetic. On December 5, 2024, Bitcoin's price broke through $100,000 for the first time, reaching about $103,800 on the day;[7] over the year as a whole, it climbed from the low $40,000s at the start of January to about $93,400 by year-end, more than doubling.[13]

One hundred thousand dollars is a psychological threshold, not a fundamental one. It mattered because it buried, once and for all, the prophecy that Bitcoin would go to zero, and made the claim that it was "just a speculative bubble" harder and harder to sustain. Price, of course, is never proof of value — tulips were once very expensive too. But price is a mirror, and what it reflected was a complete reversal in the financial system's attitude toward Bitcoin.

Chapter SixVictory, or Surrender?

So back to the original question: was this victory, or surrender?

Consider the case for surrender first. Bitcoin was born so that people would no longer need to trust any institution; fifteen years later, its most successful product asks people to trust institutions all over again — to trust that BlackRock will keep the coins safe, that the SEC will keep watch, that the exchanges will not collapse. ETF investors hold no private keys, no wallets, no sovereignty. What they hold is a certificate that says "you own the price of Bitcoin," not Bitcoin itself. Seen this way, the ETF is not Bitcoin conquering Wall Street. It is Wall Street conquering Bitcoin.

Now consider the case for victory. Without the ETF, Bitcoin would never have entered retirement accounts, never made it onto the asset-allocation sheets of pension funds and university endowments, never reached the portfolios of tens of millions of ordinary people. Fifteen years of defying the system, and the way to win in the end was not to overthrow it but to be absorbed by it — priced, listed, shelved — transformed from a marginal cypherpunk experiment into a line item in mainstream portfolios. That is not surrender. That is infiltration.

The truth probably lies between the two. The ETF did not kill Bitcoin's ideals, but it did change Bitcoin's nature: it no longer belongs only to those willing to guard their own private keys; it belongs also to those who only want to see it on a price screen. Bitcoin has changed from a "monetary experiment" into an "asset class." There is no right or wrong in that transformation, but there is no going back.

If Satoshi Nakamoto were still around, would he buy the ETF? Or would he look at his invention displayed in Wall Street's shop window and think that this was how it was always going to end?

EpilogueThe Middleman Is Dead; Long Live the Middleman

What 2024 taught us is this: the most likely ending of a revolution is not the overthrow of the old world but absorption by it. Bitcoin did not destroy the middleman, and the middleman did not destroy Bitcoin. They absorbed each other and reached a new symbiosis: Bitcoin gained legitimacy and liquidity, and Wall Street gained a new product and new fees.

That may be the fate of every disruptive technology. The internet once promised to overturn everything, and ended up as the backyard of five large companies. Bitcoin is no different; it simply traveled further than most.

So when you buy a share of IBIT, what are you buying? A ransom paid for freedom, or a ticket into the system? The answer is not on the price screen. It is in your own mind — it depends on whether, when you buy it, you are thinking "I want to escape this system" or "I want to catch this train." The same certificate, two faiths. And that may be the most worthwhile lesson 2024 left us: in financial history there are no pure victories, only compromises rewritten again and again.

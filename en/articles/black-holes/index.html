Black Holes: The Unseen Heart of the Universe

From a letter written in 1783 to a photograph taken in 2019 — it took humanity 236 years to photograph the invisible

SlowFolio Editorial Team · Reading time 13 min

繁中

Narrated in Mandarin · Female voice HsiaoChen

The Same Era · 2019

  • 201915 April 2019 — Fire at Notre-Dame de Paris. Five days after humanity unveiled the first image of a black hole, a major fire engulfed Notre-Dame Cathedral during Holy Week, collapsing its roof and spire. The Gothic cathedral, begun in the 12th century, is one of the great symbols of human civilisation. In a single April, humanity saw into an abyss 55 million light-years away — and watched eight centuries of stone and timber burn at home. The farthest seeing and the nearest loss happened in the same week.
  • 201918 October 2019 — First all-female spacewalk. NASA astronauts Christina Koch and Jessica Meir stepped outside the International Space Station for a 7-hour-17-minute spacewalk to replace a faulty power controller — the first spacewalk in history carried out by two women. In the same year humanity photographed a black hole and sent two women walking together into space: seeing the darkest place in the universe, and letting more people walk into it, were two sides of one year.

PrologueA Photograph of Nothing

On 10 April 2019, press conferences around the world lit up with the same image: a lopsided ring of orange light, with a patch of pure black at its centre. It was not a work of art, nor a computer simulation. It was humanity's first sight of a black hole — M87*, at the heart of the galaxy Messier 87, some 6.5 billion times the mass of the Sun and about 55 million light-years from Earth. The true subject of the photograph was precisely that patch of nothing: the ring is gas heated until it glows; the black hole itself is the void the ring encircles.

A picture of something invisible made the front page of every newspaper on Earth. Why?

Because the story of the black hole was never only about astronomy. It is a story about how human beings treat the impossible: how something that mathematically should not exist was first calculated on paper, then rejected, mocked and forgotten by generation after generation, until, more than a century later, it became a photograph everyone recognises. Across that span lie wars, prejudice, wagers and a whole century of stubbornness. And the black hole fascinates us for exactly this reason: it forces us to admit that the deepest truths of the universe hide where we cannot see.

Chapter OneThe Dark Star

On 27 November 1783[1], a country rector in Yorkshire named John Michell wrote a letter to his friend Henry Cavendish. Michell was no grand figure; he spent his life between Cambridge and a rural parish, studying earthquakes, magnetism and the distribution of stars. His letter was not meant to invent a monster. He was thinking about a practical problem: could light be used to weigh a star?

People then believed light was made of particles. Following Newton's reasoning, Michell saw that if light were particulate, a star's gravity would slow it down; and if a star were massive enough, the speed needed to escape its surface would exceed the speed of light — so its light could never get out, and the star would be wholly invisible in the sky. He calculated that a body with the Sun's density and 500 times the Sun's diameter[1] would pull all its own light back. Such a body, he wrote, would have "all light emitted from such a body made to return towards it, by its own proper gravity." He even thought of a way to detect one: if such a dark star had a visible companion, the companion's orbit around something unseen would give it away.

Thirteen years later, the French mathematician Pierre-Simon Laplace independently reached the same idea in his Exposition du système du Monde of 1796, and even supplied a formula. Then the idea vanished. As the wave theory of light rose, no one believed any longer that gravity could hold light back; Laplace deleted the passage from later editions. The dark star became an unheard-of notion, lying in old papers for more than a century.

This is the first lesson of the black hole: a correct intuition born in the wrong era is not refuted — it is forgotten. Science is not only a matter of right and wrong; it is also a matter of timing. What Michell and Laplace lacked was not intelligence but a theoretical framework in which their idea could survive. That framework would not arrive until the early 20th century.

Chapter TwoEquations from the Trenches

On 25 November 1915[3], Einstein presented the field equations of general relativity to the Prussian Academy of Sciences: matter and energy determine how spacetime curves, and curved spacetime determines how matter moves. Gravity was no longer a pull but geometry itself. Once published, mathematicians across Europe tried to solve the equations — a set of fiendishly complex nonlinear partial differential equations for which even Einstein had only approximate solutions.

Within two months, the answer came from the least likely place. Karl Schwarzschild, a German astronomer, was serving in the trenches on the Russian front in 1916. Between bombardments he read Einstein's paper and, with pencil and paper, worked out an exact solution: the spacetime geometry around a spherically symmetric mass. Hidden in that solution was a strange boundary — inside a certain radius, not even light could escape. Later generations would call that radius the Schwarzschild radius and the boundary the event horizon. Schwarzschild sent the paper to Einstein, who replied in admiration. A few months later, Schwarzschild died of an illness contracted at the front, aged 42.

The irony is that Einstein himself did not believe what his own equations were saying. He regarded the boundary as a mathematical blemish, not a real thing. In 1939 he even published a paper arguing that a star could never contract to such a state. In that same year, Robert Oppenheimer and his student Hartland Snyder used the very same theory to reach the opposite conclusion.

Here is the second lesson: even the creator of a theory can become its greatest opponent. Einstein did not fail at the mathematics; he could not accept where the mathematics pointed — a place where the concepts of time and space themselves break down. For a man who believed the universe should be rational and orderly, that was absurd. But the history of science shows, again and again, that equations are often more honest than their authors.

Chapter ThreeThe Death of a Star

Oppenheimer and Snyder's 1939 paper[2] bore the unremarkable title "On Continued Gravitational Contraction." They asked a simple question: what happens to a massive star after it burns through its nuclear fuel? A star spends its life fighting its own weight, held up by the pressure of fusion. When the fuel runs out, the pressure vanishes and only gravity remains. For a heavy enough star, no known force can halt the collapse. The star would shrink on and on until not even light could escape, sealing itself off from the rest of the universe and leaving behind only its gravitational field. The paper ends: "The star thus tends to close itself off from any communication with a distant observer; only its gravitational field persists."

Almost no one paid attention at the time. The reasons are not hard to see: in 1939 the world was sliding into war, Oppenheimer himself was soon drawn into the atomic bomb project and never returned to the subject. More deeply, physics held a stubborn belief that stars ought to be stable. From Eddington onward, the mainstream held that celestial bodies would ultimately find equilibrium — that collapsing to a point "could not really happen." However correct Oppenheimer's calculations were, they could not overcome an era's intuition.

The turn came in the 1960s. In January 1965[4], the British mathematician Roger Penrose published a paper that used entirely new topological methods to prove that black hole formation was no coincidence under special conditions but a robust prediction of general relativity. Once a collapsing star formed what he called a "trapped surface," a singularity was unavoidable — no matter how irregular the star had been. In 2020, Penrose received the Nobel Prize in Physics[4] "for the discovery that black hole formation is a robust prediction of the general theory of relativity."

In that same decade, the black hole finally got its name. In 1967, the Princeton physicist John Archibald Wheeler[9] complained in a lecture that "gravitationally completely collapsed object" was a mouthful, and someone in the audience shouted: "How about black hole?" Wheeler knew at once he had found it. The phrase had in fact appeared in print as early as 1964, but only through Wheeler's repeated use did it truly catch on. A good name is a scientific revolution's passport — from then on, ordinary people could talk about black holes.

Meanwhile, on the observational side, evidence began to accumulate. In 1964, a sounding rocket detected a powerful X-ray source in the constellation Cygnus: Cygnus X-1. In 1971, radio observations pinned it to the blue supergiant HDE 226868; in 1972, two teams of astronomers independently found that the supergiant was orbiting an unseen companion with a period of about 5.6 days, and that the companion was too heavy to be a neutron star. By the end of 1973, the astronomical community generally conceded that Cygnus X-1 was most likely a black hole — humanity's first widely accepted black hole candidate.

There is a charming footnote. In 1975, Stephen Hawking bet Kip Thorne that Cygnus X-1 was not a black hole; Thorne bet that it was. Hawking later said he actually hoped to lose — for if black holes did not exist, much of his life's work would have been wasted. In 1990, as the evidence grew overwhelming, Hawking conceded, paying up with a year's subscription to Penthouse magazine. A scientist wagering on his own theory and conceding gracefully: that is science at its most likeable.

Chapter FourBlack Holes Glow

On 1 March 1974[5], Hawking published a short paper in Nature whose title ended with a question mark: "Black hole explosions?" Its conclusion shook physics: black holes are not entirely black. They emit radiation, slowly lose mass, and eventually evaporate away.

The idea came from the meeting of the two great theories. Quantum mechanics says the vacuum is not empty; pairs of virtual particles constantly pop into existence and annihilate. At the edge of the event horizon, a pair can be torn apart: one falls in, the other escapes, carrying a little energy away. Seen from afar, the black hole appears to glow faintly. The trail leads back to Jacob Bekenstein's insight two years earlier, in 1972, that a black hole should possess entropy proportional to its surface area. Hawking had at first opposed the idea, then carried it to its conclusion himself, showing that black holes have a temperature and radiate.

But Hawking radiation immediately raised a deeper trouble. The radiation is random; it carries no information. So where does the information in things that fall in — a book, a star, the records of a whole civilisation — go? Quantum mechanics says information can never be destroyed; general relativity says a black hole erases it. This contradiction became known as the black hole information paradox, and it still has no accepted answer. Hawking himself at one point claimed to have solved it, then withdrew the claim. Fifty years on, the question remains on the front lines of theoretical physics, bound up with the fates of quantum gravity, string theory and the holographic principle.

This is the most thought-provoking chapter of the story: the black hole changed from a place nothing could escape into a place from which everything slowly leaks; from a marginal topic in astronomy into a mirror held up to the cracks in the foundations of physics. A theory is at its most valuable not when it explains something, but when it forces out a question no one can answer.

Chapter FiveHearing and Seeing

The black hole's next century waited a long time for its age of evidence. The evidence arrived twice: once to be heard, once to be seen.

On 14 September 2015[6], the two LIGO detectors in Louisiana and Washington State simultaneously caught a signal lasting about 0.2 seconds. Analysis showed it was a gravitational wave from the merger, 1.3 billion years earlier, of two black holes — about 36 and 29 times the mass of the Sun — forming a single black hole of about 62 solar masses, with about 3 solar masses' worth of energy radiated away as gravitational waves. The detection was announced on 11 February 2016[6]. It was humanity's first direct detection of gravitational waves, and the first time we had "heard" a black hole. Einstein had predicted gravitational waves in 1916; a century later, we finally heard them.

Then came 10 April 2019[7]. The Event Horizon Telescope (EHT) linked eight radio telescopes around the planet into a single virtual telescope the size of the Earth, aimed it at the black hole at the centre of M87, gathered more than a petabyte of data, and after two years of computation produced the image of the orange ring. The black hole's shadow is roughly the size of the Solar System, yet sits 55 million light-years away; photographing it is like discerning a doughnut on the surface of the Moon from Earth. One of the project's leaders called M87* the "heavyweight champion" of black holes in the universe.

In 2020, the Nobel Prize in Physics went to black holes once again: half to Penrose, and half jointly to Reinhard Genzel and Andrea Ghez, who had tracked the orbits of stars at the centre of the Milky Way since the early 1990s and shown that an unseen object of about 4 million solar masses lurks there. On 12 May 2022, the EHT released its second black hole image[8]: Sagittarius A*, at the heart of our own galaxy, about 4 million times the mass of the Sun and about 26,000 light-years from Earth. We had finally seen the heart of our own galaxy.

From a letter in 1783 to a photograph in 2019: 236 years. Humanity took more than two centuries to travel from imagining an invisible thing to photographing it. Every step along the way required someone willing to trust mathematics over intuition, and evidence over authority.

EpilogueWhy Look at What Cannot Be Seen

Black holes are good for nothing. They generate no electricity, cure no disease, and make no one's life more convenient. Spending billions of dollars and mobilising more than 200 scientists around the world just to take a blurry orange ring — is it worth it?

Anyone who asks that should first ask another question: why are human beings so obsessed with things they cannot see?

The answer may be hidden in the black hole's own history. Michell wanted to weigh a star; Schwarzschild solved equations in a trench; Oppenheimer wrote of a dying star on the eve of war; Hawking imagined a black hole evaporating from a wheelchair. None of them was chasing anything useful. Each was gripped by a single thought: somewhere in the universe there is a place where our theories fail — and where they fail is precisely where new knowledge begins. A black hole is a boundary marker of physics; only by standing there can we see the edge of the known world.

There is a deeper reason still. The black hole defies humanity's most stubborn intuition: seeing is believing. We are used to trusting only what we can see. But the black hole was first calculated, then indirectly confirmed, and only finally "seen" — and what was seen was not the black hole itself, but the light around it and the shadow it casts. Science here teaches a humble lesson: the real is not the same as the visible, and what is visible is often only the edge of the real.

The photograph of 2019 is blurry and crude: one orange ring, one patch of black. It moves us precisely because it is imperfect. It is not a perfect photograph; it is a photograph that says "we finally got it." Two hundred and thirty-six years of waiting, countless rejections, generations of relayed effort — all condensed into that small ring of light.

There are more invisible things in the universe: dark matter, dark energy, the truth about quantum gravity. The story of the black hole tells us how to deal with them: first believe the mathematics on paper, then spend a generation verifying it, and finally, patiently wait for the day the photograph arrives.

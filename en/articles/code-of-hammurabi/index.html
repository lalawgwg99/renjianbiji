The Code of Hammurabi: How One Stone Framed a Kingdom

Around 1754 BCE, a king who had just conquered all of Mesopotamia had 282 laws carved into basalt. At the top of the stele he stands before Shamash, god of the sun and of justice, receiving the rod and the ring; below it, the strong are told not to harm the weak — and the same eye is given three different prices. This essay asks: whom does the law protect?

PrologueA Stone That Has Stood for Nearly 4,000 Years

In a gallery of the Louvre in Paris stands a block of black stone. It is 2.25 metres tall, half a body taller than an adult, its top rounded like a great finger pointing at the ceiling. Thousands of visitors pass it every day; some stop to look at the relief carved at the top and the dense columns of cuneiform below, and move on. They may not know that they have just walked past the most famous law code in human history — and past an argument that has lasted nearly 4,000 years: is the law a shield that protects the weak, or a sword that secures the power of kings?

The stone is the Code of Hammurabi. Around 1754 BCE, a king who had just conquered all of Mesopotamia ordered 282 laws carved into basalt.[4] It is not the first law code humanity produced, nor the longest. But it is the best preserved, the longest remembered, and the most often invoked. Its fate is itself a story: carried off as war booty, then dug up in the winter of 1901 and shipped to a museum in Paris.

This essay follows the stone: from the palace of Babylon to the relief at the top of the stele, into the laws themselves, and finally to the present day — to a question each of us must ask: if the law is written down where everyone can read it, does that make it fair?

Chapter OneThe New King of Babylon

Hammurabi was not a conqueror born to the role. He was the sixth king of the First Dynasty of Babylon, taking the throne from his father Sin-muballit around 1792 BCE.[5] Babylon was then only one city-state among many in the land between the rivers. Larsa dominated the south, Eshnunna lay to the north-east, Mari held the middle Euphrates, and the kings of Assyria watched from the north. For the first thirty years of his reign, Hammurabi did what a small king could do: he made alliances, fortified cities, dug canals, and waited.

The opportunity came late. Around 1763 BCE he took Larsa, and the whole south passed to Babylon; around 1761 BCE he took Mari, his former ally.[5] In less than a decade, almost all of Mesopotamia was under his rule.[1] Babylon grew from a city into the capital of an empire, and Hammurabi from a city king into a king of the four quarters of the world. He died around 1750 BCE, after 42 years on the throne; his son Samsu-iluna inherited the empire and could not hold it together.[1]

Yet Hammurabi's name did not fall with his empire. He is remembered not for his conquests, but for his code. Why would a king who rose by war spend his final years on a block of stone? The answer is not on the battlefield. It is in the daily life of an empire.

Chapter TwoWhy Carve the Law into Stone?

Consider the problem Hammurabi faced. His lands held Babylonians, Sumerians, Amorites and Elamites, each city with its own customs and its own gods. A merchant travelling from one city to the next found the same deal judged differently in two places; a farmer quarrelling with a neighbour over a canal had no one to turn to. An empire without a set of rules that everyone recognises cannot be held together by armies alone.

The prologue of the code says this plainly. Hammurabi claims the gods called him "to bring about the rule of righteousness in the land, to destroy the wicked and the evil-doers; so that the strong should not harm the weak".[3] Two faces hide inside that sentence: protection — the strong shall not harm the weak — and power, for who decides what righteousness means? The king does, and a king appointed by the gods. The relief at the top of the stele draws the answer: Hammurabi stands, while Shamash, god of the sun and of justice, sits enthroned, holding the rod and the ring.[4] Whether the king is receiving the law or reporting to the god, the source of law is not among men.

So the law had to be cut into stone — into basalt, a stone almost impossible to break. Clay tablets shatter, paper rots, spoken commands are forgotten, but stone endures. The stele stands 2.25 metres high and carries some 4,130 lines of cuneiform, written in Akkadian, the common language of the day, rather than Sumerian, the language of scholars — written to be read by people, not by gods.[4] The epilogue goes further: the oppressed man with a case at law may come and stand before the image of the king of righteousness, read the inscription, and understand what is just.[3] For the first time, the law was placed in public, where an ordinary person could walk up and read it.

But how many ordinary people could actually read cuneiform? The stele stood in a temple, and literacy was rare. Making the law public is one thing; making it readable is another. The publicity of the code was above all a declaration: the king's rules stand here, and no one may change them — not even the king himself. The epilogue curses any future ruler who dares to alter the laws. A king used the name of the gods to bind himself to his own law. Is that self-restraint, or a subtler art of rule? The code does not answer. It carved the question into stone and left it to us.

Chapter ThreeAn Eye for an Eye — Really?

Mention the Code of Hammurabi and almost everyone thinks of one phrase first: an eye for an eye, a tooth for a tooth. The phrase is indeed in the code. Law 196 reads: "If a man put out the eye of another free man, his eye shall be put out."[3] But that is only half the story. Read on, and the picture changes at once.

The code divides people into three ranks: the free man of full standing, the commoner, and the slave. The eye-for-an-eye rule applies only between free men. If a free man blinds the eye of a commoner, Law 198 says he pays one mina of silver. If he blinds the eye of a slave, Law 199 says he pays the slave's owner half the slave's value.[3] One eye, three prices — because in the eyes of the code, what a person's body is worth depends on where that person stands.

The ranking runs through the whole code. A surgeon who operates on a free man with a bronze lancet and the patient dies shall have his hand cut off; if the patient is a slave, the surgeon need only replace the slave for the owner (Law 218).[3] If a builder's house collapses and kills the owner, the builder is put to death; if it kills the owner's son, the builder's son is put to death; if it kills a slave, the builder replaces the slave (Laws 229–230).[3] The logic is chillingly calm: its reason is rank itself.

But to see only the cruelty is to miss the other side of the code. With the same calm pen, it draws a few lines for the weak. A man who accuses another of murder and cannot prove it shall himself be put to death (Law 1);[3] a debtor may hand his wife, son or daughter to a creditor, but for no more than 3 years — in the fourth year they must be set free (Law 117);[3] a slave who says to his master "You are not my master" shall have his ear cut off (Law 282) — cruel, yet it proves something too: the law heard the slave speak.[3] Most of the code, in fact, is not about eyes and teeth at all, but about fields, canals, leases, wages, marriage, inheritance and adoption — the small business of an empire's everyday life. Its ambition was not to frighten, but to gather every dispute into a single language.

Perhaps the real meaning of an eye for an eye is not revenge, but a ceiling. Without the code, a blood feud could escalate without limit: you take my eye, I kill your whole family. With the code, revenge stops here — no more and no less than one eye. For commoners and slaves, revenge was converted into silver, and even the right to revenge was taken away. Fair? By today's standards, plainly not. But in the 18th century BCE, the idea that even the king's subjects must pay according to a rule was itself a step forward — the question is who got to eat its fruit, and who did not.

Chapter FourHe Was Not the First to Write the Law

The Code of Hammurabi is often called the first law code in human history. That is wrong. Before it came the Code of Ur-Nammu. Ur-Nammu founded the Third Dynasty of Ur, and his code, written around 2100 BCE, is the oldest surviving law code known today — more than 3 centuries older than Hammurabi's.[6] It was written on clay tablets in Sumerian, contained 57 laws, of which about 30 have been reconstructed, and was translated by the scholar Samuel Noah Kramer in 1952.[7]

Set the two codes side by side and the difference leaps out. In Ur-Nammu's code there is almost no mutilation: knock out another man's eye and pay half a mina of silver; knock out his tooth and pay 2 shekels of silver.[6] In Hammurabi's hands, the same injury to a free man became an eye for an eye. In just over 3 centuries, the law moved from fines to bodily punishment. That was no accident. Ur-Nammu ruled a relatively homogeneous Sumerian kingdom; Hammurabi faced an empire freshly stitched together by war. A new empire needed harder rules and a more visible threat. The severity of a law often grows in step with the anxiety of those who rule.

One more point is often missed: Hammurabi's code was probably not invented from nothing by one man. The laws of the land between the rivers already existed before him, written on clay tablets and kept in temples and palaces. What Hammurabi did was closer to collecting, organising and unifying — then stamping the result with his own name and the name of the gods, turning it into a single code for the whole empire. His originality was not in inventing law, but in making law part of the infrastructure of empire. Later ages remember his name not because he was the first to write the law, but because he was the first to write it so large, so publicly, and so hard to erase.

Chapter FiveThe Stone's Second Life

After Hammurabi's death, the empire soon fell apart. The code did not: Mesopotamian scribes copied it for more than 1,000 years, and the laws became required reading for apprentice scribes.[4] The stone had fallen, but its words went on living on clay. That was the code's first second life: from monument to textbook.

The journey of the stele itself was more dramatic. Around the 12th century BCE, the Elamite king Shutruk-Nahhunte marched into Babylon and carried the stele off to Susa as war booty; he even had 7 columns of laws polished away to make room for an inscription of his own victories — an inscription that was never carved, so the blank space remained.[2] The stele lay at Susa for 600 years, until between December 1901 and January 1902, the French mission under Jacques de Morgan dug up 3 large fragments on the acropolis of Susa and pieced them together; Father Jean-Vincent Scheil published the first edition of the text in 1902.[2] Today it stands in the Louvre in Paris, inventory number Sb 8, among the most visited objects in the Department of Near Eastern Antiquities.[2]

Since its rediscovery, scholars have argued for more than a century over whether the code influenced later laws. Some trace similarities between it and the Mosaic law of the Old Testament; others warn that similarity is not descent. The argument is still unresolved — but one thing is certain: the Code of Hammurabi gave concrete shape, for the first time, to the idea that the law should be written down, made public, and known to all. Later codes may not have copied its provisions, but they inherited its posture: a law that would stand must be like stone — hard, public, and difficult to erase.

EpilogueWhose Law Is It?

Look at the stone once more. It settles no one's lawsuit now, and no one walks up to it to read the law and demand justice. But it is still there, reminding us of something simple: the law was never only a set of provisions. Behind the provisions stands a question — who has the power to decide right from wrong, and who pays the price of that decision?

Hammurabi's answer was that the king, appointed by the gods, writes the law, protects the weak, and secures his own power. In his hands the two purposes were not in conflict, because they were two faces of the same coin: protecting the weak made royal power look legitimate, and securing royal power made protection possible. The most honest thing about the code is that it never pretended all men were equal. It wrote three ranks into the law, so that anyone who reads it can see at a glance what justice looked like in that age.

Today's law no longer divides people into three ranks, and no longer prices eyes in a table. But none of the questions Hammurabi left behind has gone out of date: the law is written down — who can read it? The law is public — who can reach it? The law promises to protect the weak — are the weak truly protected? A stone nearly 4,000 years old is still asking us these questions. And we have not finished writing the answers.

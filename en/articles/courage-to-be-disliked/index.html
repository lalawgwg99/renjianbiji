The Courage to Be Disliked: Why a Book from 2013 Is Still on the Bestseller Lists in 2026

In five nights of dialogue, Ichiro Kishimi and Fumitake Koga turn Adlerian psychology into a single line: freedom is being disliked by others. This deep read asks whether their Adler is the real Adler — and whether a book can fairly call every trouble in life a personal choice.

PrologueA Book That Has Sold for Thirteen Years

On December 13, 2013, Diamond Inc. published a 296-page book in Japan with a provocative title: Kirawareru Yuki — The Courage to Be Disliked.[4] In December 2023, the book marked its tenth anniversary, with a special tenth-anniversary feature added to the ebook;[3] and in the first half of 2026 it was still sitting in the top 30 of the Eslite and Books.com.tw bestseller lists. How does a 2013 dialogue about psychology keep selling in 2026?

The Taiwan edition came out on October 30, 2014, from Cite Publishing, translated by Yeh Hsiao-yen, 304 pages.[1] In Japan it was Amazon's book of the year, spent 300 consecutive days on the bestseller lists, took third place in the 2014 Japan Business Book Awards, and was picked as Manager magazine's business book of the month for April 2014.[2] As for sales — the numbers disagree, and that reckoning can wait until Chapter Five. First, a more basic question: what is this book actually selling? The answer is not Adlerian psychology — at least, not only that.

Chapter OneFive Nights, One Conversation

The book's form is simple: a young man walks into a philosopher's study, and the two argue for five nights.[2] The young man stands in for the reader — angry, sceptical, unconvinced; the philosopher stands in for Adler — the world is simple, happiness is within everyone's reach. There are two authors: Ichiro Kishimi, born in Kyoto in 1956, a specialist in Platonic philosophy who has studied Adlerian psychology since 1989 and is a certified counsellor of the Japanese Society of Adlerian Psychology;[3] and Fumitake Koga, born in 1973, a freelance writer who works by transcribing interviews — this book grew out of his repeated visits to Kyoto, turning Kishimi's words into dialogue.[3]

The dialogue form is the book's cleverest choice. Readers never have to study theory; they only have to listen to an argument, and every objection the young man raises is the reader's own “it can't be that simple”. But the form has a built-in flaw: the philosopher always wins. The young man arrives furious on the first night and leaves converted on the fifth. Is what we are reading a debate, or a sermon? Hold that question until Chapter Five.

Chapter TwoTrauma Does Not Exist? Teleology Against Aetiology

To read this book you first need to meet Alfred Adler (1870–1937).[7] An Austrian physician, he joined Freud's Wednesday discussion group in 1902 and became president of the Vienna Psychoanalytic Society in 1910; on February 23, 1911, he walked out with nine companions and founded his own individual psychology.[8] The split between Freud and Adler comes down to one question: are we determined by the past, or do we live for the future?

Freud asks “why”: you are afraid to leave the house today because a childhood trauma made you that way; the trauma decides you. Adler asks “what for”: you are afraid to leave the house because staying in serves a purpose — it spares you failure and being disliked. The past is not the cause; it is material you reach for to justify a purpose. That is teleology against aetiology. Adler's psychology is teleological, not causal.[7]

The book pushes this claim to its extreme, into a single sentence: “Trauma does not exist.” It is the most powerful line in the book, and the most easily misread. Adler does not mean that suffering is unreal; he means suffering cannot decide you. But the book states it too absolutely, and to someone who has genuinely been hurt, “trauma does not exist” sounds less like liberation than like an insult. That is a debt Chapter Five will collect.

Chapter ThreeSeparation of Tasks — Whose Task Is It?

If the book could keep only one move, it would be separation of tasks. The method is simple: ask first, whose task is this? Studying is your task; your parents' anxiety is theirs. How you live is your task; whether others like you is theirs. Once the line is drawn, half your troubles disappear — because most troubles in human relationships come from carrying someone else's task on your own back.

The book adds three keywords: self-acceptance, confidence in others, and contribution to others. Separation of tasks is not a licence for coldness; it is an instruction to stop interfering — not to decide other people's lives for them, and not to hand your own life over to other people's judgement. Why is this move so popular? Because it turns the vague slogan “be yourself” into an action you can perform: the next time you are about to nod along for fear of being disliked, ask first — whose task is this?

But there is a question of attribution here that readers deserve to know. A fact-check published in November 2026 traced the book's 12 core ideas against Adler's own writings and found nine of them in Adler's books from 1927 to 1931 — Understanding Human Nature, The Science of Living, What Life Should Mean to You — but separation of tasks was not among them; the Japanese Adlerian scholar Shunsaku Noda traces it instead to Rudolf Dreikurs, a student of Adler's.[9] The idea itself is not wrong, but the book's label — “the teachings of Adler” — is looser than it looks.

Chapter FourCommunity Feeling, or Living Like Dancing

The destination of Adlerian psychology is not personal victory but community feeling: seeing yourself as part of a community, contributing without demanding a return. The book says happiness is not something you get but someone you become; all the troubles of life come from human relationships, and the way out of them is not to beat others but to walk with them.

The same part of the book talks about living in the moment: life is not a line running from the past into the future but a series of moments; live like you are dancing — the dance is the purpose, and there is no need to wait until it ends for it to count. This is the most philosophical chapter in the book, and no wonder: Kishimi trained in Platonic philosophy, and he writes Adler as a philosophy of life, not merely a form of therapy.

After the book took off, the story went on: in February 2016, the sequel, The Courage to Be Happy, was published in Taiwan, devoted to the practice of happiness;[3] the book has been adapted for television and the stage, and the English edition came out first from Allen & Unwin in Australia and New Zealand in 2017,[6] then from Atria Books in the United States on May 8, 2018, at 288 pages.[5] A book introducing a psychologist from a century ago became an international business.

Chapter FiveWhere It Is Right, and Where It Falls Short

First, where it is right. One, teleology hands the initiative back to you: you are not a prisoner of your past but the author of your purposes. Two, separation of tasks is a practical tool for relationships; knowing whose business something is cuts arguments in half. Three, community feeling pulls a self-help book back towards ethics: the end of happiness is not winning but contributing. On those three points alone, the book earns every copy it has sold.

Now where it falls short. One, oversimplification. The book treats the troubles of life as personal choices, but some troubles are not choices at all: poverty, workplace bullying, structural discrimination. Telling someone on the weaker side of power that “this is your task” sounds less like liberation than like blame. Adler himself cared about social reform; the book turns his psychology into a course in personal cultivation.

Two, the fairness of the dialogue. The young man is converted every night, so what the reader sees is not a debate but a finished script. A real dialogue should give the other side a chance to win; this book gives it none. Three, fidelity. Key ideas such as separation of tasks may not come from Adler himself, yet the book sells itself under his name — not academic fraud, but a gap between the label and the contents that is never marked.

Finally, the sales figures, stated honestly: the publishers do not agree. Simon & Schuster's official page says more than 10 million copies worldwide;[5] a December 2025 Diamond Online conversation between the two authors gives 3.36 million in Japan and 15 million worldwide;[9] a 2022 Nikkei interview gives 2.76 million for the book alone in Japan and 9 million for the series worldwide;[10] and an older Books.com.tw listing gives 1 million for the series in Taiwan and 5.5 million worldwide.[3] Single book or series, counted up to which year — the scopes differ, so this article will not rule between them. One thing is certain on any count: this is a book that has sold for 13 years and is still selling.

Questions Without Answers Yet

To be honest, the following three things still have no reliable answers, and this article will not pretend otherwise:

  • How many copies has it really sold? The publishers disagree (see Chapter Five); their scopes — single book or series, counted up to which year — differ, so this article will not rule between them.
  • Is the young man in the book a real person? The dialogue is a literary reconstruction assembled by Koga from repeated interviews, not a verbatim transcript; the philosopher's nightly victories cannot be read as a record of a real debate.
  • To whom do ideas like separation of tasks belong? The fact-check cited above finds some core ideas absent from Adler's own writings, and scholars still differ on their attribution; this article follows the fact-check but will not rule on it.

EpilogueFreedom Is Being Disliked by Others

On the back cover runs a single line, the soul of the whole book: freedom is being disliked by others.[2] Why has that line sold for 13 years? Because it turns “be yourself” from a slogan into a transaction: if you want freedom, you pay for it by being disliked. There is no free freedom.

A book from 2013 still selling in 2026 is not Adler coming back to life; it is that this transaction always finds a buyer. You do not have to agree with every sentence in the book — but the next time you are about to nod along for fear of being disliked, you will at least stop for one second and ask yourself: is this my task, or someone else's? Stopping for one second is sometimes the whole difference.

CRISPR: How a Pair of Scissors Let Humanity Rewrite Itself for the First Time

From a bacterium's immune memory to a $2.2 million dose — the power to rewrite the code of life has fallen into ordinary hands for the first time

PrologueJune 28, 2012: A Paper Nobody Noticed

On June 28, 2012, the journal Science published a paper with a title so long nobody wanted to read it twice: "A Programmable Dual-RNA-Guided DNA Endonuclease in Adaptive Bacterial Immunity." No reporter called. There were no headlines. Jennifer Doudna, one of the authors, later recalled that her only quote that day appeared in a U.S. Department of Energy press release. "Although we've not yet demonstrated genome editing," she said, "given the mechanism we describe, it is now a very real possibility."[13]

Eight years later, she and Emmanuelle Charpentier shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Three years after that, the first medicine made with these scissors reached the market — priced at $2.2 million a dose. From an unnoticed paper to a $2.2 million drug took just 11 years. This is not a technical history of a pair of scissors. It is about an older question: now that the power to rewrite life has become cheap enough for anyone to use, is humanity ready to answer whether it should?

Chapter OneThe Bacterium's Memory

The story begins in 1987. That year, a team led by Yoshizumi Ishino at Osaka University, sequencing a gene in E. coli, stumbled on a strange sequence at the end of it: repeating units of 29 DNA letters, separated by 32-letter spacers that had nothing in common with each other. It was like finding a page of code in the middle of a book, with no idea what it said. When the paper was published, the sequence earned only a brief mention on the last page.[5]

The answer took 18 years to surface. In 2005, three research groups independently discovered that the spacers were not the bacterium's own DNA at all, but fragments of bacteriophages — viruses that attack bacteria. Francisco Mojica, at the University of Alicante in Spain, was the first to see what it meant: the bacterium had kept a record of its invaders. This was not junk DNA. It was a wanted list.[5]

In 2007, Rodolphe Barrangou's team at Danisco ran the decisive experiment, using the bacterium that makes yogurt. Streptococcus thermophilus is the workhorse of yogurt production, and bacteriophages are the yogurt factory's worst enemy. The experiment proved it: after a bacterium is infected by a virus, it stores a small piece of the virus's DNA in its own genome. The next time the same virus comes back, the bacterium recognizes it, and a Cas protein cuts it to pieces. Bacteria have no antibodies, no white blood cells, no immune memory cells — only this list, which they pass on to their descendants.[5]

In 2002, Ruud Jansen and colleagues gave these sequences their formal name: CRISPR, clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats. The name is a mouthful, but the meaning is plain: repeating fragments, with trophies torn from the enemy tucked in between. In 2011, Charpentier found another crucial piece of the puzzle: a small molecule called tracrRNA, without which the whole system stalls.[2]

Evolution spent billions of years writing this system. Bacteria use it to remember their enemies. It took humans 25 years to learn to read it — and then to ask a question no bacterium ever asked: can this system work for us?

Chapter TwoScissors in a Test Tube

On June 28, 2012, the answer appeared in Science. Doudna and Charpentier's team showed that Cas9, a protein from the bacterium Streptococcus pyogenes, is a pair of molecular scissors guided by two RNA molecules. Fuse those two RNAs into a single guide RNA, change 20 letters in it, and Cas9 will cut DNA at any chosen location. The paper ran in Science, volume 337, pages 816–821, and its title stated its ambition plainly: a programmable dual-RNA-guided DNA endonuclease.[1]

The point was not the cutting. It was the programmability. Before CRISPR, rewriting a gene meant zinc-finger nucleases or TALENs: for every new target, a new protein had to be designed and built from scratch, a job of months and considerable expense. CRISPR turned that into swapping one short RNA — a job of days, at a fraction of the cost. What collapsed was not the precision, but the price and the speed.

From test tube to living cell took just six months. On January 3, 2013, Feng Zhang's team reported in Science that CRISPR worked in human and mouse cells too. The same scissors had walked out of a bacterial test tube and into the nucleus of a mammalian cell. The paper went on to become the most-cited paper in the genome-editing field.[6][7]

As for the original 2012 paper: by June 2022 it had been cited more than 15,000 times and downloaded nearly 65,000 times — on the day it appeared, it did not make a single news story.[13]

"Although we've not yet demonstrated genome editing,
given the mechanism we describe, it is now a very real possibility."
— Jennifer Doudna, June 28, 2012

Doudna's words were cautious. But science has a recurring pattern: when a technology's cost falls to one percent of what it was, the people who use it are no longer the same people. Once scissors are cheap, the hands holding them are no longer only surgeons' hands.

Chapter ThreeWho Owns the Scissors

The patent war began the same year the scissors appeared. In May 2012, Doudna and Charpentier filed for a patent covering all cells; Zhang filed seven months later, but, having taken the accelerated route, received his patent first, in April 2014. In April 2015, the University of California asked the U.S. Patent Office to declare a patent interference: who had actually invented these scissors first?[16]

On February 15, 2017, the U.S. Patent Trial and Appeal Board ruled that the two inventions did not overlap. The Broad Institute's patents covered only eukaryotic cells — cells with a nucleus, including human cells — while the University of California's application was not limited to any environment. The Broad kept its patents. On February 28, 2022, the board ruled again, awarding the Broad priority of invention for CRISPR-Cas9 in eukaryotic cells.[7][8]

The Nobel Prize gave a shorter, harsher answer. On October 7, 2020, the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences awarded the chemistry prize to Charpentier and Doudna, with 10 million Swedish kronor — about $1.1 million — to share. They were the first two women ever to share a Nobel Prize in the sciences. Zhang did not win it. Neither did Virginijus Siksnys of Lithuania, who had independently produced similar results.[2]

The logic here belongs to institutions, not laboratories. Patent law protects a "patentable invention," not the discovery of a natural phenomenon; the Nobel Prize goes to at most three people. The people who discovered the scissors and the people who first pushed them into a human cell were not the same people — and whose name goes into the textbooks, and who collects the licensing fees, is decided in courtrooms and committee rooms, not at the bench. A scientific discovery belongs to all humanity; a scientific invention can have an owner. CRISPR is both at once, and that is where all of its disputes begin.

Chapter FourLulu and Nana

On November 25, 2018, the Chinese biophysicist He Jiankui uploaded several videos to YouTube; the next day, he announced that the world's first gene-edited babies had been born. Twin girls, Lulu and Nana, born in October 2018; a third gene-edited baby was born in 2019. Using CRISPR, he had altered the embryos' CCR5 gene, hoping to reproduce a natural mutation, CCR5-Δ32, that confers resistance to HIV.[9]

What he did crossed more than one line. First, germline editing is inherited: the change is not only in these two children, but in their descendants, in the entire bloodline. Second, the children were never at meaningful risk of HIV in the first place: their father carried the virus, their mother did not, and sperm washing combined with in vitro fertilization had been an established way since the 1990s for such couples to have healthy children — no gene editing required.[15] Third, he did not even achieve what he claimed: only one of Lulu's two chromosomes carried the edit, and both children still carried unedited, functional copies of the gene.[9]

Fourth, and most damning: the entire experiment was conducted in secret, with no peer review, no published paper, and ethics-review documents that were apparently forged. The story was broken by MIT Technology Review, working from China's clinical-trials registry. Days later, He presented his work at the Second International Summit on Human Genome Editing in Hong Kong, and was met with condemnation.[9]

Three years earlier, science had drawn its own line. From December 1 to 3, 2015, the First International Summit on Human Gene Editing met in Washington, D.C., with nearly 500 scientists, physicians, and ethicists in attendance. Its closing statement said it would be irresponsible to proceed with heritable genome editing until there was broad societal consensus about whether it should be done at all.[10]

He Jiankui crossed that line. On December 30, 2019, the Shenzhen Nanshan District People's Court sentenced him to three years in prison and a fine of 3 million yuan for illegal medical practice; his two collaborators received two years and 18 months. He was released in April 2022. In 2020, China's Civil Code and an amendment to its Criminal Law explicitly banned human gene editing and cloning, with violators facing up to seven years in prison in serious cases.[9]

The lesson of this chapter is not about technology. It is about people. Six years after the scissors were invented, someone used them to rewrite people who had not yet been born. The question was never "can we cut precisely enough?" It was "who has the right to decide for someone who has not been born yet?" A person who wants to be first does not need to wait for societal consensus — only a laboratory, a couple willing to take the risk, and a night when nobody is watching.

Questions Without Answers

To be honest, two things still have no reliable answers, and this article will not pretend otherwise:

  • Exactly how much of the three children's genomes was edited, and how precisely, has never been published in a peer-reviewed paper.
  • The children's long-term health is protected and not publicly disclosed; anything said about their future is speculation.

Chapter FiveA $2.2 Million Dose

The same scissors, at the other end, lead to a clinic. On November 16, 2023, the U.K.'s Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency approved Casgevy, the world's first licensed CRISPR therapy; on December 8, 2023, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration followed, approving it for sickle cell disease patients aged 12 and older with recurrent vaso-occlusive crises; on January 16, 2024, the indication was extended to transfusion-dependent beta-thalassemia.[3][4]

What Casgevy does is surprising: it does not repair the faulty gene. Sickle cell disease comes from a single-letter error in the beta-globin gene, and Casgevy never touches it. Instead, it takes a detour. Doctors take the patient's own blood stem cells out of the body and use CRISPR to cut an enhancer of the BCL11A gene — the switch that turns off fetal hemoglobin after birth. With the switch broken, fetal hemoglobin comes back online, diluting the sickling hemoglobin so red cells stop deforming. After chemotherapy clears the bone marrow, the edited cells are infused back in.[11]

The efficacy is real. The FDA's review records that of 44 patients treated, 31 had enough follow-up to evaluate, and 29 of those 31 — 93.5% — went at least 12 months without a severe vaso-occlusive crisis. The U.K. approval rested on 28 of 29 patients remaining free of severe pain for at least a year after treatment.[11][4]

The price is real, too: $2.2 million for a single dose. Lyfgenia, the other gene therapy approved the same day, was priced at $3.1 million. About 100,000 people in the United States live with sickle cell disease, and roughly 300,000 babies are born with it worldwide every year; yet by mid-2026, two and a half years after approval, only about 69 people in the world had received Casgevy. On July 1, 2026, the FDA lowered the eligible age to two years old, adding roughly 5,500 newly eligible children.[12][14]

This is the most honest chapter of the CRISPR story. The scissors themselves are cheap; one cut costs so much that only a handful of people can afford it. A triumph of technology and a failure of distribution can be true at the same time. The barrier has not disappeared — it has moved from the laboratory to the invoice, and before an invoice, people are not equal.

EpilogueThe Scissors Are Already in Our Hands

Bacteria spent billions of years learning to write their enemies' features into their own genomes. Humans spent 25 years learning to read that memory, and 11 more turning it into a medicine. The next lesson is not technical. It is restraint: what to change, who decides, who pays, and who bears the consequences.

What CRISPR truly rewrites is not genes, but the boundary of the question "what may humans do to themselves?" And that boundary was never decided by the scissors. Scissors only cut. Deciding where to cut — and whether to cut at all — has always belonged to the hands that hold them.

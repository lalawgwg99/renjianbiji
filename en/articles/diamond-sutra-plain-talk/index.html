The Diamond Sutra in Plain Words: How a 1,158-Year-Old Text Made the 2026 Bestseller List

In 17 lessons, Wang Sixun retells the Diamond Sutra as psychology — and lands at number 8 on Eslite's annual chart. What the book gets right, what it leaves out, and why the scroll printed in 868 is still speaking.

PrologueAn Ancient Sutra on the 2026 Bestseller List

On November 17, 2026, Eslite announced its annual bestseller list, and The Diamond Sutra in Plain Words: A Practice of Letting Go took number 8.[6][7] Published by If Publishing on July 28, 2025, the book runs 368 pages in paperback, priced at NT$600,[2] and works through the Diamond Sutra's 32 sections in 17 lessons, line by line.[11] The sutra it expounds was composed between the 2nd and 5th centuries CE,[10] first translated into Chinese by Kumārajīva in 401.[10] A Buddhist text nearly 2,000 years old outsold almost every new book in Taiwan in 2026 — a fact worth asking about.

One number needs correcting first. "2,500 years" is the age of the Buddha's lifetime, not of this sutra. The Buddha lived about 2,500 years ago; the text was written down and transmitted in India over the centuries after his death, and Kumārajīva carried it into the Chinese-speaking world.[10] Making the sutra 500 years older than it is sounds more impressive, but it is not accurate. This deep read will do the same kind of checking throughout: take apart why the book is popular, verify each piece, and then ask what it gets right and what it misses.

Chapter OneWho Is Wang Sixun?

Wang Sixun is neither a professor of Buddhist studies nor an ordained monk. The public record on him runs to only a few lines: a graduate of the Graduate Institute of Philosophy at Nanhua University, a former lecturer in bioethics at Hsuan Chuang University, a former editor-in-chief at Cite Publishing's Fruit imprint, editor-in-chief of the Chinese edition of The Cambridge Dictionary of Philosophy in 2002, and now editor-in-chief of If Publishing, founder of the Le Yi Lecture Hall, and a lecturer on China Broadcasting Corporation's Zizi audio platform.[4] He has immersed himself in the Book of Changes for 40 years and taught for more than 10, publishing The Book of Changes in Plain Words in 2022 and The Diamond Sutra in Plain Words in 2025.[4]

His connection with the Diamond Sutra began in his second year of university. Then a student at National Chung Hsing University, he passed the office of the Wisdom Sea Society on a hot afternoon, drawn in by an atmosphere of stillness and coolness, and sat down to read a copy of the Diamond Sutra from its bookshelf. He could not understand it then, but the feeling stayed with him for life.[3] He began teaching the Heart Sutra and the Diamond Sutra about 7 years ago, starting with the Heart Sutra and then the Diamond Sutra — only to find that the Heart Sutra, short as it is, runs deeper and is harder for beginners, so he reversed the order and now teaches the Diamond Sutra first.[3]

That background sets the book's tone: it is not academic Buddhist scholarship, nor a monk's sermon, but the notes of an editor and lecturer who has read the classics for decades,gathered into a book. Wang is candid about this in his preface — he is not a specialist in Buddhist studies, and what the book offers is his own learning and lived practice, useful as reference but not to be taken as the standard answer.[3] That sentence is the most important thing to remember before reading the book.

Chapter TwoWhat the Diamond Sutra Actually Says

The sutra's full Sanskrit title is the Vajracchedikā Prajñāpāramitā Sūtra — "the perfection of wisdom that cuts like a thunderbolt," a wisdom hard and sharp as diamond, able to cut through all attachment. It belongs to the Mahayana "perfection of wisdom" literature; scholars date its composition somewhere between the 2nd and 5th centuries,[10] and the earliest Chinese translation is Kumārajīva's Vajracchedikā Prajñāpāramitā Sūtra of 401. Prince Zhaoming, son of Emperor Wu of Liang, later divided it into 32 sections for recitation.[10] At just over 5,000 characters, it is a text almost every Chinese speaker has heard a line or two from.

The whole sutra is a dialogue: the Buddha answers his disciple Subhuti's question, "How should one subdue the mind?" Wang points out a telling contrast: the Heart Sutra talks about emptiness and never about the mind — the character for "mind" does not appear in it at all — while the Diamond Sutra talks about the mind and never directly about emptiness. Its title contains no "mind," yet the character appears more than 40 times in the text, while "emptiness" appears only 3 times, and in senses that have nothing to do with Buddhist emptiness.[3] So the sutra's method, as he calls it, is "speaking of emptiness through the mind" — emptiness cannot be described directly; it can only be known through how we use the mind.[3]

The sutra's most famous line is "arouse the mind without dwelling anywhere."[1] The mind should not come to rest on anything — not on gain or loss, liking or disliking, success or failure — and in that state of not dwelling, it naturally begins to work. Paired with it is the line "all appearances are false": everything the eyes see and the hands grasp is only what the mind has decided it to be, not the thing itself.[1] Wang renders this in plain language: the mind likes to decide that some things are good and others bad; the decision hardens into a tendency, the tendency into control, until head and body are both pulled along by the decision — and that is what it means to drift with karma.[3] Letting go means breaking out of that decision.

The sutra returns again and again to the example of giving: a person who gives away great wealth earns great merit, but one who can let go of the very idea of merit earns merit greater still, immeasurable and inconceivable.[3] Wang's reading is that as long as you keep score in terms of cause and effect, you remain bound by cause and effect; only by letting go of merit do you win freedom from it.[3] That is the key to the whole book: "letting go of attachment" does not mean wanting nothing — it means refusing to be tied down by your own fixed judgements.

Chapter ThreeThe 868 Scroll from Dunhuang

One printed copy of this sutra is extraordinary. Printed on May 11, 868, it now sits in the British Library under the shelfmark Or.8210/P.2.[8] Its colophon reads: on the 15th day of the 4th month of the 9th year of the Xiantong reign, Wang Jie reverently made this sutra for his two parents, for universal free distribution.[8] Converted to the Western calendar, that is May 11, 868 — the 9th year of the Xiantong era under Emperor Yizong of Tang.[8] The scroll is more than 5 metres long, made of 7 sheets of paper pasted together, and opens with a woodcut frontispiece of the Buddha seated on a lotus throne, preaching to Subhuti.[12] It is the world's earliest surviving complete printed book with a definite date — 587 years before the Gutenberg Bible.[12]

The scroll's fate is as ironic as its teaching. It was printed to circulate — Wang Jie paid for it to pray for his parents, and the colophon says it was made "for universal free distribution." But it was later sealed into the Library Cave, Cave 17 of the Mogao grottoes at Dunhuang, where it lay in the dark for nearly 900 years, until the Daoist caretaker Wang Yuanlu discovered it in 1900 and the explorer Aurel Stein carried it away in 1907, to end up in London.[8] A scroll printed to be given away reached the world by first disappearing for nearly 900 years, then reappearing as "the world's earliest printed book."

The British Library states the claim carefully: it is "the world's earliest dated printed book," the earliest complete survival of a dated printed book.[8][9] Earlier printed matter does exist, but it carries no date that can be firmly established, or survives only in fragments. The qualifier matters: 868 is not the year printing was invented, but the first year in which we can point to a complete printed object and state its exact birthday. Chinese Buddhists had been using printing since the 8th century,[8] and by the time this scroll was made, printing was already a mature craft.

Chapter FourWhy It Took Off in 2026

Eslite's own note on the list says it plainly: the book "approaches the classic from a de-religionised, practical-psychology angle, readable even without faith, and reaches many young readers and spiritual seekers through the long tail of social video."[6] The United Daily News report used the same judgement: a de-religionised, practical-psychology reading that speaks precisely to the modern pain of mental burnout.[7] In other words, the book is popular not because Taiwan suddenly wants to study Buddhism, but because it translated a sutra into the language Taiwan understands best in 2026: psychology.

That translation has three points of entry. The first is the word "letting go." On the same 2026 Eslite list, The Let Them Theory took number 7, one place ahead, with the same message of releasing attachment and cutting burnout;[6] the two books entering the chart together speak to one mood of the times: people are not short of advice, they are short of a way to let go. The second entry is the author's voice. Wang has taught the classics for years on the Zizi audio platform,[4] and Eslite's own podcast invited him on in August 2025 for an episode titled "You don't have to believe in Buddhism to meet the Diamond Sutra."[5] The voice came first, the book followed, and listeners became readers. The third entry is age. Wang observes that a person's "religious nature" generally awakens between the ages of 40 and 45 — halfway through a life, when one starts asking what life means.[3] Readers of that age are the bookshop's most willing buyers of a book about letting go.

The numbers on the side confirm the heat: on Books.com.tw, as checked on November 18, 2026, the book held a 5-star rating from 25 raters, with 10 reviews almost uniformly positive.[2] Readers put it directly: one spent a month reading it slowly and felt as if sitting there listening to the Buddha teach; another wrote that much of what psychology and brain science now say, the Diamond Sutra had already pointed out.[2] These reviews are not all accurate, but they show what is really being bought: not a sutra, but the comfort of discovering that the ancients had already thought of it.

Chapter FiveWhat It Gets Right — and What It Misses

The book's greatest merit is that it takes the threshold down. Traditional commentaries on the Diamond Sutra, starting from Kumārajīva's translation itself, stand behind a wall of classical Chinese and Buddhist terminology. Wang's approach goes around the wall: instead of starting from the abstract concept of emptiness, he starts from the mind — from the likes, dislikes, judgements, and things we cannot let go of, which everyone already has.[3] For a reader with no Buddhist background, that is the only road in. His other merit is honesty: he does not pose as an authority, and says plainly at the outset that this is personal understanding, not to be taken as the standard answer.[3] A bestseller willing to write that deserves more trust than many motivational books that state everything as fact.

But the misses grow in the same soil. De-religionising is a double-edged blade: it carries the Diamond Sutra into bookshops and podcasts, but it also files off the hardest parts of the text. Giving, keeping precepts, patience, and the bodhi mind are all gathered in this book into the single pocket of "letting go of attachment," and turned into techniques of self-management. Yet for a Mahayana sutra, giving is not a psychological exercise — it is where the practitioner stands inside cause and effect; and the bodhi mind is not self-improvement, it is a vow made for all beings. When everything is translated into "how to feel better," the sutra becomes a high-grade self-help book — readable, but thinner.

The second gap is that "speaking of emptiness through the mind" easily slides into "speaking of emptiness through psychology." One Books.com.tw reviewer writes that the Diamond Sutra is practically the founding ancestor of modern brain science and psychology.[2] It sounds like praise, but it is a misreading: the "appearances" the sutra speaks of are the falsehoods the mind decides upon, not a popular account of neural circuits; and "letting go" is not an emotion-management skill, but a release of one's grip on the whole world of cause and effect. To call 2,000-year-old wisdom a prophecy of 21st-century psychology is to measure an old cloth with today's ruler — the measurement may look neat, but it is not the cloth's original size.

The third gap is the most practical: the word "letting go" is too useful, and therefore too easily misused. In the sutra, letting go comes after seeing — first notice where you are attached, then release the grip.[3] In everyday use, it is often read as giving up, not caring, never mind. A person mistreated at work reads "let go" and swallows the insult; a relationship that needs attention reads "let go" and is left unattended. That is not Wang's fault — his book is clear that awareness must come before letting go.[3] But once a book is popular enough to be in every hand, its fine print gets eaten by its slogan, and only the two words "let go" keep circulating. That is the shared fate of every bestselling plain-words classic, and something readers must watch out for themselves.

EpilogueAfter Letting Go

Close the book and return to the scroll of 868. Wang Jie paid to have it printed, and its colophon says it was made "for universal free distribution" — for his parents, and for everyone who would read it.[8] 1,158 years later, the sutra circulates in another form: a 368-page plain-words lecture that reached number 8 on Eslite's annual list.[2][6] The means of circulation have changed. The question the sutra asks has not: where does your mind dwell right now?

Wang's answer is that awareness and observation are the root of all practice, and letting go of attachment is the key to practising the perfection of wisdom.[3] Whether that answer holds, each reader must test it in their own life. The sutra says, "arouse the mind without dwelling anywhere";[1] to dwell is to come to rest — on a thought, an identity, an idea of what "I should be." Letting go is not throwing things away; it is letting the mind stop resting on them.

There is only so much a book can do: it can take a text 2,000 years old and tell it in a way that makes someone scrolling on the metro stop and listen to a lesson. This book does that. What happens after the listening — whether the mind learns to stop dwelling — is a matter for after the book is closed.

Faraday: How a Bookbinder's Apprentice Lit the World

On 29 August 1831, in a basement laboratory, he wound two coils around an iron ring — and the world gained electricity

PrologueThe Day in the Basement

Monday, 29 August 1831. Albemarle Street, Mayfair, London. In the basement laboratory of the Royal Institution, a 39-year-old man was doing something that looked exceedingly dull: winding copper wire around an iron ring.[1]

The ring was soft iron, 6 inches across — about 15 centimetres. Around one half of it he wound 3 lengths of copper wire, each about 24 feet long, separated by twine and calico; around the other half he wound 2 lengths, about 60 feet in all. Then he connected a battery — 10 pairs of metal plates, 4 inches square — and set a magnetic needle 3 feet away, and watched it.[2]

The moment he connected the battery, the needle moved. Then it stopped.

Most people would have put that down to the jolt of connection. He did not walk away. He disconnected the battery — and the needle moved again, in the opposite direction. He connected and disconnected, over and over; every time, the needle jumped, and every time in the opposite direction. In that moment he understood: a current is not produced while magnetism flows, but while it changes. Change the magnetic field, and electricity is born.[1][2]

He wrote the day's experiments into his diary, numbered from 1. That diary would become one of the most important experimental records in the history of science. And 29 August 1831 became the day humanity first drew an electric current out of a magnet with its own hands.[2]

Every unit of electricity in your home today — hydro, thermal, wind — runs on the same principle: change the magnetic field, and a current appears. Generators, transformers, electric motors — all of them grew out of that iron ring in the basement. And the man who found it was a bookbinder's apprentice who never finished secondary school.

Chapter 1The Boy in the Bookbindery

Michael Faraday was born on 22 September 1791 in Newington, south London. His father was a blacksmith; the family was poor, and six mouths often went hungry. He later recalled that in the worst times, a single loaf of bread had to last a week.[4]

The Faradays belonged to the Sandemanians, a small sect that had split from the Church of Scotland, devoted to reading the Bible literally and opposed to the accumulation of worldly wealth. The faith stayed with him all his life and shaped every decision he later made: he never chased money or titles — not out of snobbery, but because he genuinely believed those things did not matter.[4]

He had only the most basic schooling. At 13 he became an errand boy in a bookshop; at 14 he was apprenticed to a bookbinder, and stayed for 7 years. The shop was on Blandford Street, run by George Riebau. To other apprentices, binding was a trade; to Faraday, it was a library. Every book that passed through his hands, he read at night — chemistry, electricity, natural philosophy, anything. He also attended lectures at the City Philosophical Society, taking notes as he listened, building his own record of what he heard.[5]

In 1812, a customer gave him tickets to hear Sir Humphry Davy, the most celebrated chemist in Britain, lecture at the Royal Institution. Faraday turned his notes from four lectures into a book of nearly 300 pages, bound it himself, and sent it to Davy with a letter: please give me a chance to work in science.[5]

Davy was won over. On 1 March 1813, Faraday was hired as a chemical assistant at the Royal Institution. He was 21 years old. From that day on, he never left Albemarle Street.[1][4]

When he arrived, Davy was the most famous scientist in Britain, and Faraday was an apprentice who barely knew Latin. No one — Davy included — could have guessed that this apprentice would become the soul of the Royal Institution within 50 years.

Chapter 2The European Tour and the First Motor

Soon after he joined, Davy took him on a tour of Europe — France, Italy, Belgium, Switzerland, from 1813 to 1815, nearly 2 years. The journey was Faraday's first university: he met Ampère and Volta, the great scientists of the Continent, climbed Mount Vesuvius, and watched a Europe in the throes of the Napoleonic Wars remaking itself.[5]

Back in London, he began to experiment on his own. In 1821, he built the first device in history to turn electricity into motion: a wire carrying a current, spinning endlessly around a magnet. That was the principle of the electric motor.[1]

But the success got him into trouble. When he published, he failed to mention Davy's and Wollaston's earlier work on the problem, and Davy was displeased. Faraday was reassigned to other projects — optical glass, six years of it, with little to show. It was not until Davy died in 1829 that he could give his time back to electromagnetism.[1]

The episode says much about Faraday's position: a man with no degree and no pedigree, in an academic world that prized birth and manners, with less room to make mistakes than anyone else. But he had something no one else had: patience. Those six years with optical glass were not wasted — they gave him a pair of hands no one could match, and it was those hands that later let him do experiments no one else could do.

In June 1821, he married Sarah Barnard, the daughter of a Sandemanian elder. They had no children, and stayed together for life.[4]

Chapter 3Ten Years of Waiting

The years from 1821 to 1831 were Faraday's decade of waiting. He was not idle: in 1823 he liquefied chlorine, proving that so-called "permanent" gases could be turned into liquids under pressure; in 1825 he isolated a new compound from the residue of London's illuminating gas, calling it "bicarburet of hydrogen" — we call it benzene today; the same year, he became director of the laboratory at the Royal Institution.[1][5]

In 1825 he also founded the Christmas Lectures for young people, explaining science through live demonstrations during the Christmas break; in 1826 he founded the Friday Evening Discourses for adult audiences. He gave 19 series of Christmas Lectures himself. The most famous was The Chemical History of a Candle in 1848 — six lectures on a single candle, later published as a book and still a model of science writing. The series continues to this day, the longest-running science lectures in the world.[5]

But one question kept nagging at him. In 1820, the Dane Ørsted had shown that an electric current deflects a magnetic needle — electricity makes magnetism. Faraday turned the question around: if electricity can make magnetism, can magnetism make electricity? He tried for 10 years, and failed for 10 years.

The trouble was that he was looking in the wrong place. He assumed that if you put a magnet next to a coil, current would flow out like water. But the magnet just sat there, and nothing happened. It was not until August 1831 that he changed the question: perhaps the point is not the existence of magnetism, but its change.

Chapter 4The Ring, the Coils, and the Lines of Force

The answer was in those two jumps of the needle on 29 August 1831: a current appears only in the instant the magnetic field changes. Connect the battery, the field goes from nothing to something, and an induced current appears for a moment; disconnect it, the field goes from something to nothing, and a current appears in the opposite direction. A stationary magnetic field does nothing; only a changing one makes electricity.[1][2]

Over the following weeks he pushed the discovery to its limit: plunging a magnet into a coil and pulling it out, current appearing and vanishing with it; spinning a copper disk between the poles of a magnet, and a steady current flowing out without end — the first electric generator in history.[3]

The American Joseph Henry made the same discovery independently in 1832, but Faraday published first, and priority was his. Both men's names later became units: the farad for capacitance, the henry for inductance.[3]

Faraday did not stop at "it moves." He asked: how does magnetic force actually travel? Physicists of his day believed in action at a distance — a magnet pulling a needle through empty air, with nothing in between. Faraday did not believe it. Watching iron filings arrange themselves in patterns around a magnet, he proposed an entirely new picture: the space around a magnet is filled with invisible "lines of force," and force travels along those lines.[1]

The idea seemed strange at the time. Faraday knew almost no mathematics — he never used more than arithmetic in his life — and could not write lines of force as equations. The mathematicians of the academies dismissed them. But 30 years later, a young Scotsman translated Faraday's lines of force into mathematics: James Clerk Maxwell. Maxwell was born in 1831 — the very year Faraday discovered electromagnetic induction. In 1865, Maxwell published his equations of the electromagnetic field, unifying electricity, magnetism and light into a single phenomenon. Faraday's intuition became the foundation of modern physics.[1][3]

Between 1833 and 1834, Faraday also completed his laws of electrolysis: the mass of a substance liberated in electrolysis depends only on the quantity of electricity passed; the same quantity of electricity always liberates chemically equivalent amounts of different substances. The laws hinted at a conclusion no one then dared to say aloud: electricity itself comes in discrete portions, not as a continuous fluid. A century later, that idea of "portions" grew into electronics.[1]

He also popularised words we now take for granted: anode, cathode, electrode, ion.[4]

Chapter 5The Cage, the Light, and the Refusals

In January 1836, Faraday staged a famous demonstration: he had a wooden frame pasted with paper and covered in wire mesh built into a room 12 feet square, stepped inside it, and had it electrified. Outside the cage, sparks flew; inside, he felt nothing. Charge resides only on the outer surface of a conductor; the interior is untouched. The "Faraday cage" still protects aeroplanes, cars, and the microwave ovens in our kitchens today.[1][5]

But years of relentless work broke him. In 1839 he suffered a nervous breakdown, and for six years he did almost no creative science. When he returned to the laboratory in 1845, he came back with two great discoveries at once: on 13 September, he found that a magnetic field rotates polarised light — the Faraday effect, the first proof that light and magnetism are related; that same autumn, he discovered that all matter responds to a magnetic field — diamagnetism.[1][4]

Fame and honours poured in, and Faraday turned them away one by one. He declined the knighthood Queen Victoria offered him, saying he wished to remain "plain Mr. Faraday to the end." He twice refused the presidency of the Royal Society. During the Crimean War, he refused to work on chemical weapons for the British army. He once told a publisher that he had always loved science more than money, and that a life in science could not make a man rich — nor did he want it to.[4]

He was no hermit, though. During the Great Stink, he wrote to The Times protesting the foul state of the Thames; he investigated industrial pollution at Swansea and air pollution at the Royal Mint. In his hands, science was never only a laboratory matter.[4]

Faraday died on 25 August 1867 at Hampton Court, aged 75. The house the Queen had provided for him there became his last home. He declined burial in Westminster Abbey, and was buried in Highgate Cemetery, beside Sarah's grave.[4]

EpiloguePhysics Without Equations

Faraday left behind some 450 publications, yet never wrote a single differential equation. His weapons were of another kind: a pair of hands that could do experiments, and a pair of eyes that could see what was invisible. The lines of force around a magnet — others could not see them; he could. The current that appears only while things change — others did not notice; he did.[1]

In his diary he once wrote: "Nothing is too wonderful to be true, if it be consistent with the laws of nature." The line is almost a portrait of the man himself: a bookbinder's apprentice who, with no degree, no mathematics, and no pedigree — only doing, watching, and waiting patiently — rewrote the way the world uses energy.[1]

If Faraday were alive today, and saw his lines of force turned into equations, into power grids, into every chip in every phone — would he be delighted, or would he feel something had been lost?

Generators all over the world are still turning, and they still turn on the principle of that iron ring of 1831. The Christmas Lectures are still given, still in the manner he began in 1825: science performed live, for children to see. Faraday left no company, no patents, no title. What he left was proof of how far an ordinary person can go.

"God Wills It": How One Sentence Sent an Age Eastward

The First Crusade, 1095–1099: an expedition with no name, and the nine-hundred-year echo it left behind

SlowFolio Editorial Team · Reading time 14 min

繁中

Narrated in Mandarin · Female voice HsiaoChen

The Same Era · 1095

  • March 1095In March 1095, King William II of England confronted Archbishop Anselm of Canterbury at the Council of Rockingham: Anselm sought leave to travel to Rome to receive his pallium from the pope, the king refused on the grounds of recognising no pope, and the council ended without a decision — the same Investiture Controversy playing out across Europe in different forms.
  • June 1094In June 1094, the Castilian mercenary leader El Cid (Rodrigo Díaz de Vivar) captured Muslim-ruled Valencia after a siege of nearly 20 months, ruling it directly until his death in 1099 — while Urban II was calling for the East, Christians and Muslims on the Iberian Peninsula were fighting a war of a similar kind that no one called a crusade.

PrologueA Sentence in a Field

On 27 November 1095, outside the walls of the small French town of Clermont, a crowd too large for the cathedral stood in an open field and listened to a pope speak. No one there knew they were witnessing the start of the largest mass mobilisation in European history — one sentence that would send tens of thousands of people to abandon their fields and march 3,000 kilometres to war.

The Council of Clermont had not been convened to start a war. From 18 to 28 November 1095, Pope Urban II presided over a church council concerned with familiar reform business: simony, clerical marriage, and the adultery of King Philip I of France.[3] On the final day, the pope stepped outside the cathedral — so many people had come that the building could not hold them, and the papal throne was set up in a field beyond the eastern gate.[3]

The speech we read today survives in four or five versions that differ considerably. Fulcher of Chartres, writing about five years after the event, is regarded as the closest to what was actually said; the versions by Robert the Monk, Baldric of Dol, and Guibert of Nogent each add their own colour.[1] In 1906, the historian D. C. Munro concluded in the American Historical Review that the pope's exact words can no longer be recovered.[1] Yet the versions agree remarkably on the substance: Christians in the East were suffering, Jerusalem lay in infidel hands, the Franks should stop fighting each other, and all who took the road would have their sins remitted.[3]

The crowd's answer became the name of the movement — although the name itself would not appear for another century. According to tradition, the field rang with a shout: "Deus vult!" God wills it.[3] Whether those words were truly cried out that day is still debated; but they capture the essence of the moment perfectly. When a decision is presented as God's will, hesitation loses its footing.

Chapter OneOne Plea for Help, Two Imaginations

The story began not at Clermont but with a plea for help. In March 1095, envoys from the Byzantine emperor Alexios I appeared at the Council of Piacenza, asking the pope for aid.[6] Twenty-four years earlier, at Manzikert in 1071, the Seljuk Turks had captured the Byzantine emperor Romanos IV, and the empire had since lost great swathes of Anatolia.[6] What Alexios wanted was specific: a mercenary force to help him recover lost territory.

What he got was far more than he asked for, and far different from what he imagined. Urban II rewrote a military request as an armed pilgrimage. The emperor wanted a few hundred professional soldiers; the pope mobilised an entire society. From the start, East and West understood this enterprise differently: the Byzantines thought reinforcements were coming; the Franks thought they were pilgrims marching to liberate the Holy City. The emperor wanted his provinces back; the crusaders wanted kingdoms of their own. This misunderstanding was not an accident — it was the starting point. Everyone heard the sentence they wanted to hear.

Nor was Urban inventing the idea from nothing. In 1074, Pope Gregory VII had called for a similar expedition, and almost no one had answered.[6] By the late 11th century, pilgrimage to Jerusalem was already fashionable: in 1064, Bishop Gunther of Bamberg led some 7,000 pilgrims there, and the hardships they suffered were retold across Europe.[6] Pilgrimage, penance, and holy war — three separate threads — were twisted into a single rope in Urban's hands. As the historian Jonathan Riley-Smith put it, crusading was not a gradual merging of traditions; it was fused by Pope Urban II in a single act in 1095.

Chapter TwoWho Abandons Their Fields and Walks 3,000 Kilometres?

One sentence can send tens of thousands of people marching only if those people are already standing at the roadside. Late 11th-century Western Europe was a society full of surplus violence and surplus anxiety. With the Vikings and Magyars converted and the frontiers quiet, Europe was left with a class of warriors who had nothing to do but fight each other.[6] The Church's Peace of God and Truce of God movements tried to restrain knights from fighting on certain days and in certain places, with little effect.[6] Urban's genius was that he did not try to abolish violence; he gave it an exit. Stop fighting here. Go and fight in the East.

For knights, this was a chance to bundle every desire into a single bargain. Under primogeniture, younger sons inherited no land; Jerusalem, said to be paved with gold and gated with sapphire, promised land, plunder, and fame. Raymond of Toulouse, old and rich and wanting for nothing, was the first of the great lords to take the cross; Godfrey of Bouillon mortgaged his estates before departing, staking everything he owned.[6] Yet it would be unfair to call them mere opportunists. Many were sincerely devout: medieval people believed hell was real, that time in purgatory could be shortened by penance, and that the pope was offering a plenary indulgence. To someone who believed in the Last Judgement, that was not rhetoric. It was the terms of a deal.

Deeper still was the logic of pilgrimage. A pilgrimage was more meritorious the harder it was: the longer the road, the greater the danger, the heavier the remission. The crusade simply pushed that logic to its extreme — exchanging the pilgrim's staff for a sword, and a city to be visited for a city to be taken. On 15 August 1096, the Feast of the Assumption, the princes' armies set out: some 60,000 soldiers, plus a roughly equal number of non-combatants, a total approaching 100,000.[6] This was not an army. It was a migration.

Chapter ThreeThose Who Set Out First

But history remembers those who set out first, not those who followed the plan. The pope's appointed departure date was 15 August 1096, and the princes' armies kept to it. Months earlier, however, another force was already on the road: Peter the Hermit, a monk from Amiens, had been preaching across France and Germany, gathering a "People's Crusade" of peasants, women, and children.[6]

This host had no pay, no discipline, and no maps — only enthusiasm that had been set alight. When enthusiasm finds no outlet, it looks for a scapegoat close at hand. In May and June 1096, the Jewish communities of the Rhineland were slaughtered: Speyer, Worms, Mainz, Cologne — thousands killed, many more forced to convert.[6] Bands led by Count Emicho marched under the sign of the cross and did the work of robbery and murder. Local bishops tried to shelter the Jews and were powerless to stop it. This was the first blood of the crusade, shed not in the Holy Land but in Europe's own cities. It revealed a cruel pattern: once a movement defines the "infidel" as the enemy, the nearest available "other" is usually the first to suffer. The Jewish communities had nothing to do with the Muslims, yet because they too stood outside Christendom, they became the first victims.

The People's Crusade ended as miserably as it had begun. Looting its way through Hungary and the Balkans, it reached Constantinople with a ruined reputation, and Alexios hurried it across the Bosporus. In October 1096, this untrained host was ambushed by the Turks at Civetot in Asia Minor and almost annihilated; the survivors were sold into slavery.[6] The pope had envisaged a disciplined noble expedition; what reality delivered first was an uncontrolled mass movement. The first lesson of crowd psychology: once a call goes out, it no longer belongs to the one who issued it.

Chapter FourThree Years on the Road: Hunger, the Holy Lance, and Quarrels

The princes' advance was a slow war of attrition. In June 1097, the combined armies besieged Nicaea, capital of the Sultanate of Rum. As the city fell, the Byzantine emperor negotiated its surrender first, and the crusaders were denied even the plunder.[6] It was the first settlement of the East–West misunderstanding: the crusaders felt betrayed, while the emperor felt he was simply recovering what was his. On 1 July 1097, at Dorylaeum, the crusaders defeated Sultan Kilij Arslan, opening the road across Anatolia.[6]

The true ordeal was Antioch. From October 1097 to June 1098, an eight-month siege brought famine, disease, and desertion. During the siege, Baldwin of Boulogne broke away and seized Edessa, founding the first crusader state.[6] On 3 June 1098, Antioch finally fell through a gate opened by an insider — only for the crusaders to find themselves besieged inside the city within days by a relief army under Kerbogha of Mosul. In the depths of despair, word spread that the Holy Lance — the spear said to have pierced Christ's side — had been discovered in the city.[6] Whether it was genuine remains a mystery, but it did its work: on 28 June, the crusaders marched out and routed Kerbogha's army.

The story of the Lance is worth pausing over. How could a spear of unknown provenance convince an army that had starved for eight months and lost half its men that it would win? Because in desperate straits, people need meaning more than evidence: we are not trapped remnants; we are chosen instruments. The second lesson of crowd psychology: once a belief becomes collective, it starts producing the very evidence it needs.

Victory was followed by quarrelling. Who should hold Antioch, what the emperor had promised, and when to march on Jerusalem — the lords argued for months. Adhemar, Bishop of Le Puy, the papal legate and the one man the whole army respected, died of plague in the summer of 1098, leaving the army without an arbiter.[6] In the end it was the ordinary soldiers who forced the leaders onward: at Ma'arrat they tore down the walls to compel the hesitant Raymond to continue south.[6] In January 1099, the army finally marched on Jerusalem, with only some 12,000 to 15,000 fighting men left.[6]

Chapter FiveJerusalem, 15 July 1099

On 7 June 1099, the crusaders reached Jerusalem. The city was no longer in Turkish hands — in 1098, the Fatimid Caliphate of Egypt had retaken it from the Turks.[6] The siege lasted more than five weeks. Short of water and timber, the army built siege towers from wood stripped from ships at Jaffa; on 17 June, a Genoese fleet arrived with engineers and timber.[6] The final assault began on the night of 13–14 July; on 15 July, the city fell.

What followed was the darkest page of the expedition. The chroniclers of the assault — Fulcher of Chartres and Raymond of Aguilers — recorded the massacre of the city's Muslim and Jewish inhabitants in tones approaching celebration: those who sheltered in the mosques of the Temple Mount and in the synagogues were not spared.[2] Modern historians disagree widely on the death toll, with estimates ranging from thousands to tens of thousands. But the scale and deliberateness of the killing are attested consistently across the sources.[6] Fulcher wrote that the victors "waded in blood" in the Temple of Solomon. These words would be quoted for centuries, becoming one of the deepest wounds in Muslim collective memory.

It is worth noting that the massacre was recorded and celebrated by the same people. Fulcher was no enemy historian; he was an army chaplain, a witness and an advocate of the victory. He did not conceal it, because in his telling it was not an atrocity but the completion of a divine judgement. The same event is glory in the victor's narrative and trauma in the victim's memory, and the historical truth often lies in the crack between the two stories.

After the fall of the city, the crusaders established the Kingdom of Jerusalem; Godfrey refused the title of king, styling himself Advocate of the Holy Sepulchre. Together with Edessa, Antioch, and the later County of Tripoli, four crusader states took shape along the Levantine coast.[6] On 12 August 1099, the crusaders repelled a Fatimid counterattack at Ascalon, and the First Crusade came to an end.[6] Urban II, who had set it all in motion, died on 29 July 1099 — the news of Jerusalem's fall reached Rome 14 days after his death. He died never knowing that his call had come true.[6]

Chapter SixThe Name Came Later

Throughout the whole expedition, one word never appeared: "crusade". The people of 1095 did not call themselves crusaders, nor did they call this war a crusade. Their words were iter (journey), expeditio (expedition), and peregrinatio (pilgrimage); they called themselves peregrini — pilgrims.[7] They sewed crosses onto their garments, which in time gave rise to crucesignati — "those signed with the cross"; the Spanish crozada is first attested in 1212, and the English word "crusade" was not fixed until 1706.[7]

Names are labels pasted on by later generations, and labels rewrite memory in return. Once it is called "the Crusades", a tangled migration is filed neatly into a clean framework of religious war: Christians against Muslims, a holy war. But the scene in 1095 was far messier: the pope was thinking of papal authority, the emperor of mercenaries, the knights of land and remission, the peasants of escape from their lords, the merchants of trade — each walking their own road, which happened to run in the same direction.

It was neither a conspiracy designed by one man nor the collective will of a nation, but an accidental convergence of many motives. Urban II's genius lay not in inventing something new, but in finding a single sentence — remission and the Holy City — capacious enough to hold so many unrelated longings at once. Once spoken, the longings found their own way.

If on 27 November 1095 no one in that field outside Clermont had cried out — or if they had cried out and no one had followed — would the next nine hundred years of Europe and the Middle East have taken a different shape?

EpilogueAfter "God Wills It"

The military achievement of the First Crusade lasted fewer than two hundred years: in 1187, Saladin retook Jerusalem; in 1291, Acre, the last stronghold, fell, and the crusader states vanished.[6] But what it left behind outlived the land it conquered.

The first survivor was trade. An expedition needs ships, ports, and supplies, and the Italian maritime cities — Venice, Genoa, Pisa — became indispensable carriers and financiers, rebuilding a commercial network across the Mediterranean.[6] Spices, silk, sugar, and glassware flowed into Europe; a merchant class rose, and cities grew rich. The war ended. The shipping lanes remained.

The second was a channel for knowledge. The crusader states became "contact zones" where Latin Christians, Byzantine Greeks, Muslim scholars, and Jewish communities met directly; through Sicily, Toledo, and the Levant, the works of Aristotle, Galen, and Euclid — Greek learning preserved and developed in the Arabic world — were translated into Latin and entered the schools of Europe.[6] This was not loot the soldiers carried home, but a by-product of contact itself.

The heaviest legacy was the hardening of relationships. The massacres of Jerusalem in 1099 and of the Rhineland in 1096 became founding traumas in Muslim and Jewish collective memory; in later Arabic and Turkish usage, the word "crusade" became a synonym for Western aggression, and it is still invoked in political language today. At the same time, the movement remade Europe itself: papal prestige reached its peak, and the military orders — the Knights Templar, founded in 1119, and the Hospitallers, recognised by the pope in 1113 — became military and financial powers spanning the continent, while chivalry and pilgrim fervour coloured the culture of late medieval Europe.[6]

So back to the original question: why could one sentence send tens of thousands of people to abandon their fields and walk 3,000 kilometres? The answer is not in the sentence, but in the people. An age that believed in judgement after death; a younger son with no inheritance; a killer seeking remission; a peasant wanting to escape his lord — each of them had already wanted to leave. All they lacked was a reason that made leaving seem legitimate, even holy. Urban II gave them that reason. The most dangerous moments in history are often not when bad men issue a call, but when ordinary, devout people finally find a reason they no longer need to hesitate.

On 29 July 1099, the pope died in Rome, unaware that Jerusalem had fallen. The peasants who died at Civetot, the Muslims and Jews who died beneath the walls of Jerusalem, the crusaders who died at Antioch — most of their names are lost. What remains is a sentence — Deus vult — and the question behind it, which has not aged at all: when a person is certain that God is on their side, what limits can they still set for themselves?

The Great Fire of London: How One Bakery Burned Down a City

Four days, 13,200 houses, six official deaths — how a fire forced a city to replace its own bones

PrologueOne O'Clock in the Morning, at the Bakery

Just after 1 a.m. on Sunday, 2 September 1666, a bakery caught fire in Pudding Lane, in the City of London. The baker, Thomas Farriner, and his family escaped across the rooftops. Only a maidservant, too frightened to climb out, stayed inside — the fire's first, and perhaps only officially certain, victim.[1] Nobody that night imagined that the embers of one bakery would burn down a city in four days.

Farriner baked for the King, supplying bread to the Royal Navy. His ovens never went cold — that was how a bakery worked then: the fire stayed lit, the business stayed open. No one knows which ember escaped first that night: a spark from the oven, firewood stacked against the wall, a lump of coal not properly banked. A fire always starts with something small, too small to be worth recording.

This book is not about a fire. It is about how a city discovered, in a single night, that the home it had spent 600 years building was a pile of kindling waiting for a match.

Chapter 1A City Built of Wood

In 1666, London was the third-largest city in the Western world, home to some 400,000 people. Inside the old Roman walls, the ancient City held about 80,000, packed into houses that leaned toward each other until their upper storeys nearly met above the street.[1]

The city was built of wood. Brick and stone belonged to the merchants' mansions at the centre; the poorer parishes were timber frames, wattle and daub, thatch and wooden shingles. Since 1580, successive governments had ordered new houses to be built of brick and stone, but the poor could not afford brick, and the law governed new building, not the wooden city that already stood.[3] London's fire service was buckets, leather hoses, and one ancient rule: when a house caught fire, pull down the houses around it, so the flames had nothing left to eat.

The fire came in a year when London had barely survived another disaster. The Great Plague of 1665 had killed about 100,000 people, roughly a quarter of the city's population, and the royal court had only dared return to London on 1 February 1666.[9] That same year, England was at war with the Dutch — the Second Anglo-Dutch War, whose Four Days' Battle had just been fought in the North Sea in June. Plague, war, a dry summer, an east wind: a city can collect all its bad luck into a single September.

The plague left behind more than empty houses. It left a state of mind: disaster was God's judgment, something to be endured. So when the fire started, many people's first instinct was not to run or to fight it. It was to kneel and pray.

Chapter 2The Mayor Went Back to Bed

Not long after the fire started, someone woke the Lord Mayor. Sir Thomas Bloodworth arrived at Pudding Lane, took one look, and said it was nothing — the story goes that he said a woman might piss it out — then refused to authorise pulling down houses and went home to bed.

He was not a villain. He was making a decision that looked entirely reasonable on an ordinary day: pulling down houses meant paying compensation, angering owners, taking responsibility; and the fire still looked small. What power does best is refuse, while a thing is still small, to admit it will grow.

By morning, the naval administrator Samuel Pepys had climbed the Tower of London and seen 300 houses already burning along the river.[4] He rowed to Whitehall to tell Charles II: unless houses were pulled down ahead of it, nothing would stop the fire. The King ordered demolition, and soldiers set to work — but the fire was no longer a bakery fire. By Sunday evening, the east wind had turned it into a moving wall of flame, and it leapt every gap the demolition crews opened.

Pepys wrote that day in his diary one of the most famous lines of the whole fire. From his boat he watched "the churches, houses, and all on fire, and flaming at once, and a horrid noise the flames made."[4]

Chapter 3Four Days

On Monday, 3 September, the fire pushed west into the City. Tuesday, 4 September, was the worst day: it leapt the River Fleet, swept through Cheapside, the richest shopping street in London, and ringed St Paul's Cathedral, which stood wrapped in wooden scaffolding for repairs. The scaffolding caught at once, and the lead roof melted and ran down the streets.[1]

Pepys buried his parmesan cheese and a bottle of wine in his garden — the possessions he could least bear to lose. The diarist John Evelyn wrote that burned London looked "like Sodom, or the last day."[5] The Thames was crowded with boats fleeing the fire, loaded with mattresses, chests, children. In the fields outside the walls, tents went up for some 70,000 homeless people — of the City's 80,000 residents, nearly everyone.[1]

On Wednesday, 5 September, the wind finally dropped. The garrison of the Tower of London used gunpowder to blast a firebreak, and the fire stopped at the edge of the ruins. Four days, 436 acres of ashes: 13,200 houses, 87 parish churches, 44 company halls, the Royal Exchange, the Custom House, and Old St Paul's Cathedral, all gone.[1] The property destroyed was worth some £10 million.[1] The quieter loss was paper: parish registers of births, marriages and burials burned in large numbers, and a city's memory turned to ash along with its houses.

When it was over, Pepys climbed the steeple of Barking church and looked across the City. He wrote: "the saddest sight of desolation that I ever saw."[4]

Chapter 4Six People, and a Scapegoat

The official death toll of the Great Fire was six. Six people, for 13,200 houses. The number is so absurd that no historian since has believed it.

The historian Neil Hanson has argued that the true toll may have been in the hundreds, possibly the thousands.[1] The deaths of the poor were never recorded; the heat of the firestorm was enough to cremate its victims, leaving no recognisable remains; and the people who froze or starved in the refugee camps that winter were never entered in the fire's account. Six is not a statistic. It is evidence of how few people the system could see.

A city burned down needs someone to blame. When disaster strikes, people look for a culprit before they look for a cause. A French watchmaker named Robert Hubert stepped forward and confessed that he had started the fire — first in Westminster, he said, then, changing his story, at the bakery in Pudding Lane. His account was full of holes, and even the judge doubted his fitness to plead. He was hanged at Tyburn on 29 October 1666 anyway.[1]

When disaster strikes, people look for a culprit before they look for a cause.

After Hubert's death, a parliamentary committee still blamed a Catholic plot. In 1681, an inscription was added to the Monument denouncing "Popish frenzy"; the words stood carved in stone until they were removed in 1830.[1] An accident took 164 years to be admitted as an accident.

Chapter 5The London of Brick

On the very day the fire went out, people were already back on the smoking ruins, marking out where their houses had stood. Three days later, Charles II issued a proclamation: rebuilding could begin, but it would be orderly — every new house was to be built of brick or stone.[3]

In February 1667, Parliament passed the Rebuilding of London Act: no wood on the outside of any building; houses classified into four types by location, with storey heights, wall thicknesses and cellar depths all prescribed; streets widened; wharves along the Thames opened up, with no houses allowed to block access to the river; and the public works paid for by a duty on coal. A special Fire Court was set up to decide who should pay for rebuilding, landlord or tenant.[3]

Some wanted to use the disaster to remake London entirely. Christopher Wren and John Evelyn both drew Baroque grids of wide avenues, and the King was tempted. The plan died on property law: compensating thousands of owners would have cost more than anyone could pay. So London rose again on its old medieval street plan — the plan it still keeps today.[1]

The rebuilding was astonishingly fast: most private houses were complete before 1671. Wren designed 51 new churches and a new St Paul's Cathedral — consecrated in 1697, completed in 1710. Near Pudding Lane rose a 202-foot Doric column: the Monument, finished in 1676, its height exactly its distance from where the fire began.[1]

The fire also gave birth to an entirely new industry. In 1680, the economist Nicholas Barbon founded the Fire Office, the first fire-insurance company, which hired its own private firemen to protect its policyholders' houses. The private fire brigades competed, quarrelled and eventually merged into what became the London Fire Brigade.[3] The most unexpected legacy of a disaster is often not a monument. It is a business.

EpilogueWhat the Fire Did Not Burn

The Great Fire burned 13,200 houses and did not burn London. What it burned was a city's misunderstanding of itself: that wooden houses could go on being built forever, that disaster only happened to other people, that a mayor saying "it's nothing" made it nothing.

What the fire left behind outlasted what it destroyed. Rules for building in brick and stone, fire insurance, a fire brigade, a fire court — none of them was built as a memorial. All of them were built for the next time. They share one premise: disaster will come again, so pay the price in advance.

1666 held more than fire. In the same year, Newton left a plague-closed Cambridge for Woolsthorpe, where he worked out calculus, optics and universal gravitation — the year later called his annus mirabilis;[8] and in the same June, English and Dutch fleets fought for four days in the North Sea, one of the longest naval battles in history.[9] A city burned, a man in the countryside wondered why apples fall down, a fleet fired on another fleet — in the same year, in the same world, disaster and discovery always happen at once.

The gilded flame on top of the Monument still burns above London. It was not raised to celebrate anything. A city raises a monument to itself not for a victory, but for a lesson — and a lesson is worth carving in stone precisely because most cities, after a disaster, learn nothing at all.

Kokuhō: The Gangster's Son Who Became Japan's Living National Treasure

Shuichi Yoshida's career-staking 20th-anniversary novel: serialised in the Asahi Shimbun for a year and five months, published in two volumes in September 2018, winner of two literary prizes in 2019. The 2025 film broke Japan's live-action box-office record, and the novel flowed back onto the bestseller lists. This deep read asks: how can a novel about kabuki keep readers who know nothing about kabuki turning pages — and between bloodline and talent, who decides?

PrologueNew Year's Day, 1964: Snow in Nagasaki

On New Year's Day, 1964, in the first snow at a Nagasaki restaurant, the future national treasure of Japan was born. That is how Shuichi Yoshida's novel Kokuhō begins: the son of a gang boss, who loses his father at 15 and is taken in by a great kabuki house, setting out on a road half a century long.[2][4] The novel ran in the Asahi Shimbun for a year and five months, from January 1, 2017 to May 29, 2018, and was published in two volumes by Asahi Shimbun Publications on September 7, 2018.[4]

The Traditional Chinese edition was not slow to follow: New Classic Culture published the two-volume set on September 2, 2020, translated by Liu Zijun, with an exclusive afterword by the author, "Was Kikuo's Life a Happy One? Written at the End of the Serialisation."[1][2] In 2019 the book won two major prizes: the 69th Art Encouragement Prize from the Minister of Education, and the 14th Chūō Kōron Literary Prize.[2] On June 6, 2025, the film adaptation opened in Japan, directed by Lee Sang-il and starring Ryō Yoshizawa; its box office kept rewriting records, and the novel flowed back onto the bestseller lists, ranking fifth in buzz in the first half of 2026.[5][7]

This deep read asks three questions: how can a novel about kabuki keep readers who know nothing about kabuki turning pages? Between bloodline and talent, who decides? And after the film's success, what is left in the book that the film cannot give?

Chapter OneTwo Boys, One Stage

There are two heroes. One is Kikuo, the son of a gang boss, born with what the novel calls "a beauty not of this world." At 15 he loses his father in a gang war and is taken in by Hanai Hanjiro, head of a great Osaka kabuki house. The other is Shunsuke, Hanjiro's eldest son, a young prince of the kabuki world, raised in applause and expectation.[2][6] Two boys from opposite worlds walk the same road into acting: one to avenge his murdered father, one who ran away from home after quarrelling with his father. They are rivals, stage partners, and each other's only true friend in life.

Yoshida has said that writing Kokuhō for the 20th anniversary of his debut felt much like writing Villain a decade earlier: he wanted to test how much he had grown as a writer in ten years, and did not mind failing — he wanted a book on which to stake his whole career.[2] The word "stake" is not rhetoric. In 2015 he met the kabuki actor Nakamura Ganjirō, and after watching Kenji Mizoguchi's 1939 film The Story of the Last Chrysanthemums, he made up his mind to write about kabuki actors. At their first meeting, Ganjirō had a stagehand's black costume made for him and invited him to travel with the troupe. The sweat of actors working hard backstage, the endless applause from the audience along the runway, the singular fragrance of the kabuki stage — these are what made Kokuhō.[2][8]

The novel comes in two volumes — Youth and The Way of Flowers — twenty chapters in all, beginning in Nagasaki in 1964, moving through Osaka and Tokyo, through the high-growth years and into an entertainment world changing from stage to film to television.[2][5] From their teens, the two boys are pushed forward, one drumbeat at a time, through betrayal, separation, fame, and ruin, living a life on fire. Ordinary people wish life were like a play. These two turned the play into a life.

Chapter TwoWhat Is an Onnagata?

To read Kokuhō, you need one word first: onnagata. On the kabuki stage, female roles are played by male actors. That is the onnagata. Kikuo walks exactly this road: a boy learning to perform a woman with his body, his voice, his eyes. The premise is the novel's core: gender, birth, and bloodline can all be remade by performance. Only skill cannot be faked.

Kabuki began in the early 17th century and has run for more than 400 years. Its rules are many: hereditary succession, master-disciple lines, stage names, ceremony. For an outsider to enter, enthusiasm is not enough. What it takes is daily practice. The book writes the boys' childhoods plainly: while other children go to play baseball, they go to practice; when other children go home after school, they stay in the rehearsal room. The Asahi Shimbun review quotes a line from the book: the master's wife tells Shunsuke that he was born a performer's child and his blood will protect him; to Kikuo she says, in five years with us, you have never once missed a day of practice.[5] Bloodline and effort are weighed in a single sentence.

Yoshida spent four years on research — not imagining kabuki from a desk, but standing backstage in black, watching actors sweat, hearing audiences applaud, smelling the stage.[5] That is why the details carry weight: the sound of the wooden clappers, the length of the runway, the smell of the dressing room, the pressure of inheriting a stage name. Readers need no prior knowledge of kabuki, because the author walks them backstage himself. The critic Mina Saitō, reviewing for the Asahi Shimbun, called the book a "gorgeous, spectacular epic."[5]

Chapter ThreeDid It Go Viral Because of the Film, or Before It?

First, the timeline. The book came seven years before the film: the novel in 2018, the prizes in 2019, the paperback in September 2021 — 838 pages across two volumes.[4][6] The film opened on June 6, 2025, directed by Lee Sang-il, with Ryō Yoshizawa as Kikuo, Ryūsei Yokohama as Shunsuke, Ken Watanabe among a top cast, running 175 minutes.[5][6]

Then the box office climbed and kept climbing. In November 2025 it reached 17.37 billion yen, passing Bayside Shakedown 2 (2003, 17.35 billion yen) to become the highest-grossing live-action Japanese film in history, a record rewritten for the first time in 22 years. By February 2026 it had passed 20 billion yen with more than 14 million admissions — the first live-action Japanese film ever to pass 20 billion yen.[5][7] It was also Japan's submission for the Academy Award for Best International Feature Film.[2] The novel sold with it: the paperback took first place on both the Nippon Shuppan Hanbai and Tohan annual bestseller lists for 2025, and first and second places on Oricon's annual paperback chart — a "triple crown." Total sales of the original passed 1.2 million copies in September 2025.[5][6]

So the answer is: the book won its prizes first, the film came later and went further, and the book flowed back with it. In the first half of 2026, among the 34 titles on Eslite and Books.com.tw's top-30 lists, Kokuhō ranked fifth in online buzz, with 3,524 mentions.[7] The order matters. This is not a forgotten novel rescued by a film. It is a prize-winning novel pushed by a film in front of a much wider audience. The film is an amplifier, not a resurrection.

Chapter FourBloodline or Talent — Who Decides?

The novel's central question is a single one: how does a person with no bloodline become a national treasure? Kikuo has nothing: no kabuki surname, no father's protection, no inborn legitimacy. What he has is a face, a body, and practice he never once skips. Shunsuke has everything: bloodline, a stage name, a father's expectations. Two people on the same stage — the audience's eyes will tell you who is better. But the audience's eyes can also be blinded by bloodline.

The cruellest thing about the novel is that it refuses a simple answer. Kikuo becomes famous. Shunsuke becomes famous too. But their roads diverge. After losing Shunsuke — his closest comrade and rival — Kikuo pours himself entirely into his art, until gradually he cannot even see the audience that supports him. In his afterword, Yoshida writes that the end of such a solitary artist is often that those who dislike him outnumber his loyal fans, a failure for a popular actor. And yet the devotion of a man in his sixties, single-minded about his goal, overlaps with the reckless boy who, at ten-something, tried to stab a gang boss at a school assembly to avenge his father.[2]

That passage is the book's thesis. A "national treasure" is not a prize or a title. It is a way of living: doing one thing to its extreme, until only you and the stage remain. Was Kikuo's life a happy one? The author asks it in the title of his afterword and gives no answer. After twenty chapters, readers will have their own.

Chapter FiveWhat It Gets Right, What It Misses

Three things are right. First, the craft details are real. Four years of backstage research make the rehearsal rooms, the runway, and the inheritance of stage names read like things seen with one's own eyes. Readers who know nothing about kabuki can still follow Kikuo into that world. Second, the span of time is large enough. From 1964 to nearly half a century later, a whole life is gathered in. The editor at New Classic Culture, Chan Hsiu-ping, wrote that even at the second volume her hair stood on end, excitement and calm rising together, as if a grenade were pressed to her chest and she had to hold the pin with full concentration.[3] Third, film and novel complete each other instead of replacing each other. The film gave the novel an audience; the novel gave the film its depth.

Three things miss. First, the barrier is genuinely high. Two volumes, 838 pages in paperback, twenty chapters of epic — readers need time and patience.[6] Second, the density of kabuki terms and rules is high. Even though the author writes as accessibly as he can, midway through there are passages where readers must stop and think. Third, the female characters carry less weight. The two-hero structure is so strong that other figures are easily pulled out of the light — a common problem in two-hero epics, not unique to this book, but readers will feel it.

One more note: watching the film before reading the book, and reading the book before watching the film, are two different experiences. In 175 minutes the film can only take the novel's skeleton; in 838 pages the novel holds the flesh and blood. Those who saw the film will keep putting Yoshizawa's face on Kikuo; those who read first will keep comparing the screen with their imagination. Neither is better. But they are different, and readers can choose.

Questions Without Answers Yet

To be honest, three things still have no reliable answer, and this piece will not pretend otherwise:

  • Actual sales of the Traditional Chinese edition in Taiwan: the publisher has not released Taiwan sales figures, so no reliable number can be checked, and none is estimated here.
  • Box-office figures for the film in Taiwan: Taiwan box-office statistics were not verified while this piece was written, so no figures are quoted.
  • The latest cumulative sales of the original: the figure of 1.2 million copies dates from the collector's edition release in September 2025; later totals have not been verified.

EpilogueA National Treasure Is a Person, Not a Thing

Close the book, and what remains is a quiet thought. The words "national treasure" are usually for things — a painting, a piece of porcelain, a building. But this book says a national treasure can also be a person: someone who does one thing to its extreme, until only they and the stage remain. Kikuo had no bloodline, no background, no shortcut. He had only practice he never skipped and a face that would not bow.

A baby born in the first snow of a Nagasaki restaurant on New Year's Day, 1964, became a national treasure in the end. It reads like a legend. But the novel is not about legend. It is about process: every day of practice, every performance, every loss and return. A legend takes one sentence. A process takes half a century. Finish this book and you will understand why Yoshida staked his career on it: some stories can only be written at the length of a lifetime.

My Gangster Aunt: Why a Life Lived Wrong Taught Taiwan to Let Go

Huang Shan-Liao, <i>My Gangster Aunt: 99 Things She Taught Me</i>, Sun Color Publishing, May 29, 2026, 296 pp.; No. 3 on Kingstone's 2026 Autumn Literature Chart and No. 1 on its Modern Chinese Writing live chart. A woman who left home at 17, went to prison twice, and — by one account — died of liver cancer at 45, left behind 99 maxims. This deep read asks: why her, why now, and what does "Don't live right. Live free" actually cost?

PrologueThe Man Who Hands Over the Scissors

When Huang Shan-Liao hands someone a pair of scissors, he always turns the sharp end toward himself. Choosing a drink at a convenience store, he picks through the glass first, then opens the door, so as not to waste the shop's electricity. Lying back for a hair wash, he lifts his neck so the girl washing his hair won't strain her arms.[2] His aunt was the exact opposite. When she handed him the scissors, she shouted: "Catch! If you miss, I'll cut your dick off!" She opened the fridge and stood there enjoying the cold air for three minutes, telling him to take his time choosing. At the hair salon she asked for extra work on the back of her head, then tried to set the girl up with a boyfriend, just to stretch the wash a little longer.[2] Huang writes: "She never burned out, because she burned other people instead."[2] This deep read asks three questions: how did a woman whose life was full of scars produce 99 maxims about living free? How did a book published on May 29, 2026 climb to No. 3 on Kingstone's 2026 Autumn Literature Chart and No. 1 on its Modern Chinese Writing live chart within six months?[3][4] And is "Don't live right. Live free" a cure, or just another package?

The facts first. Huang Shan-Liao, My Gangster Aunt: 99 Things She Taught Me, Sun Color Publishing, May 29, 2026, 296 pages, NT$420, ISBN 978-626-358-961-2.[1][2] The book collects 20 stories about his aunt and 99 life maxims, in three parts: Part One, "The Aunt I Saw"; Part Two, "The Aunt in Her Own Words"; Part Three, "The Aunt in Her Best Friend's Eyes".[2] In the book, the author describes himself as an anxious "good boy"; his aunt, by contrast, was a "weirdo" who never thought about the future.

Chapter OneThe Good Boy and the Mad Aunt

To read this book, you first have to read the contrast it is built on. Huang Shan-Liao was born on May 3, 1992 in Kinmen, and grew up in Taipei. In 2017 he started the YouTube channel "A Shirt: The City Scenery You Paint," and in 2021 he became a full-time writer.[6] His writing has one clear thread: from heartbreak novels to coming-of-age pain literature, from healing essays to self-exploration, he writes about wounded people and how they keep living. His aunt is the latest stop on that thread, and the most extreme one.

The aunt is built out of small actions. She pressed a half-smoked cigarette into a man's skin. When she learned her nephew was being bullied at school, she showed up to take revenge for him. When he told her he had a date with a girl he liked, she said she was coming along.[2] None of these actions is "correct," but every one of them is precise: she refused to shrink herself so that others could be comfortable. She didn't save electricity, effort, or trouble for others — she lived as the loudest person in the room.

Her nephew is the opposite. Huang admits in the book that he burns out because he is afraid of burning other people: he worries that leaving the fridge open too long wastes the shop's power; that taking something without both hands is rude; that the girl washing his hair looks unhappy because his head is too heavy.[2] Taiwanese readers know this anxiety well. Raised on "don't cause trouble for others," they grow up with that sentence running as autopilot: before doing anything, think about what others will think. His aunt's arrival is a slap to that autopilot. She says: "Don't over-examine everything. Never try to guess other people's motives or judgments, because those things have nothing to do with you. What concerns you is only your money, the people who care about you, and whether you're happy."[2]

Chapter TwoAn Aunt's Life, from 17 to 45

Her life, as the book tells it, runs roughly like this: she left home at 17, had a baby at 20, her boyfriend died, she remarried, went to prison, and when she got out, her child no longer recognized her.[2] She had liver cancer and still drank every day. She was brave enough to take a knife for a friend. She ran with gangsters, drew her relatives' disgust, and, blocked by her relatives, missed her own mother's funeral.[2] According to a book-summary video, she died of liver cancer at 45, having made a list and said goodbye with a smile by the sea.[7] The age of 45 appears only in that secondhand account, and the exact figure remains unverified — see "Questions Without Answers Yet" at the end.

The two fullest lives in the book both sit in Part Two. Story 08 is about the aunt at 20: two months pregnant, and not even a "girlfriend." When her boyfriend heard she was pregnant, all he said was "Damn." She asked him if he would take responsibility; he asked back, "Huh? You're going to have it?" She decided to earn money and raise the child herself: she quit her hostess job and hid her pregnancy when interviewing at a drink shop. Later the boyfriend came back, took work at a pawnshop, and handed her most of what he earned every month. When their daughter turned one, he suddenly knelt down, apologized, proposed, and slipped a gold ring on her finger. The story ends: that year, the baby had just turned one and the aunt was 21, and "at that moment, she was happy."[2]

Story 06 is the most moving piece in the book, and the most honest one. The aunt told her nephew she would go to prison in seven days, and told him: "Don't tell Grandma." She said Grandma was already tired enough and shouldn't have to worry; but the nephew guessed it was because this was her second time going in.[2] For those seven days, Grandma cooked the aunt's favorite dishes every day — shrimp and egg, eaten with relish. Seven days later the aunt went in, and only then did the nephew talk to Grandma about it. Grandma said: "I knew all along. The notices were sent to our household address — how could I not know?"[2] On the prison visit, the aunt said she wanted to hug Grandma, but through the glass, they couldn't even touch hands. Grandma kept saying "Wait until you're out," but the aunt was taken away before she heard it. And Grandma, at that moment, was hiding her own cancer from the aunt.[2] Two women, each hiding something from the other, each carrying it alone. There is no maxim in this chapter — only shrimp and egg, a pane of glass, and a hug that never happened.

Chapter ThreeWhy It Took Off

The record first. Published on May 29, 2026, the book took No. 3 on Kingstone's 2026 Autumn Literature Chart within six months,[3] and climbed to No. 1 on Kingstone's live Modern Chinese Writing chart,[4] ahead of Taiwan Travelogue, which topped Eslite's annual chart in the same period. The publisher and bookstore copy calls Huang a writer who has "topped the Eslite annual bestseller chart five years running," with "total sales past 600,000 copies."[5] But honesty requires a label: this is publisher and bookstore promotional copy, with no independently verified sales total. What public reporting can support is four straight years at the top of Eslite's annual chart, starting in 2021;[6][8] which year and which book the "fifth year" refers to is not consistent across sources. In 2026, Eslite's annual No. 1 was Taiwan Travelogue, and Huang's 2025 book Let the Days Slowly Get Better placed No. 2 on the annual new-books chart.[8]

What really made it catch fire was timing. 2026 was the year of letting go: on Eslite's 2026 annual Top 10, The Let Them Theory, The Diamond Sutra in Plain Words and Life Has Only Two Things: None of Your Business, and None of My Business all said the same thing — stop holding on.[8] The aunt's freedom landed exactly on that bruise. Readers were not buying the aunt's life; they were buying permission. The line "I'd rather be an unrepentant madwoman than a presentable good person"[2] is less the aunt's motto than the collective voice of Taiwanese readers in 2026.

The form matters too. This is a book written for the social media age: 99 maxims, each one splittable into a post, a short video, a story card; 20 stories, each opening with a scene after an em dash, like the opening of a short video. The scissors in Story 01, the cigarette pressed into a man's skin in Story 02, the schoolyard revenge in Story 04 — every scene is punchy enough to retell.[2] That is not a criticism; it is the technical reason it spread.

There is one more reason, less comfortable but impossible to skip: controversy is itself traffic. In June 2026, the YouTuber Duomiduoluo, with over a million subscribers, posted a video calling Huang's work empty, his prose weak, his story structures repetitive, and rating him "0 out of 10." On November 19, Huang responded for the first time while appearing at the Taipei District Court for a separate case: "No — he wrote it well. I think he put it very well. He helped me share my book."[9] The people attacking him and the people buying his books may be the same people — the first group watches the video, then buys the book to check; the second group buys the book, then watches the video to find their tribe. The zero score didn't cost him sales; it introduced the book to more people.

Chapter FourThree Angles, One Life

The most interesting formal choice in the book is its three perspectives. The publisher's copy says these three angles "puncture the unspoken rule of seeing through things without saying them."[2] The design is genuinely clever: the same life told three ways forces the reader to assemble the picture.

Where the three angles work best is in making the aunt three-dimensional. In the nephew's eyes she is all laughter and exaggeration: throwing scissors, burning a man with a cigarette, storming into a school for revenge. In her own account she is heavy and concrete: two months pregnant and not even a girlfriend; married to a rich man and miserable; during the divorce, her husband saying his affairs were her fault; six years without seeing her mother, secretly married.[2] The same woman is a madwoman in someone else's story and a victim in her own. The best friend in Part Three fills in what the family never saw: friends warming each other, a best friend who loved her more than her husband did.[2] Only with all three does the aunt feel like a whole person rather than a maxim machine.

But the design has one problem it cannot dodge: where does Part Two, "The Aunt in Her Own Words," come from? The aunt is gone. Are these first-person passages from interviews, letters, a diary, or the author's later reconstruction? The book never says. The best friend in Part Three is the same: no name, no background, nothing to check. Readers are asked to trust three voices while knowing only one author. That doesn't mean the content is false — it means the book outsources "truth" to the reader's trust.

One more detail is worth noting: of the three angles, the mother's is missing. Grandma is the most moving supporting character in the book — cooking shrimp and egg, hiding her cancer, saying "Wait until you're out" through the glass — yet she never gets a voice of her own.[2] One of the most quoted lines in the book is "A mother will always wait for you, but she won't wait forever."[2] It is moving, but it is written from the daughter's and the nephew's point of view. What did the mother herself think? The book never asks. A book that sells itself on three perspectives somehow leaves out the fourth, most important one — perhaps because once the mother's perspective is written in, the aunt's freedom stops looking so good.

Chapter FiveWhat It Gets Right, What It Misses

Three things are right. First, it honestly faces the price of being "good." An entire generation in Taiwan was raised on "don't cause trouble for others"; as adults, they put other people's feelings first. The book's first chapter works like a mirror: readers recognize themselves in Huang, and see the life they never dared live in his aunt. Second, its maxims stand on real scenes, not thin air. Behind every maxim there is something that actually happened: the scissors, the cigarette burned into a man's skin, the schoolyard revenge, the shrimp and egg.[2] Third, it doesn't sugarcoat family. The way Grandma and the aunt "didn't talk about themselves" — chatting away yet never going deep[2] — is truer than any tearful mother-daughter reconciliation.

What it misses is also real, and not small. First, turning a life into maxims sands the scars smooth. Two prison terms, a child who no longer recognized her mother, liver cancer, dead at 45 — these costs get folded into one line, "Don't live right. Live free," and passed over.[2] Who paid the bill for that freedom? The book doesn't write it. That is the danger of the maxim: it compresses a complicated life into a shareable sentence, and the costs are the first thing thrown away in the compression.

Second, survivorship bias. The aunt lived freely, and her life became a book. But of the people who walked the same road — leaving home at 17, running with gangsters, going to prison, getting liver cancer — how many survived? The book doesn't say. Readers easily conclude from one sample that freedom is the right answer. But the book gives the reader one sample, and a selected one. Was the aunt's freedom choice, or luck? The book never asks.

Third, romanticization. "Taking a knife for a friend" and "missing her mother's funeral" are written as medals — but what did they actually weigh? After the knife? The book never follows up. As for the funeral, the book says relatives blocked her — but for a woman who died at 45, was missing her mother's funeral really only the relatives' fault? The book turns these into legend; behind legend is usually a mess that never got written down.

Fourth, the gap between slogan and reality. "Don't live right. Live free" — what does that mean for a reader with a mortgage, children, and parents? The aunt had no mortgage, no job inside the system, nobody depending on her; her freedom was partly possible because she had little to lose. A person with little to lose teaching a person with everything to lose to "stop living right" — the book never deals with that gap. It hands the reader a line that feels great and no way to take it home. After finishing the book, readers still go back to their mortgages and their good-boy lives.

Finally, back to Duomiduoluo's zero. His criticism — empty content, weak prose, repetitive story structures[9] — was harsh, but not entirely baseless. The 20 stories share one structure: maxim, scene, closing line; by the tenth story the reader already knows the pattern. But zero is also wrong: the shrimp and egg in Story 06, the proposal in Story 08, Grandma hiding her cancer — those are not things a zero-point writer can write. The honest score sits somewhere in the middle: this is not literature, but it is an honest bestseller.

Questions Without Answers Yet

To be honest, a few things still have no reliable answer, and this piece will not pretend otherwise:

  • The claim that the aunt died at 45: it appears only in a book-summary video,[7] and neither the publisher's copy nor the book's preview states her age. The exact figure has not been verified.
  • "Five straight years as Eslite's annual bestseller champion" and "total sales past 600,000 copies": publisher and bookstore copy,[5] not independently verified; public reporting supports four straight years, 2021 to 2024.[6][8]
  • The sources of the Part Two and Part Three accounts — interviews, letters, or the author's reconstruction — are not explained in the book and cannot be verified.
  • Single-title sales have not been disclosed; the Autumn Literature Chart No. 3 and the live No. 1 are snapshots from different dates, and rankings shift over time.[3][4]
  • Details of the memoir: the proposal scene in Story 08 and the prison-visit dialogue in Story 06 come from the author's own narration and cannot be independently verified.

EpilogueDon't Live Right. Live Free.

Back to the scissors. Huang never became his aunt — he still turns the sharp end toward himself. But he learned one thing: turning the sharp end toward yourself doesn't mean turning your whole life toward yourself. What his aunt taught him, more than 99 maxims, is two ways of reading one sentence: "Taking responsibility for how you feel right now is the most important responsibility."[2] The responsibility is not for how others judge you, but for how you feel right now. The sentence sounds simple; but for someone raised on "don't cause trouble for others," it takes a whole book to learn.

But the other side has to be said clearly too: the aunt died at 45. Freedom wasn't free; she paid for it, and she paid dearly. She never lived to see her story become a bestseller. The best way to read this book is to hold two facts at once: she lived free, and she died young. The first is what the book sells you; the second is the bill it doesn't show you.

Close the book and ask yourself one question: in your life, is there something "correct" that is really just something you don't dare not to do? If there is, the aunt would say: try it once — but first, work out whether you can afford the price. Freedom isn't having no price; it's knowing the price and choosing yourself anyway. The aunt managed that much. Whether the rest of us can is another book's question.

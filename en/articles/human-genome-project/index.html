The Human Genome Project: Reading a Book of 3 Billion Letters

Thirteen years, three billion dollars, six countries — one team set out to copy down the book that says what a human being is.

PrologueThe Book Inside Us

On 14 April 2003, a short announcement travelled around the world: the Human Genome Project was complete. Thirteen years, three billion dollars, scientists from six countries — they had finished reading the book written inside every human body: 3 billion letters, only four symbols, A, T, C and G, yet the complete instructions for what makes a person a person.[1][3]

The date was chosen with a sense of occasion. In that same month, exactly 50 years earlier, in April 1953, Watson and Crick had published the double-helix structure of DNA. Half a century took humanity from knowing the shape of the book to copying out the whole of it.[3]

But what moves about this book is not the numbers. It was an experiment in human nature: could human beings, in order to understand themselves, set aside borders, patents and pride, and do together what no single country could do alone? On that day in 2003, the answer was yes. The other half of the answer — what we do with it, now that we can read it — is still being written.

Chapter OneA Proposal Bold to the Point of Recklessness

In the mid-1980s, three unconnected voices spoke up, one after another. Robert Sinsheimer, chancellor of the University of California, Santa Cruz, convened a workshop in 1985 to discuss whether reading the entire human genome was feasible; Renato Dulbecco of the Salk Institute wrote in Science in 1986 that sequencing was the shortcut to understanding cancer; and Charles DeLisi of the U.S. Department of Energy pushed the Santa Fe workshop that same year, putting the idea on the table.[6]

The objections came flooding in at once. Biologists feared that a big-science project swallowing astronomical sums would drain the lifeblood of thousands of small laboratories; others asked bluntly: we do not even know how many genes there are — what makes you think that reading 3 billion letters will give us the answers?

The argument did not stop the project. In 1988, the National Institutes of Health and the Department of Energy signed a memorandum of understanding to pursue it together, and Watson, one of the discoverers of the double helix, became the first director of the newly created National Center for Human Genome Research. In April 1990 the two agencies published the first five-year plan; on 1 October 1990 the project officially began, with 15 years allowed and completion expected in 2005.[2]

On 4 April 1993, after Watson stepped down, Francis Collins — a physician and geneticist — took over as director and carried the project through its remaining ten years.[2]

It was never one country's project. Laboratories in the United States, the United Kingdom, Japan, France, Germany and China formed an international consortium, each taking responsibility for different chromosomes, sequencing in parallel and sharing the results. No single nation could have afforded — or finished — this book alone; cooperation was not a slogan, it was the only way.[6]

Chapter TwoA Public Race Against a Private One

In May 1998, the biologist Craig Venter founded Celera Genomics and announced that, using whole-genome shotgun sequencing, he would read the human genome before 2001 — and sell the data only to paying customers.[7]

What the public project feared most was not losing, but seeing genes fenced off and charged for. As early as February 1996, at a meeting in Bermuda, the public project's partners had set the rule: sequence data would be released every 24 hours, free for anyone to use. Openness became the public project's weapon against patent monopoly.[7]

The race made both sides run faster. The public project pulled its completion date far ahead of 2005; Celera pieced together a draft in just three years. On 26 June 2000, in the White House East Room, President Bill Clinton and Prime Minister Tony Blair joined by video link as Francis Collins and Craig Venter stood shoulder to shoulder, announcing together that both sides had completed a working draft of the human genome. Clinton called it “the most wondrous map ever produced by mankind.”[7]

In February 2001, the two drafts were published side by side in Nature and Science. The race was over, and both sides had won.[4][5]

Chapter ThreeFar Fewer Genes Than Expected

Before the work began, scientists had estimated that humans have 100,000 genes. Once the draft was published, the number kept sliding downward, finally settling at around 20,000 protein-coding genes — only slightly more than a roundworm one millimetre long.[3]

The proportions were even more surprising: only about 1.5% of the genome codes for protein. The other 98.5% was once casually dismissed as “junk DNA.” Only later did it become clear that those non-coding sequences hold the instructions that switch genes on and off — beyond the score, there is an entire conducting system.[3]

One more set of numbers deserves to be read quietly: the DNA of any two people is more than 99.9% identical, differing at only about 3 million base pairs. The differences between us are far smaller than we imagine — and that small difference writes skin colour, height, disease risk, and every face that has ever been unique.[3]

Human complexity was never a matter of how many parts we have. Twenty thousand genes, wired together by precise regulation and combination, are enough to write a species that thinks, dreams, and reads its own genome. That is the deepest lesson the project left biology: life is not a parts list, but a piece of music.

Chapter FourWho Has the Right to Read This Book?

Before the project had read a single letter, Watson had already promised to set aside 3% to 5% of the budget to study the ethical, legal and social implications of this knowledge. In 1990 the ELSI Research Program was formally established, and the U.S. Congress later required the National Human Genome Research Institute to devote at least 5% of its research budget to it. For the first time in the history of science, a great project invited ethicists to sit at the same table from the day work began.[1]

The list of fears was concrete: would insurers refuse coverage because of a risk written in someone's genes? Would employers use genes to pick workers? Once genetic information leaks, how much privacy does a person have left? And deeper still lay the fear of genetic determinism — if everything is written in the letters, what remains of free will?

The Bermuda Principles answered one of those fears: the data would be public, belonging to all humanity, not to any single company. The other questions have no standard answers. The value of the ELSI programme was never that it solved them, but that it forced scientists to ask them before they acted.

Chapter FiveFrom Reading to Writing

In 2001, reading one person's genome cost about 100 million dollars. By around 2015, the cost had fallen to about 1,000 dollars — a million-fold drop. Reading a genome went from a national engineering project to a routine test in the clinic: children with rare diseases no longer needed years of searching for a diagnosis, cancer treatment began choosing drugs by the tumour's genetic type, and prenatal screening entered ordinary households.[8]

Once we can read, someone will want to rewrite. After CRISPR gene-editing arrived, editing an embryo moved from imagination to technique. In November 2018, the Chinese researcher He Jiankui announced that the gene-edited twin girls “Lulu” and “Nana” had been born, their embryos' CCR5 gene edited in the hope of making them resistant to HIV. Condemnation poured in from scientists around the world; on 30 December 2019, a court in Shenzhen sentenced him to three years in prison and a fine of 3 million yuan for illegal medical practice, and he was released in April 2022.[9]

He Jiankui's case had no medical necessity, no genuine ethical review, and no real informed consent. It stands like a mirror, reflecting the hardest question after the project: when technology runs ahead of consensus, who steps on the brakes? The book finished in 2003 taught humanity to read; the events of 2018 forced humanity to learn restraint.

EpilogueThe Book Is Finished; the Questions Are Just Beginning

The “completion” announced in 2003 still left about 8% unfinished — repetitive sequences too hard to assemble. Only in 2022 did the Telomere-to-Telomere Consortium publish the first truly gapless human genome: 3.055 billion base pairs, not a single letter missing.[10]

But the larger gap is not in the letters. Reading a genome is not the same as understanding a person. A genome is a dictionary, not a verdict of fate; the same letters, in different lives and different choices, write different stories.

The true legacy of 2003 may not be the 3 billion letters at all, but a proof: that human beings can set aside their differences for a shared curiosity and do something great together — and can also step on the brakes for a shared fear. The book has been read. The chapters that come next will be written by every one of us.

I May Be Wrong: From CFO to Forest Monk, a Life in One Sentence

PrologueA Book Written Before Goodbye

In 2020, a slim book appeared in Sweden. Its author was a former forest monk whose body was slowly failing him. Less than two years later, he was gone, aged 60.[4] He left behind one sentence — five words in Swedish, four in English: I may be wrong.[7] This deep read asks three questions: why would a man walk away from everything to spend nearly 17 years as a monk in a Thai forest? How did a single sentence carry a book to the top of the charts in Sweden and Taiwan? And what can that sentence do for the rest of us, who are anxious and certain in equal measure?

The facts first. The Swedish original, Jag kan ha fel och andra visdomar från mitt liv som buddhistmunk, was published by Bonnier Fakta in 2020, written with Caroline Bankler and Navid Modiri.[1] The Traditional Chinese edition, translated by Guo Tengjian, followed on February 1, 2023, from Xianjue Publishing.[2] The English edition came out on February 17, 2022, from Bloomsbury, as I May Be Wrong: And Other Wisdoms from Life as a Forest Monk.[8] This is not a book about success, or about happiness. It is what a dying man made of a lifetime of learning.

Chapter OneAt 26, He Quit as CFO

Björn Natthiko Lindeblad was born on August 16, 1961, and took a master's degree in economics from the Stockholm School of Economics.[4] At 23 he graduated, joined the Swedish multinational AGA in Spain as deputy finance manager, and within two years became the youngest chief financial officer of one of its subsidiaries.[4][5] By every worldly measure, the road ahead was straight and steep. He did not like the job.[4] At 26, he resigned.[3]

He did not go to Thailand at once. He worked in a preschool, studied literature, taught as a substitute, and spent a year in India as an economist for the United Nations.[4] The years look like detours; they were a search. In January 1992 he entered Wat Pah Nanachat, the International Forest Monastery in northeastern Thailand, and was ordained with the name Natthiko — "one who grows in wisdom."[3][4] The monastery had been founded in 1975 by Ajahn Chah for non-Thai practitioners, with English as its working language.[12] He stayed for nearly 17 years, disrobing in October 2008.[4]

Monastic days had little variety: sitting, alms rounds, chores, teachings, repeated day after day. He later said the most precious thing those 17 years taught him was to stop believing every one of his thoughts.[7] It sounds simple; it is the starting point of the whole book. Thoughts come and go like weather. Believing every one of them is mistaking the weather for fate.

Chapter TwoA Magic Mantra

The sentence in the title comes from an all-night meditation session.[7] A novice brought in a cauldron of hot, sweet coffee to keep everyone awake. Then the abbot, Ajahn Jayasaro, took the stage and announced he would pass on "a magic mantra."[7] The room froze: the Thai Forest Tradition is famous for rejecting anything to do with magic or mysticism. Jayasaro — a disciple of Ajahn Chah who had succeeded Ajahn Pasanno as abbot[7][13] — said calmly, in near-flawless Thai: the next time you feel a conflict brewing, when a relationship is about to break, repeat this mantra to yourself three times, sincerely and with conviction, in whatever language you like, and your worries will evaporate like dew on the grass on a summer morning.[7] Then he leaned forward, paused, and said: I may be wrong. I may be wrong. I may be wrong.[7]

Why does it work? Because it does not ask you to admit you are wrong. It asks you to admit you might be. "Might" opens a crack between "I am right" and "I am wrong." In England, Lindeblad heard another abbot, Ajahn Sucitto, say: "Being right is never the point."[9] Together, the two sentences are the core of the book: when we fight over who is right, the things that actually matter — the relationship, the feelings, how we go on living together — get pushed aside.

He is disarmingly honest about himself. A monk from Oklahoma, he writes, hated him for four whole years without a break.[9] Looking back, he realized he needed someone to hate him, to see how pointless it is to want everyone to like you. It is the same lesson as the mantra: let go of the thought "I should be liked," and life gets easier.

Chapter ThreeAfter the Robe: Depression and ALS

In October 2008, Lindeblad took off the robe and went home to Sweden.[4] He assumed nearly 17 years of practice had made him immune to ordinary suffering. It had not. Back in the world, he fell into depression, and life was harder than it had been in the forest.[3] The robe was gone; the thoughts remained. Without the monastery's schedule and precepts, he had to learn all over again how to live with his own mind. This is the most persuasive part of the book: wisdom is not a certificate earned in retreat. It is whether it still works after you come home.

Slowly he found his feet: leading meditation retreats, teaching leadership in companies. In 2012, listeners voted him host of Sommar i P1, Swedish national radio's beloved summer show.[4] In September 2018, he was diagnosed with ALS.[4] There is no cure; the body gradually loses all control while the mind stays clear. He does not use the book to sell pity. He treats the illness as a final lesson: since everything passes, practice letting go. He even had another mantra engraved inside his wedding ring: "This too shall pass."[9]

One passage is worth remembering. He writes that he would not encourage anyone to practice positive thinking — not at all. He does not believe it has any great power; he finds it shallow, a surface thing.[9] On a shelf full of books telling you to "look on the bright side," that sounds harsh. It is also honest. What he proposes instead is reconciliation: making peace with what has already happened — not to become a saint, but to protect your own mental health.[9] He quotes the Norwegian TV series Skam: "Everyone you meet is fighting a battle you know nothing about. Be kind. Always."[9]

Chapter FourWhy It Took Off

The record first. The Swedish original went straight to number one on publication in 2020,[8] and that year won Adlibris's Best Non-Fiction of the Year and the Storytel Award for Best Non-Fiction; the audiobook won Sweden's "Voice of the Year" in 2021, and the e-book won the Nextory E-book Award in 2022.[3] The English edition reached the Sunday Times bestseller list and has been translated into 29 languages.[8] In Taiwan, the Traditional Chinese edition was named one of Kingstone's Ten Most Influential Books of 2023,[3] then took number one on Books.com.tw's 2024 paperback and e-book charts and topped the Readmoo and Kobo e-book bestseller charts;[11] on Kingstone's 2024 Top 100 bestseller list it ranked number 9.[6] The publisher says 180,000 copies have sold in Taiwan.[3] The Dalai Lama's endorsement runs on the cover: "This book will benefit everyone."[3]

But numbers tell only half the story. What made it catch fire was timing and honesty. The book came out in 2020, a year when many people were forced to stop for the first time and ask: what if tomorrow is different? A man dying of ALS, writing about how he let go of the need to be right, reads less like advice than testimony. And the book sells no success formula and no happiness recipe. Even its cover is not the author's face but a painting — Adoración by the Cuban artist Tomás Sánchez: a figure kneeling by a dark forest lake.[3] It does not look like self-help, and it does not read like it either.

The publisher's copy also claims worldwide sales past one million copies and rights sold in more than 30 countries.[10] Honesty requires a note: the rights figure differs across years of publisher copy — 29, 30, and 33 countries have all appeared[8][10] — and no independently verified worldwide sales total is public. Those gaps are listed at the end, under "Questions Without Answers Yet." Nothing is estimated here.

Chapter FiveWhat It Gets Right, What It Misses

Three things are right. First, the book is written from a road actually walked, not from ideas retold. Nearly 17 years of practice, depression after disrobing, more than three years with ALS — the price he paid is on every page, and readers can feel it. Second, it runs against the self-help grain. Where most books say "think positive," this one says: don't believe every thought you have, and don't put your faith in positive thinking.[7][9] Third, what it offers is not a theory but a sentence you can use at once. Before a quarrel, in a wave of anxiety, when the whole world seems against you — "I may be wrong" needs no preparation at all.

Some things miss. First, the book is thin and its ideas are few. It circles a handful of sentences; the first reading lands hard, the second yields visibly less. As a reference to return to, it runs out quickly. Second, "don't believe every thought" is never quite given its boundary: when should you doubt a thought, and when should you trust your own judgment? Readers who take only half the sentence may mistake avoidance for letting go. Third, the book's bedrock is the Theravada teaching of impermanence, and readers without that background may carry away a slogan without the practice behind it. What nearly 17 years in a forest produced cannot be compressed into a sentence and handed over; the work in between cannot be skipped — and the book offers no step-by-step practice that could replace it.

One more caution: many of its stories are the author's own memories — the night of the all-night meditation, the monk who hated him for four years — and those details cannot be independently verified. Read them as an honest man telling his own story, not as documented history.

Questions Without Answers Yet

To be honest, a few things still have no reliable answer, and this piece will not pretend otherwise:

  • Worldwide sales: the publisher's copy claims more than one million copies, but no independently verified total has been published, and none could be verified here, so none is estimated.
  • Countries where rights were sold: publisher copy from different years gives 29, 30, and 33 countries, and the exact figure has not been verified.
  • Taiwan sales of 180,000 copies: the figure comes from the publisher's copy and has not been independently verified; treat it as indicative only.
  • Details of the memoir's stories: the night of the all-night meditation, the monk from Oklahoma who hated him for four years, and similar episodes come from the author's own recollection and cannot be independently verified.
  • The Traditional Chinese audiobook: the publisher says it topped several platforms' bestseller charts after its January 2025 release, but the platform rankings were not independently verified here, so none are quoted.

EpilogueI May Be Wrong

On January 17, 2022, Lindeblad died surrounded by his loved ones, aged 60.[4] Before that, he had said publicly that he would end his life on his own terms; his parting words were: "I will leave without hesitation and without fear."[5][10] The book had been out for less than two years. What readers hold is something a man wrote with the last of his strength.

"I may be wrong" is not a sentence of weakness. It is a practice: every time you feel one hundred percent certain, stop, and leave a place for "might." Once that place exists, relationships have room, and life has a way back. Inside his wedding ring he had engraved "This too shall pass."[9] At the start of the book he called his superpower the refusal to believe every thought of his own.[7] Together, the two sentences are the whole of what he left us: thoughts pass, and so does attachment. What you thought you could never give up turns out, once released, not to bring life down at all.

Close the book, and what remains is not a method but a posture: a little more humility toward your own ideas, a little more generosity toward other people, a little more honesty toward the unknown. He said the sentence three times. Reading it once is enough — the rest is remembering to say it the next time you are absolutely sure you are right.

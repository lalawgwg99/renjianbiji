Jie Jin: Seven Small Knives Cutting Open the Gilded Cracks of Everyday Life

Huang Li-chun's first story collection in 14 years, published by Current Publishing on October 20, 2026: 192 pages, 7 short stories, from a wedding banquet tasting to the boredom of a ghost. It reached No. 3 on Kingstone's modern Chinese fiction chart and No. 30 on Eslite's October online bestsellers. This deep read asks: why does a novel that refuses to offer a theme prove so hard to put down?

PrologueA Knife That Cuts Open the Everyday

On October 20, 2026, Current Publishing released a 192-page collection of short stories: Jie Jin. Its author, Huang Li-chun, had not published a story collection since The Room by the Sea in 2012 — 14 years earlier.[1][3][9] The title is not a metaphor. It is a craft: kirikane, a traditional Japanese technique in which the artisan folds sheets of gold leaf thinner than a cicada's wing onto supple deerskin, cuts them with a bamboo knife into threads finer than a hair, then lifts the threads with the tip of a brush and lays them on Buddhist statues, washi paper, and everyday objects.[2][7]

The book holds only 7 stories in 192 pages, and the author warned readers in advance in the publisher's copy: "The author hasn't published fiction in a long time. She writes, but not much. This one just came out." "Indigestible. Languishing all day." "We wish you, in this book, no -ism and no theme to be found."[2] A thin book, by an author who refuses to offer a theme — and yet it climbed the bestseller charts in its first month. This deep read asks: what exactly did it cut into?

Chapter OneIt Went Viral Because Readers Waited 14 Years

The numbers first. In October 2026, Jie Jin ranked 30th among Chinese-language bestsellers on Eslite's online store; by November it had climbed to 3rd on Kingstone's modern Chinese fiction chart; and on Books.com.tw's 2026 literary fiction round-up it came in 4th.[5][8][10] OKAPI's November editors' picks at Books.com.tw chose it too, with the line "a knife made of language, cutting open the gilded cracks of life."[6]

The first reason it caught on is the wait. Her last story collection came out in 2012; 14 years is a long time to keep readers waiting, and DailyView's October round-up opened with exactly that — "a new work of fiction after more than a decade." The book had not even been officially released before the literary world was already anticipating it.[5][9]

The second reason is that the book itself knows how to talk. Current Publishing worked with printmaker Tsai Chun-sheng on a "Somehow Meow" limited edition, sold only in independent bookstores: a cloth hardcover, the title in matte gold, spray-painted page edges.[2][5] When pre-orders opened, readers commented "I'd pay 600 for anything Li-chun puts out," and some worried the edition would sell out before they returned to Taiwan to vote at the end of the month.[5] A book priced at NT$520 became a collector's item because of its limited edition.

But honesty first: the chart positions are real; the sales are not. The publisher has not released actual sales figures, and neither Eslite nor Kingstone publishes numbers — only ranks. How many copies this book has actually sold has no reliable answer yet.

Chapter TwoWhat Kirikane Is

The title deserves a careful look, because the whole way this book is written hides inside those two characters. Kirikane is a traditional Japanese art of decorating with gold leaf. It arrived in Japan from mainland Asia during the Asuka period (552–645) and reached its peak between the 8th and early 14th centuries.[7] The work is painstaking: gold leaf is so thin that a breath can tear it, so the artisan layers several sheets, lifts them with bamboo tweezers, warms them briefly over hot ash until they bond, moves them to a deerskin cutting board, and cuts them with a bamboo knife into threads finer than a human hair — then, holding a brush in each hand, pastes the threads one by one onto the folds of a Buddha's robe or the surface of an object, using a special glue of seaweed-based funori and animal-skin nikawa.[7]

The craft nearly died. In the Edo period (1603–1867), faster and easier gold paint replaced it, and at one point the technique survived only at Kyoto's Higashi-Honganji and Nishi-Honganji temples; it revived only after the Second World War, when it spread from religious objects to tea utensils and artworks. In 1981 it was designated an Important Intangible Cultural Heritage of Japan, and to this day only 3 people have been recognised as its holders.[7]

Huang Li-chun writes the way a kirikane artisan works. Everyday life is one large sheet of gold leaf — splendid, whole, apparently without a flaw. She takes a bamboo knife, cuts the thinnest thread, and traces it along a crack. The crack was always there. No one had traced its edge before.

Chapter ThreeSeven Stories, Seven Gentle Cuts

The collection holds 7 stories: "A Person Sitting," "The Tasting," "The Best Day," "An Egg," "Taken for a Sheep," "A Ghost's Ghost Story," and "Jie Jin."[3] The publisher's copy sketches each scene in one line: a banquet tasting full of competing intentions, the class gap inside and outside a luxury fitting room, the desires that surge when a care association comes knocking, the surface and the underside of a home cleaning visit — and the boredom of being a ghost.[2]

Only "The Tasting" has a published excerpt, so only it can be read closely. A husband and wife go to taste dishes for their eldest son's wedding banquet and order a whole table, almost all of it the chicken dishes their son loves. The husband thinks: why is there nothing he likes — no sweet-and-sour pork ribs, no eight-treasure duck — nothing his wife likes either, no pineapple shrimp balls, and why has nobody considered what the bride likes? His wife is about to become a wicked mother-in-law. But he does not say it. He just bows his head and picks the bones out of the fish for her, one by one.[4] The steamed grouper arrives, as always, when the feast is already falling apart; the waitress urges them to book today for 20 percent off; the wife smiles and asks only for a copy of the wedding package to take home and consider.[4] Nobody quarrels in the whole story. Nobody loses their temper. And yet the tension never lets up: the husband has to think twice before even making a joke, in case his wife bursts like a bubble.[4]

This — words left unsaid — is Huang's signature move. The quotable lines in the book work the same way: "Wealth is spatial; nobility is temporal," from "A Person Sitting"; and in "A Ghost's Ghost Story," a ghost so bored in the human world that it watches a neighbour rename their wi-fi network — "You Can't See Me La La La" — and remarks, "Even a great secret, however evil, cannot be denied its splendour. Only the trivial is unbearable."[2] One sentence, two worlds: great secrets have their own splendour. What grinds people down is the trivial stuff.

To be honest about the limits: apart from "The Tasting" and the two quoted lines, the other 4 stories have no published excerpts to check against, and the publisher's scene sketches are not matched to titles one by one. This deep read will not invent stories for the pieces it has not read.

Chapter FourWho Is Huang Li-chun?

Huang Li-chun was born in Taipei and graduated in philosophy from National Chengchi University.[3] She has won the China Times Literary Award, the United Daily News Literary Award, the Lin Rong-San Literary Award, the Chiu Ko Annual Fiction Award, the Golden Tripod Award, and the Openbook Annual Chinese Writing Award, and her essays were selected for the Chiu Ko Annual Essay Selection for 7 consecutive years.[3] Her essay collections include Songs from Behind, Something Slightly Extravagant, and I Don't Go Out with the Cat; she edited the Chiu Ko 2020 Essay Selection and wrote the interview-based biography Silent Realm: Seeing Kuo Ying-sheng.[3]

Her first story collection, The Room by the Sea, came out in 2012 with 13 stories about cruelty and darkness inside families and intimate relationships; Kuo Chiang-sheng, Ko Yu-fen, and Chi Ta-wei all wrote appreciations of it.[9] Interviewed in 2012, at 33, she said she wrote slowly — about one story a year — and that she was not particularly diligent about writing, just writing casually.[9] She grew up reading her late father's large collection — Kao Yang, Lin Yutang, Wu Lu-chin, Liang Shih-chiu — which gave her a classical literary foundation.[9]

Over the 14 years since, she never stopped writing; she simply did not publish fiction. She wrote essays, edited anthologies, conducted interviews, and ran a channel called "Or Would You Rather Be Scolded," full of verbal fencing.[2] Jie Jin is not a comeback. It is the moment she finally gathered 14 years of stories into one book.

Chapter FiveWhat It Gets Right, What It Misses

Three things are right. First, the language is precise. Huang's sentences are cold, short, and spare; one sentence carries two meanings, and a reader who glides past it only feels the cut when they stop. In "The Tasting," the husband picking the fish bones for his wife says more than any sentence about whether their marriage is good or bad. Second, the scenes are small. A wedding tasting, a fitting room, a home cleaning — places everyone has been. Because readers have been there, they fill in what is left unwritten, and the story grows longer than what is on the page. Third, it refuses to preach. The author tells you first that there is no -ism and no theme to be found, so you stop hunting for one — and then you find it anyway: the tug-of-war between people over things they cannot say out loud.

Three things should also be said plainly. First, it is too thin. 192 pages, 7 stories — just as the reader starts to feel the cut, the book ends, and anyone who likes the slow immersion of a long novel will find it leaves them wanting more. Second, the publisher's copy outshines the fiction. "Indigestible. Languishing all day" is so striking that readers remember the copy before they read the stories — and after finishing, what stays in the mind may still be the copy, not the stories. Third, the limited edition cuts both ways: selling it only in independent bookstores genuinely brought those shops foot traffic, but it also left readers who could not get one feeling that the NT$520 regular edition was second best.[5] Whether that is a marketing success or a literary awkwardness is a matter of opinion. Fourth, and most practically: apart from "The Tasting," the other stories have no published excerpts, so critics can only guess at the whole from the publisher's copy and two quoted lines — this deep read included.

Questions Without Answers Yet

To be honest, four things still have no reliable answer, and this piece will not pretend otherwise:

  • Actual sales of the Traditional Chinese edition: the publisher has not released figures, and Eslite's and Kingstone's charts publish ranks, not numbers, so there is no way to check how many copies this book has sold.
  • The plots of "The Best Day," "An Egg," "Taken for a Sheep," and "Jie Jin": there are no published excerpts, and the publisher's scene sketches are not matched to titles, so they cannot be read closely.
  • The price and print run of the "Somehow Meow" limited edition: the publisher has not disclosed them, and the NT$600 figure in readers' comments is a remark, not a price.
  • Reader ratings and review distributions on Books.com.tw: these were not individually verified while this piece was written, so no scores or comments are quoted here.

EpilogueThe Gold Thread Stays in the Crack

Kirikane was designated an Important Intangible Cultural Heritage of Japan in 1981, and to this day only 3 people have been recognised as its holders.[7] It did not die out because someone was always willing to spend a lifetime on a slow task: layering gold leaf, cutting gold threads, pasting them on one by one. Huang Li-chun writes fiction the same way: 14 years, 7 stories, looking slowly, cutting gently. She wrote in the publisher's copy, "Indigestible. Languishing all day," and readers laughed — but after the laughter, the sentence stays. Some things cannot be digested, not because they are too hard, but because they are too true. The gold thread is pasted on; the crack is still there, only now it has an edge of light. The next time you meet a similar crack in daily life, you will recognise it. That is enough.

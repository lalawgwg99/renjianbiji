Just Keep Buying: Even God Couldn't Beat Dollar-Cost Averaging

Nick Maggiulli's book came out in Britain in 2022 and reached Taiwan in 2023. Its message is a single sentence: stop guessing the timing, and just keep buying. This deep read asks whether lump-sum investing really wins two-thirds of the time, why even God couldn't beat dollar-cost averaging, and whether an answer computed from a century of American market data still holds in Taiwan.

PrologueA Man Who Gambled Away His Retirement

On April 12, 2022, Harriman House in Britain published a 272-page book with a three-word title: Just Keep Buying.[3][7] A little over a year later, on May 30, 2023, it reached Taiwan as 《持續買進：資料科學家的投資終極解答，存錢及致富的實證方法》 (Just Keep Buying: A Data Scientist's Definitive Answers on Investing, Saving, and Building Wealth), published by Business Weekly and translated by Lee Fang-ling.[1][2] The author, Nick Maggiulli, is not a Wall Street trader or a television money guru. He is the chief operating officer and data scientist at Ritholtz Wealth Management, an economics graduate of Stanford University who made his name writing the blog Of Dollars and Data.[2]

The book opens with the story of his grandfather. He retired at 55, with a pension and benefits that should have carried him comfortably through the rest of his life. But over the next 26 years he gambled the money away, and died with almost nothing left. Maggiulli did the maths: if his grandfather had put half of his retirement money into the stock market on a regular schedule, he would have ended up a millionaire even after living through bear markets.[11] The whole book grows out of that calculation: a family's regret, turned into a data scientist's question — what does it actually take for money to stay?

The book's method is unusual. It tells no rags-to-riches stories and teaches no stock-picking. Its 21 chapters each answer one question: how much should you save? Should you borrow? Rent or buy? When can you retire? What should you invest in? When should you sell?[2] The first half is about saving, the second about investing. Maggiulli has only one tool: historical data. He lays out nearly a century of market numbers and takes the old myths apart one by one. This deep read asks why a personal-finance book written with spreadsheets became a bestseller — what it gets right, and what it misses.

Chapter OneIt Sells Because It Tells No Stories

First, how big is it? The English original became an Amazon bestseller in investing on publication and was named BookAuthority's best finance book of 2022, with recommendations from James Clear, author of Atomic Habits, and Morgan Housel, author of The Psychology of Money.[2] In Taiwan, it reached the top 30 of the bestseller charts in the first half of 2026, ranking 7th by visibility, and has stayed near the top of the Books.com.tw investing list. The sequel, The Wealth Ladder, came out in Chinese in September 2026, using the same trademark: data.[2]

The first reason it sells is timing. 2022 was a turbulent year: on February 24, Russia launched a full-scale invasion of Ukraine; on November 11, the cryptocurrency exchange FTX filed for bankruptcy, with roughly an US$8 billion hole in its accounts. War and a crypto collapse in the same year — the question on every investor's mind was exactly the one the book's title answers: in times like these, should you still buy?

The second reason is the writing. The most popular money books of recent years win on stories: The Psychology of Money on human nature, Atomic Habits on behaviour. Maggiulli does the opposite: he tells no stories, he does the maths. There is no "I know a friend who got rich" anecdote in the book, only chart after chart: over the past 100 years, how often did investing a lump sum beat investing it gradually? Does buying the dip actually work? What happened to the people who bought at the very top? That is what makes it stand out among money books — it does not flatter your feelings, it gives you numbers.

The third reason is that the answer is simple enough. Twenty-one chapters and 272 pages collapse into one sentence: just keep buying.[7] The answer is so simple it is almost boring — and for someone chased all day by market news, boring is precisely what makes it valuable.

Chapter TwoThe Savings Rate Is the Main Character

The first half of the book is about saving, and its conclusion is a surprise: for most people, saving matters more than investing. Maggiulli's reasoning is practical: when your capital is small, no rate of return can conjure a large sum out of it. The book gives readers two numbers to locate themselves: expected savings — how much you can save over the coming year — and expected investment growth — how much your existing assets can earn in a year. Whichever number is bigger is where your effort belongs.[11] For the young and the asset-poor, the answer is almost always saving; only as assets grow does investing gradually take over. That is the meaning of Chapter 9's "crossover point": when investment growth overtakes savings, your life genuinely shifts from earning money by working to earning money from assets.

Chapter 3 also takes apart one of the biggest lies in personal finance: that frugality makes you rich. Saving on small things has a ceiling, Maggiulli says; earning has none. The book lists five ways to earn more: selling your time and expertise, selling a skill or a service, teaching others, selling a product, and climbing the career ladder.[2] Chapter 4's "2x rule" is just as memorable: if you want to buy something you don't need, save the same amount as well — a watch that costs NT$30,000 really costs NT$60,000. Chapter 5 is about lifestyle inflation: after a pay rise, save half of the extra and spend the other half, so life gets better without the savings rate falling.[2]

What these chapters share is that none of them talks about the market. Maggiulli's point is plain: until your assets have grown, market movements have little to do with you. Your savings rate is the main character.

Chapter ThreeLump Sum or Gradual Entry?

The second half is about investing, and Chapter 13 takes on the question everyone asks when a sum of money arrives: invest it all at once, or feed it in gradually? Maggiulli's answer is: all at once. In a 2012 Vanguard study, investing a lump sum into a portfolio of 60% stocks and 40% bonds beat investing it over 12 months in about 67% of rolling 10-year periods in the United States, about 67% in the United Kingdom, and about 66% in Australia, with the lump sum ahead by about 2.3% on average in the U.S.[5] Vanguard ran the numbers again in 2023 using global stock data (the MSCI World Index, 1976–2022) and reached almost the same conclusion: the lump sum beat a three-month gradual schedule 68% of the time.[4]

Why does the lump sum win? The reason is not mysterious at all: markets rise more often than they fall, so money waiting on the sidelines mostly misses rising days. Every month of gradual investing is a decision to keep money out of the market. But Maggiulli is equally clear that the lump sum wins "most of the time", not "every time". In the remaining third of periods, gradual investing wins — and it tends to win right after crashes, exactly when you are most frightened and most in need of comfort. So gradual investing is not the mathematically optimal answer; it is a psychological seatbelt. If investing everything at once would keep you awake at night, or make you sell in a downturn, then investing gradually is the right choice for you. The book's advice was never "don't be afraid" — it is "choose the approach you won't abandon when you are afraid".

Chapter 12 takes an equally hard line: don't buy individual stocks. Maggiulli's argument is not a moral lecture but a matter of probability: most individual stocks underperform the broad market over the long run, and the odds of picking a winner are lower than you think. The book tells ordinary readers to buy a basket of the whole market through index funds — what it calls "income-producing assets", assets that steadily generate interest and returns.[2][7]

Chapter FourEven God Couldn't Beat Dollar-Cost Averaging

Chapter 14 is the most famous chapter in the book, and its heading says it plainly: why you shouldn't wait for the dip. In a blog post dated February 5, 2019, later folded into the book, Maggiulli ran a thought experiment: suppose you are God — omniscient, able to buy at every market bottom. Your opponent is an ordinary person who simply invests US$100 (inflation-adjusted) every month. Both invest for 40 years, with starting points rolling from 1920 to 1979. The result: even with perfect foresight, buying the dip still lost to dollar-cost averaging more than 70% of the time.[6] Worse still, missing the bottom by just two months cut the dip-buyer's chance of beating the regular saver from 30% to 3%.[6] The post's title says it all: even God couldn't beat dollar-cost averaging.

The book also borrows Bob, the world's unluckiest investor, from Ben Carlson. Bob started work in 1970 and saved diligently, but only dared to invest after big market rallies — so all four of his investments landed just before a crash: US$6,000 at the end of 1972, followed by a 48% fall; US$46,000 in August 1987, followed by a 34% fall; US$68,000 at the end of 1999, followed by a 49% fall; and US$64,000 in October 2007, followed by a 52% fall. Four investments totalling US$184,000, every one of them at a market peak. But Bob had one virtue: he never sold. When he retired at the end of 2013, his account held US$1.1 million.[7][8]

Bob's story and God's experiment make the same point: when you buy matters far less than you think; what matters is not selling after you buy. People who wait for a dip do not lose on the dip itself — they lose the rising days they miss while waiting.

Chapter FiveWhat It Gets Right, and What It Misses

Three things deserve credit. First, it is honest. Chapter 15 is devoted to luck: how the year you were born shapes your investment returns, and how the order of returns changes the outcome. Maggiulli does not pretend that effort conquers everything; he admits outright that the same strategy, born in a different era, ends very differently. Second, its method is open: the data sources and assumptions are printed in the book, so readers can check the maths themselves instead of trusting the author's character. Third, it sells no anxiety: it does not tell you to watch the market every day or chase hot stocks; it tells you to work, to save, and to live your life.

But the book has three clear weaknesses. First, its backtests are almost entirely American. The U.S. stock market's past 100 years are the greatest bull run in history; the two-thirds win rate computed from that history need not be the same number in Japan, Europe, or emerging markets. Chapter 17 concedes the point: some markets never recovered quickly after a crash.[2] Second, its chapters on tax and retirement accounts apply only to the United States. Chapter 19's choice between a 401(k) and a Roth 401(k) is useful in spirit for Taiwanese readers, but none of its numbers can be carried across. Third, it assumes a steady income to keep investing from. For people whose income is irregular and whose cash flow swings widely, "just keep buying" is easy to say and another thing to do.

In Taiwan, the idea is not new. Huang Pei-yuan's The Bible of Personal Finance said it back in 1996: "Buy any time, buy anything, don't sell."[9] Chueh Yu-shang's The Forrest Gump Method of Investing says "don't watch the market, don't pick stocks, don't time your buys"; Chou Kuan-nan's Buy for the Long Term makes the same case from behavioural finance: hold a market index for the long run.[10] Maggiulli's contribution is not inventing the idea, but calculating it for you — using 100 years of data to turn an intuition into an answer you can verify.

Questions Without Answers Yet

To be honest, the following three things still have no reliable answers, and this article will not pretend otherwise:

  • The book's total worldwide sales: the publisher's copy calls it an Amazon investing bestseller and a best finance book of 2022, but publishes no total sales figure; this article found no reliable total and will not estimate one.
  • The details of the grandfather's story: the family anecdote that opens the book cannot be independently checked, so this article does not vouch for its specific amounts.
  • Whether the two-thirds win rate for lump-sum investing will hold: it is a historical frequency, not a guarantee, and whether market conditions since 2022 rewrite the conclusion is still an open question.

EpilogueBuy — and Then What?

Chapter 18 answers the last question: when should you sell? Maggiulli's answer is short: when you need the money, and when you need to rebalance. Sell slowly, sell little, and let compounding have time to work.[2] Chapter 20 asks why you never feel rich. The answer is comparison — your reference point is always someone richer than you. Chapter 21 closes with the most important asset of all: not stocks, not property, but yourself — your time, your health, and your ability to earn.[2]

So the book's real title is not Just Keep Buying but Just Keep: keep working, keep saving, keep buying, keep holding. It promises nothing about making you rich. It promises only one thing: if you follow it, the numbers will be on your side — most of the time.

One thing should be clear: this deep read explains the ideas in the book; it is not investment advice. Its numbers come from the history of the American market, and history never repeats itself — it only rhymes.

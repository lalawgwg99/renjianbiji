The Great Stink: How London Learned to Wash Itself

A river stank so badly that Parliament fled — and London finally paid for the invisible

PrologueThe Nose Knows First

In June 1858, London was unnaturally hot. For the first two weeks of the month, the heat drove the Thames down until its banks lay bare — mudflats piled with two centuries of everything the city had thrown into the river. Then the wind changed, and the whole city smelled it.[3]

The Houses of Parliament stand on the riverbank. In the newly built Palace of Westminster, the stone was still fresh, but the curtains had to be soaked in chloride of lime before anyone could bear to sit inside. MPs fled committee rooms with handkerchiefs pressed to their faces, sessions ended early, and there was serious talk of moving Parliament to Oxford or St Albans.[13] The Times wrote that what finally moved the government was "the force of sheer stench".[3]

This book is not about a river. It is about an old human habit: threats we cannot see, we pretend do not exist — until they stink badly enough to reach our noses. And by the time we act, it is usually decades late, and tens of thousands of people are dead.

In the summer of 1858, three great things were happening in London at once: sewers, evolution, and the reorganisation of an empire. But what makes that year worth remembering is the moment a city finally admitted it had been drinking its own waste — and had been doing so for a long time.

Chapter 1One River, Three Jobs

In the middle of the 19th century, London was the largest city on earth, home to more than 2.5 million people.[5] Those 2.5 million shared a single river for three jobs: drinking water, sewage, and rubbish. The Thames was the tap and the toilet at once.

The city held nearly 200,000 cesspits and 360 sewers.[10] When a cesspit filled, night-soil men came after dark to empty it and cart the contents out of town as fertiliser. It was filthy, but it more or less worked — until a well-meant "reform" broke it in the 1840s.

The Building Act of 1844 required new houses to connect to a sewer instead of digging a cesspit; in 1848, the newly created Metropolitan Commission of Sewers went further, ordering the city's cesspits connected to the sewers.[10] The medicine of the day believed that smell itself was disease — if you could not smell it, it was clean.[4] Get the filth out of the house, and you had got disease out of the house.

So London, with great efficiency, sent every household's waste into the same river — the river from which most Londoners drank. The reform did not solve the problem; it moved it from somewhere visible to somewhere invisible: from the nose into the drinking glass.

That is what institutions do best: solve the trouble in front of them, create a bigger trouble behind them, and send the bill to the next generation.

Chapter 2Thirty Thousand Dead, and Still Not Enough

Cholera first reached London in 1832 and killed 6,536 people; in 1848–49 it killed 14,137; in 1853–54, another 10,738.[11] In just over thirty years, cholera killed more than 30,000 Londoners.

Thirty thousand people could not get Parliament to pass a single sewerage act. Why? Because the wrong people were dying. Cholera chose the poor, the low streets by the river, the people with no voice. MPs lived on high ground and drank cleaner water — or believed they did.

The figures for 1854 said it even more plainly: south of the Thames, the cholera death rate was about three times the rate north of the river.[6] One city, one river, and a stretch of water between them meant a threefold difference between life and death. Disease was never fair; it always finds the least defended first.

The deeper reason was theory. The medical orthodoxy of the day was miasma: disease came from bad air. Cholera arrived, and the experts blamed the air; people died, and they blamed weak constitutions, or weak morals. As long as the cause was "the air", nobody had to take responsibility for the water, and nobody had to spend a fortune digging sewers. The greatest advantage of a wrong theory is that it lets everyone off the hook.

As early as 1842, the civil servant Edwin Chadwick had published his Report on the Sanitary Condition of the Labouring Population, showing that the diseases of the poor were inseparable from filthy surroundings.[4] He was right, and he was wrong: the environment did kill — only the killer was not the air, it was the water. A half-right diagnosis is harder to dislodge than a wholly wrong one. It left everyone feeling that something was already being done, so they connected the cesspits to the sewers and flushed the sewage into the Thames, making things worse while believing they were making progress.

So London learned a skill: keeping up appearances over corpses. The newspapers came out as usual, Parliament sat as usual, the river stank as usual — so long as the stink had not yet drifted into a powerful person's window.

Chapter 3A Map and a Pump

In September 1854, cholera came again, this time to Broad Street in Soho. In the first three days of September, 197 people died around Golden Square.[5] People collapsed in the street, and nobody knew why.

A physician named John Snow did what nobody else was doing: he stopped smelling the air and started counting people. He knocked on door after door, asking what water the dead had drunk, and plotted every life as a short black bar on a map. 578 black bars, packed tight around the Broad Street pump, half of them within 117 metres of it.[14]

He chased the exceptions, too. Of 83 deaths he examined, 61 had drunk Broad Street pump water;[5] the brewery workers next door almost all escaped, because they drank beer, not water; a widow who had moved to Hampstead died anyway, because she missed the taste of Broad Street water and had it delivered daily.[5] And then the comparison that settled it: households supplied by the Southwark and Vauxhall company, drawing Thames water, died of cholera at 315 per 10,000 houses; households supplied by the Lambeth company, drawing upstream, at 37.[5]

On the evening of 7 September 1854, Snow took his map to the Board of Guardians of St James's parish. The next morning, the handle came off the Broad Street pump.[15] The outbreak faded afterwards — though in honesty it was already fading, because the residents had fled. But the map survived: printed in Snow's On the Mode of Communication of Cholera in 1855, it became the founding document of epidemiology. Snow's own tables recorded the shape of the disaster: 143 fatal attacks began on 1 September alone, in a six-week outbreak that killed 616 people around a single pump.[15]

There is a coda to the story. The parish clergyman Henry Whitehead set out to prove Snow wrong, and went house to house until he found the source instead: a five-month-old infant, Frances Lewis, at 40 Broad Street, was among the earliest cases, and the water from washing her nappies had seeped into the cesspit beside the pump, and from there into the well.[15] One filthy chain linked a baby's nappy to more than 600 lives. A city's disaster rarely begins with a grand conspiracy; it begins with a small thing nobody noticed.

The medical establishment was not convinced. Nobody could see the cholera bacillus — Robert Koch would not isolate it in Egypt until 1883.[5] Until then, Snow's theory was a deduction without a murderer. On 10 June 1858, Snow suffered a stroke at work; he died on 16 June, aged 45.[7] The month he died, the Thames stank at its worst. The man who had the answer did not live to see the question admitted.

Chapter 4Eighteen Days

What moved Parliament was not 30,000 lives, nor a map. It was the smell. In the summer of 1858, the smell finally reached the right windows.

On 15 July 1858, Benjamin Disraeli, Chancellor of the Exchequer, put a bill before Parliament to build London a wholly new sewer system; on 2 August, it became law.[3] Eighteen days. The same scheme had been rejected, amended and shelved in committee for more than a decade; once the stink entered the chamber, it passed in eighteen days.

Parliament first voted £2.5 million,[1] and handed it to the Metropolitan Board of Works and its chief engineer, Joseph Bazalgette. Bazalgette had waited years for this moment; his plan was already drawn. Stop letting sewage flow straight into the river: dig great intercepting sewers along both banks, catch the waste halfway, and carry it far downstream to be released on the outgoing tide.[1]

The obstacle to reform was never technology, and never money. The obstacle was that the threat had not yet reached the decision-makers' noses.

Those eighteen days belong in every book about institutions. Not because they were fast, but because of what they exposed: the obstacle to reform was never technology, and never money. The technology existed; the money could be found. The obstacle was that the threat had not yet stunk its way to the noses of the people who could sign the cheques. What a society is willing to pay for depends on who is doing the smelling.

Chapter 5The London Under the Bricks

Work began in 1859. Bazalgette's numbers read like mythology: 82 miles of great intercepting sewers, plus about 1,100 miles of street sewers;[1] 318 million bricks; 3.5 million cubic yards of earth dug out, and nearly a million cubic yards of concrete poured;[3] 165 miles of old sewers replaced.[3]

Men dug by hand — there were no mechanical excavators. Bricklayers' wages rose by about 20%, and the price of bricks in London by about 50%.[3] Along both banks, the engineers built embankments — today's Victoria, Albert and Chelsea Embankments are the gifts that project left behind.[2] Great pumping stations rose at Abbey Mills and Crossness, like cathedrals of sewage. The embankment was part of the engineering itself: beneath it ran the intercepting sewer, above it strolled the promenaders — London's most respectable walkway, built on the roof of a drain.

In 1865, the first phase was complete, and the Prince of Wales sailed down the river to inspect it while Londoners cheered from the banks;[1] the whole system was finished in 1875, at a total cost of about £4.2 million.[13]

The proof came with the next cholera. In 1866, cholera returned and killed 5,596 Londoners, the epidemic concentrated in the East End.[11] The same city, the same disease — and from then on, the places with clean water and the places without it had different fates. Bazalgette's sewers are still working under London's streets today.[1] Beneath every street you walk, there is an answer a Victorian engineer wrote in brick.

EpilogueAfter the Stink

1858 belonged to more than a river. That same year, on 1 July, Darwin and Wallace's papers on evolution by natural selection were read to the Linnean Society;[8] on 2 August — the very day the sewerage act became law — Parliament passed the Government of India Act, taking India from the East India Company into the hands of the Crown;[9] and Florence Nightingale privately printed her report on army sanitation, showing that of 18,000 British deaths in the Crimea, some 16,000 were from preventable disease, not wounds.[12]

In a single summer, an empire relearned three things: how bodies evolve, how an empire is governed, and how an army stops dying of its own dirty water. Behind all three lay the same shift: from governing by feeling to governing by evidence.

The lesson of the Great Stink is not engineering; it is human nature. We react to threats one sense late: what we cannot see, we pretend is not there; what we can smell, we finally pay to remove. Cholera was invisible, so 30,000 people died; the stink could be smelled, so a law passed in eighteen days. The order of the senses decided the order of life and death.

Every city today has its own Thames. Each generation has things quietly being poured into the water — invisible, odourless, slow to act. The question was never whether we have the technology. London had the answer in 1858; what it lacked was a nose willing to smell.

Once the sewers were built, Londoners quickly forgot that summer. Forgetting is another skill. May we remember a little longer: a city's civilisation is not measured by how high it builds, but by how much it will spend on things it cannot see.

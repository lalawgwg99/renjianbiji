Luna: $40 Billion Gone in a Week

How a promise guaranteed by mathematics turned on itself within a week

SlowFolio Editorial Team · Reading time 12 min

繁中

Narrated in Mandarin · Female voice HsiaoChen

  • 2022On November 11, the cryptocurrency exchange FTX filed for bankruptcy, with $8 billion of customer money missing from its accounts; six months after Luna fell, the same play was staged again with a different lead.

PrologueThe One-Dollar Promise

Late on Monday night, May 9, 2022, a token that was supposed to equal one dollar forever fell below one dollar.

It was called TerraUSD, ticker UST. Before the crash it was the world's third-largest stablecoin, with $18.77 billion in circulation; its sister token LUNA had set an all-time high of $119.51 a little more than a month earlier and ranked among the ten largest cryptocurrencies by market value. [1][11] A week later, UST was worth 10 cents and LUNA was nearly zero. Estimates of the value destroyed range from $40 billion to $60 billion, depending on who is counting; by any measure, it was the largest collapse in cryptocurrency history. [2]

A stablecoin was supposed to be the most boring invention in cryptocurrency. Its only job was to be boring: one dollar today, one dollar tomorrow, never a story. UST's ambition was to do this boring thing to perfection — without dollar reserves, using mathematics alone. But a thing guaranteed by mathematics still needs people to believe in it one day. And people get frightened.

This book is not about a price chart. It is about how a promise was written into code, worshipped as a faith, and then turned around and devoured itself.

Chapter 1Two Tokens, One Faith

In 2018, Do Kwon and Daniel Shin founded Terraform Labs in Seoul and launched the Terra blockchain. [1] What they set out to build sounds simple enough: a digital currency that tracks the US dollar one-to-one, without holding any dollars behind it.

The mechanism was a pair of twin tokens. Burn $1 worth of LUNA and you could mint 1 UST; burn 1 UST and you got $1 worth of LUNA back. If UST traded above a dollar, arbitrageurs minted new UST and sold it, pushing the price down; if it traded below, they bought it cheap, burned it for LUNA, and pushed the price back up. In theory, the loop would hold the price at $1 forever. [1]

The catch was in the words "in theory." The whole design carried a premise that never made it into the white paper: LUNA had to be worth something, always. UST was not backed by dollars. It was backed by people's confidence in LUNA. Confidence itself was the collateral — and confidence is the only kind of collateral that looks stronger the more people use it, and turns out to be more fragile.

In February 2022, the Terra community voted to spend $38.15 million sponsoring the Washington Nationals for five years, renaming the ballpark's club level the "Terra Club." [1] It was the ecosystem's most triumphant moment — and its closest to the cliff.

LUNA's rise was the best advertisement of all. Through 2021 and into early 2022, it climbed all the way to its all-time high of $119.51. [1] Every new high told the same story: the system works, and everyone who doubted it was wrong. The founder's trash talk, the supporters' fervour, the media's applause — layer upon layer, they wrapped a mathematical mechanism into an identity. Holding LUNA was not just an investment. It was joining a revolution.

Chapter 2The 20% Honey

UST did not grow so fast because anyone wanted to spend it. It grew because of Anchor Protocol. Deposit UST in Anchor and you earned 19.45% a year; the US Securities and Exchange Commission would later describe it in its complaint as "as much as 20%." [1][3]

At the time, an American bank deposit paid under 1%. What does 20% mean? It means money falling from the sky. By the eve of the collapse, about 72% of all UST in circulation was parked in Anchor, waiting to collect. [10]

Where did the 20% come from? Not from borrowers' interest — that was nowhere near enough. The difference was subsidised from Terra's "yield reserve." The subsidy was burning fast, and the community only voted to start cutting the rate six days before the run began. By then it was too late. [11]

What is worth asking is why nobody called a halt. Because too many people benefited from the 20%. Retail savers treated it as a deposit account; institutions treated it as cash management; even other crypto protocols held UST as a reserve asset. Everyone in the ecosystem had a reason to believe the rate would keep being paid — as long as they were not the last one out. That is the classic collective self-deception: each person believes they have seen through the game, and that only others will be left holding it.

When a promise pays so much that it needs no explanation, people stop asking questions.

That is the human core of the whole affair. Twenty percent was not a rate of return. It was an anaesthetic. It got hundreds of thousands of people to do, all at once, something none of them would have done while clear-headed: hand their savings to a promise whose only guarantee was that more people believed in it too.

Chapter 3A Shield of 80,394 Bitcoins

In January 2022, Terraform Labs set up the Luna Foundation Guard (LFG) in Singapore, a non-profit whose job was to stockpile reserves for UST. By May 7 — the day before the peg broke — LFG held 80,394 bitcoins, worth about $3 billion at the time. [1][4]

The idea was that when the algorithm could not hold the line, real money would step in and buy. It was Terra's belated admission: a purely mathematical guarantee, it turned out, still needed collateral.

On May 8, as UST slid clearly below $1, LFG began converting its reserves into UST. It handed 52,189 bitcoins to a trading counterparty in exchange for more than 1.5 billion UST; on May 10, Terraform Labs sold another 33,206 bitcoins for 1.16 billion UST — what LFG itself called a "last ditch effort to defend the peg." [4][5]

It defended nothing. By May 16, LFG was down to 313 bitcoins. From 80,394 to 313. [4] In those same days, UST fell as low as $0.07. [5]

The numbers themselves tell the story. LFG's shield was worth about $3 billion, while the UST it had to defend amounted to $18.77 billion in circulation. The shield was one-sixth the size of the wall. [4][11] Even if every bitcoin had been sold at the top, it could not have filled the hole. The reserve was never designed to stop a full-scale run — it existed to convince everyone that a run would never happen. And when the run came, the reserve's existence became an accelerant: it proved that even the issuer knew the mathematical guarantee needed outside help.

The shield was not shattered. It was fed, coin by coin, into a hole that could not be filled. Every bitcoin sold was an admission to the market that the system needed outside help. And all the market heard was one sentence: even it no longer believed in itself.

Chapter 4May 7 to 13

Saturday, May 7. Two large wallets withdrew 375 million UST from Anchor; over the weekend, more than $2 billion left the protocol. [10][12] UST began drifting below $1 — first to $0.98. Nobody knew whether it was an attack or simply rich people heading for the exit.

Monday, May 9. UST fell to $0.90 and panic set in. Holders rushed to burn UST for LUNA, LUNA's supply began to swell, and its price fell from $80 to $60. [12]

May 10 and 11: the death spiral ran at full speed. Every redemption minted new LUNA; the cheaper LUNA got, the more of it the next redemption had to mint. UST fell to $0.30; LUNA fell from $60 to $1, then to $0.01, then to $0.0001. Its total supply exploded from about 350 million tokens to more than 6 trillion. [12]

May 12: UST hit $0.10. [1] Friday, May 13: the Terra blockchain was halted twice, a formal admission of death. [1]

Within a week, $40 to $45 billion had evaporated. The US Department of Justice would later put investor losses at $40 billion. [2][6]

In those days, exchanges suspended trading in LUNA and UST one after another; the numbers were still sitting in wallets, but they could no longer be sold. [1] For many holders, the cruellest part was not that the price went to zero — it was that the journey to zero was broadcast live, refreshing every minute: a little less, then a little less again. People posted screenshots of their accounts on social media and asked the crowd what to do. Nobody knew what to do, because the system had never contained a chapter called "what if this fails."

Chapter 5The Math Never Lied. People Did.

The death spiral was no mysterious curse. It was written into the mechanism in plain sight. Everyone involved could see it: once redemptions passed a certain size, LUNA's supply would expand exponentially and its price would collapse exponentially. The mechanism did not break. It worked exactly as designed — it had contained a self-destruct switch from the beginning; nobody believed anyone would press it.

The problem was on the human side. Founder Do Kwon was famous for his trash talk, and his supporters called themselves "Lunatics" — madmen. It was not an insult. It was a badge of honour. At Luna's peak, anyone who asked whether a 20% yield could last was dismissed as an outsider who did not understand the maths. That is the strangest thing about crowd psychology: the more a belief is surrounded by mocked doubters, the more its believers feel they possess the truth.

Later academic work reached the same conclusion. A study by the US National Bureau of Economic Research attributed the run mainly to doubts about whether the system could last, rather than to any concentrated market manipulation. [1] Nobody attacked. Everybody started doubting at the same time.

There is a deeper trap here, called outsourcing trust to code. Terra's supporters liked to say "code is law": the rules are fixed, so nobody can cheat and nobody can go back on their word. But May 2022 proved something: code can guarantee that rules are executed, but it cannot guarantee that the rules themselves are right. A flawed rule, executed faithfully to the end, produces a perfectly executed disaster.

There is also a belated footnote. In December 2024, Tai Mo Shan, an affiliate of the trading firm Jump Trading, agreed to pay $123 million to settle SEC claims that it had misled investors about the stability of UST. [1] The maths never lied. But the story about the maths was told by people.

If a system's stability depends entirely on everyone believing it is stable, is it stable at all?

Chapter 6The First Domino

Luna's collapse was not the end. It was the opening. The hedge fund Three Arrows Capital, heavily exposed to LUNA, was ordered into liquidation by a court in the British Virgin Islands on June 29, 2022. [7] The lender Celsius froze all withdrawals on June 12, trapping $4.7 billion of customer funds, and filed for bankruptcy a month later. [8] Voyager froze withdrawals on July 1 and followed into bankruptcy days later; Genesis held out until November, then filed in January 2023. [7][8]

Six months later, another exchange fell: FTX filed for bankruptcy on November 11, 2022, with $8 billion of customer money missing from its accounts. [9] Within a single year, crypto suffered two great collapses — two movements of the same piece of music.

The lawsuits followed. On June 17, 2022, American investors filed a class action against Terraform Labs in federal court in California; that November, the High Court of Singapore accepted a $56.9 million class action of its own. [1] The money was gone. All that remained was the question of responsibility — and that account would take years to settle.

On January 21, 2024, Terraform Labs filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in the United States. [1] Its story had formally moved into the courtroom.

EpilogueThe Weight of a Promise

In September 2022, South Korean prosecutors issued an arrest warrant for Do Kwon. [13] On February 16, 2023, the US Securities and Exchange Commission charged Terraform Labs and Kwon with fraud. [3] On March 23, 2023, he was arrested at Podgorica Airport in Montenegro, trying to board a flight to Dubai on a false passport. [1] The United States and South Korea fought over extradition for almost two years; on December 31, 2024, he was finally delivered into US custody. [6]

In August 2025, he pleaded guilty to two counts of fraud; on December 11, 2025, a federal court in New York sentenced him to 15 years in prison. [1][6] The South Korean case is still waiting for him. [13]

A one-dollar promise takes a few lines of code to write. Repaying it took 15 years.

After the crash, the market gave the word "algorithmic" a wide berth, and stablecoins returned to the old road of full collateral backing. The original chain was renamed Terra Classic, the original LUNA became LUNA Classic, and a new LUNA was issued — the names changed, but the lesson stayed. [1] A system can change its name, its token, its founder. What it cannot change is the most fundamental question of all: what, exactly, is your promise secured by?

Luna's lesson for the world is not simply that "algorithmic stablecoins don't work." Any promise — written on paper, written in code, or written in a person's eyes — is ultimately secured not by mathematics but by trust. And trust has one characteristic: it takes years to build and a week to destroy.

The next time someone assures you that something "cannot fail, mathematically," ask them one question: the maths may not fail — but what about the people telling you the maths?

May '68: Beneath the Pavement, the Beach

In May 1968, some ten million French workers stopped work at once — a revolution with no leader and no plan that nearly rewrote a nation

PrologueBeneath the Pavement, the Beach

Late on the night of May 10, 1968, in the Latin Quarter of Paris, students prised up paving stones, overturned cars, and built 60 barricades. At 2:15 in the morning, riot police attacked, and tear gas filled the Boulevard Saint-Germain. By dawn, 367 people were wounded and 460 arrested — and the radio had broadcast the whole night live to the entire country.[2]

Within a month, some 10 million people across France would lay down their work. Trains stopped, petrol ran out, rubbish piled up in the streets, and the government of Charles de Gaulle nearly fell.[1] The storm had no leader, no headquarters, no plan written in advance. It had only a sentence painted on a wall: beneath the paving stones, the beach.

What makes May '68 worth reading is not that it almost succeeded, but that it demonstrated a rare political phenomenon: how a society can, within a single month, lose patience simultaneously with its government, its universities, its factories, and its way of life — and then, within another month, take it all back.

Chapter 1A University That Could Not Hold Its Students

It all began with a university that could not hold its students. After the Second World War, French higher education ballooned: in ten years, student numbers rose from 192,000 to 478,000, while lecture halls, teachers, and dormitories all fell behind.[9] And opportunity did not grow more equal: in 1964, only 2% of university students were the children of workers and peasants.[9]

The University of Nanterre, west of Paris, was the problem in miniature. Built on a former army training ground surrounded by shanty towns, it crammed 11,000 students into makeshift buildings.[3] From 1967, students first protested the dormitory rules — men and women were forbidden to visit each other's rooms — then the overcrowding, the rote-learning lectures, and a university that treated them like products on an assembly line.

On March 22, 1968, about 150 students occupied Nanterre's administration building and founded the "Movement of 22 March."[2] One of its leaders was Daniel Cohn-Bendit, a 23-year-old sociology student. No one imagined that a protest that began over dormitory rules would bring the whole of France to a halt eight weeks later.

The administration's answer was to close the doors. On May 2, Nanterre was shut down, and the protesting students moved to the Sorbonne in central Paris — the symbol of French higher education. The decision to drive the protest off campus delivered it, instead, into every living room in France.

Chapter 2The Night of the Barricades

On May 3, about 300 students gathered in the Sorbonne courtyard to protest the closure of Nanterre and the threatened expulsion of their classmates. The rector called in the police to clear them — the first time in the Sorbonne's history that police had been ordered inside to enforce order.[3] The rector then ordered the Sorbonne closed. The students lost a place to meet, but gained something better: a story.

On May 6, the National Union of Students of France and the university teachers' union called a march. More than 20,000 students, teachers, and supporters walked toward the Sorbonne; police charged with batons, and students answered with paving stones. The day became known as "Bloody Monday": 422 arrests, 345 police injured.[2]

The real turning point came on May 10. Some 30,000 students gathered on the Left Bank and built 50 to 60 barricades. When negotiations broke down, riot police attacked at 2:15 a.m., and the fighting lasted until dawn: 367 wounded, 460 arrested.[2] The radio broadcast it live; television showed the pictures the next day. For the first time, France could see clearly that the people being beaten were not thugs, but their own children.

Then the walls began to speak. Students and teachers of the École des Beaux-Arts occupied their school and founded the Atelier Populaire, the People's Workshop, printing posters day and night. On the walls appeared the most famous sentence of the era: "Beneath the paving stones, the beach!" — for when students prised up the stones to build barricades, they found sand underneath. Beside it: "It is forbidden to forbid," "Power to the imagination," "Be realistic, demand the impossible."[7][8]

The power of a slogan is that it needs no argument. "Beneath the paving stones, the beach" proposed no policy, yet it said exactly what a whole generation felt: the road underfoot, which looked so solid, could be prised open — and what lay beneath was not emptiness, but another way of living.

"Beneath the paving stones, the beach!" "It is forbidden to forbid!" — graffiti and posters on the streets of Paris, May 1968

Chapter 3Ten Million People Not Going to Work

After the Night of the Barricades, the unions joined in. On May 13, the CGT, the CFDT, and the teachers' union called a 24-hour general strike, and the march through Paris drew more than a million people, while the police deliberately kept a low profile.[2] The students thought they had gained allies; the workers thought they had found an exit.

But the strike soon escaped everyone's control. From May 14, wildcat strikes spread from factory to factory: Renault and Citroën were occupied by their workers, and railways, the postal service, electricity, and television shut down one after another. By May 17, nearly 10 million workers were on strike — about two-thirds of the French workforce — the largest general strike in French history; across May and June, some 150 million working days were lost.[1][3]

Students and workers never wanted the same things. Students wanted freedom, dignity, and a different kind of university; workers wanted higher wages, shorter hours, and recognition for their unions. The two groups shared no common programme, yet in the same month they stopped at the same time — because they shared the same feeling: this country worked very well, just not for them.

That is the hardest thing about May '68 to copy, and the hardest to understand: it was not organised, it was contagious. No central committee ordered the strike; each factory watched the factory next door stop, and decided to stop too. A mass movement is at its most powerful not when it is most disciplined, but when it most resembles a mood.

Chapter 4Grenelle's Victory — and Its Refusal

On May 25, the government, employers, and the major unions sat down to negotiate at the Ministry of Social Affairs on the Rue de Grenelle. After two days and nights they reached an agreement: the national minimum wage would rise 35%, all salaries 10%, the agricultural minimum wage 56%, with union organisation recognised inside firms and partial compensation for strike days.[3] By any measure, it was an unprecedented victory.

But on May 27, when CGT secretary-general Georges Séguy carried the agreement to the Renault factory, the workers rejected it on the spot. The same day, 50,000 to 60,000 people gathered at the Charléty stadium demanding that the struggle continue.[2] What the workers rejected was not the pay rise; it was "only a pay rise" — the agreement could not give them what they actually wanted.

De Gaulle's government was equally lost. On May 24, the president announced a referendum on the radio, and almost no one listened. On May 29, he slipped out of Paris and flew to the French military base at Baden-Baden in West Germany to confirm the army's loyalty; on May 30, at 4:30 p.m., he returned to Paris and announced in a radio address of barely four and a half minutes that he would not resign, that he was dissolving the National Assembly, and that elections would be held on June 23.[4] That same evening, hundreds of thousands of supporters of order marched down the Champs-Élysées.

This is the paradox at the heart of May '68: the revolution almost succeeded precisely because it did not seek power — no one could negotiate with it, and no one could crush a movement with no centre. And the regime almost fell precisely because it did not know whom to negotiate with. Once de Gaulle reduced the choice to "me, or chaos," the scales began to tip the other way.

Chapter 5The Ballot Box Takes Back the Streets

The elections of June 23 and 30 produced a result no one had expected. De Gaulle's party won 353 seats in a National Assembly of 486 — an absolute majority such as the Fifth Republic had never seen; the Communists were left with 34 seats, the Socialists with 57, the worst defeat of the French left since the Second World War.[5] Voters frightened by the collapse of order had come home, and infighting on the left had helped.

By mid-June, the Sorbonne had been cleared, the students occupying the Odéon theatre driven out, and the street movement dissolved into the summer holidays. But the institutions remembered that May: in November, parliament passed the Faure Law, reorganising the universities — students and teachers gained a say in running them, and interdisciplinary study became a pillar of the reform.[6] The 35% rise in the minimum wage stayed, and so did the unions' new place inside the firm.

De Gaulle won the election and lost the era. On April 27, 1969, he put Senate and regional reform to a referendum, promising to resign if he lost; 52% of voters rejected it, and he resigned the next morning.[4] What forced him out was not the street, but a gamble of his own making — and the gamble was lost because, after May 1968, the French no longer believed that "order" needed just one man to guard it.

EpilogueThe Revolution That Did Not Win

May '68 lost in politics and won in culture. It overthrew no government, built no new institutions, and left behind no common programme. Yet France after it was never the same: sexual liberation, feminism, the environmental movement, the democratisation of education, suspicion of all authority — nearly every French social movement of the following half-century can find its first sentence on a wall in May 1968.

It leaves three lessons. First, revolution is not a plan; it is a contagion. No one writes the script in advance; the script is written in the streets, night after night. Second, crowds are not persuaded; they are ignited. 367 wounded and 460 arrested spoke more persuasively than any speech. Third, what a system fears most is not its opponents, but being seen by everyone at once to have stopped moving — when the universities cannot hold their students, the factories cannot keep their workers, and the president cannot even reach his own cabinet, a country has already stopped; all that remains is to wait for a day to say so out loud.

If the students and workers of May 1968 had actually seized power, what would France have become? The question has no answer — because they never seriously thought about seizing power. And that may be the hardest problem May '68 left for everyone who came after: when you finally have the chance to tear everything down, what will you put in its place?

In Paris in May 1968, the paving stones were prised up, and the beach did not appear. But from that day on, the French knew one thing as they walked: the road underfoot can be prised open.

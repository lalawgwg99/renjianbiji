Mendel and the Peas: The Genetics a Monk Counted Into Being

In 1866, a paper was printed in Brno. It took 34 years before anyone understood it. In between, science lost to intuition — and won itself back.

PrologueA Paper That Waited 34 Years

In 1866, the proceedings of the Natural History Society of Brno printed a 45-page paper titled "Experiments on Plant Hybridization." Its author was an Augustinian friar named Mendel. After it was printed, nothing happened. No debate, no letters, no visitors. Thirty-four years later, in the spring of 1900, three botanists in three different countries each announced that they had discovered the laws of heredity — and then, while surveying the literature, each in turn stumbled upon this paper. It had been lying in libraries all along, correct in every particular, waiting for an age that could finally ask the right question.[1][6]

This essay is not about how a genius was buried. The burial itself is the subject. The question worth asking about Mendel is not how clever he was, but what a society — what a discipline — must possess in order to receive a correct answer, and which missing conditions can let even a correct answer sink like noise, quietly, out of sight.

Chapter OneBlood Grows Thinner as It Mixes

Before Mendel, almost everyone believed that heredity worked like mixing paint. A father's red and a mother's white blend into pink; pink mixed with pink yields a paler pink. Generation after generation, the differences grow fainter until nothing is left. Farmers had long known that crossing could improve a breed, and gardeners knew that traits sometimes skipped a generation and reappeared, but no one could explain why. Intuition supplied the answer: traits blend, and they fade away.[2][3]

The trouble with this intuition was that it worked just well enough. In everyday experience, children often do look halfway between their parents; a mule really does fall between a horse and a donkey. An intuition that is good enough is the hardest kind to overthrow, because it never has to answer for its exceptions. When a trait came back, people called it a throwback, an exception, luck. No one considered that the exception might be the rule.

Darwin was stuck in the same place. When On the Origin of Species appeared in 1859, natural selection needed a ceaseless supply of variation — yet if heredity truly blended, variation would only grow thinner with each generation, leaving selection nothing to work on. Darwin never resolved this knot in his lifetime. He did not know that the answer had already been printed in 1866, in an obscure volume of proceedings from Brno, a paper he never saw.[10][7]

Chapter TwoThe Mathematician in the Monastery

Mendel was born on July 20, 1822, in Heinzendorf in Austrian Silesia, the son of a peasant family so poor that even school fees were a struggle. In 1843 he entered St. Thomas's Abbey in Brno and took the name Gregor. Joining the monastery was not only a religious calling; it was a way out. The abbey gave him a free education, a library, and a garden.[6]

The abbey sent him to the University of Vienna to study physics and natural history, and he returned to Brno to teach. He twice failed the oral examination for teacher certification, in 1850 and again in 1856. A man judged unfit to teach in the examination room went back to the monastery garden and began doing something no biologist of his day did at all: he counted.[8][6]

He chose the pea. Peas self-pollinate, so their lines stay pure; their flowers suit hand pollination; and most importantly, their traits come in clean contrasting pairs — round or wrinkled, yellow or green, tall or short — with no blurry middle ground. From 34 varieties he selected 22, fixed on seven pairs of traits, and began an endurance trial that no one around him understood.[8][2]

Abbot Napp sent him to Vienna, and the abbey gave him the garden for his experiments. Histories of science usually credit a single person, but Mendel's experiment was first of all a product of an institution: a monastery willing to keep a friar for eight years, doing no productive work, doing nothing but counting peas.

Chapter ThreeThe Man Who Counted to 3:1

From 1856 to 1863, Mendel cultivated and tested some 28,000 pea plants in the monastery garden. His method differed from every breeder of his time: where others looked at the whole plant, he looked at one pair of traits; where others described, he counted. In the experiment on seed shape alone, he counted 7,324 seeds from 253 hybrid plants: 5,474 round, 1,850 wrinkled — a ratio close to 3:1.[2][8][1]

One such number is a coincidence; seven of them are a law. All seven pairs of traits delivered roughly 3:1 in the second generation. The trait that vanished in the first generation returned on schedule in the second, in exactly the same proportion. Mendel's conclusion was a single sentence, yet enough to overturn the intuition of an entire age: traits do not blend. Whatever controls a trait — he called it a "factor" — exists in pairs inside the offspring, one from the father and one from the mother. One may mask the other, but the masked one is not gone; it is only hidden, waiting to reappear in the next generation.[1][2]

The masked trait is not gone; it is only hidden, waiting to reappear in the next generation.

That is why blending looked right. Blending theory only ever saw the first generation: cross round with wrinkled, and the first generation is all round, as if wrinkled had been washed away. It never waited for the second generation. Mendel waited. There was nothing abstruse about his method — patience, counting, a single question — and the hard part was only to trust the numbers rather than the eyes.

The seven pairs of traits assorted independently of one another, a finding later called independent assortment. Mendel had no word for "gene," no word for "chromosome"; he did not even know what was inside a cell. He had only numbers, and the logic behind them. But that was enough: heredity is not a liquid, it is particulate; not a fusion, but a distribution.[1]

Chapter FourWhy No One Understood

On February 8 and March 8, 1865, Mendel read his paper at two meetings of the Natural History Society of Brno. Local newspapers reported it, and the reception was apparently not unkind. In 1866, the paper appeared in volume 4 of the society's proceedings, pages 3 to 47. Then the silence began.[1][6][9]

The paper was buried not because it was poorly written, but because it fell into every wrong crack at once. The proceedings had a small circulation, sent to few institutions, and were barely known outside central Europe; the title spoke of "hybridization," not heredity, so most readers took it for a gardener's report; and most decisively, the paper was biology written in mathematics, while the botanists of the day did not read numbers — they looked at specimens, drew, and classified. A paper full of ratios and probabilities read to them like something written in a foreign language.[7][2]

Mendel sent a reprint to Carl Nägeli, the most eminent botanist in Munich. Nägeli wrote back encouragingly, but suggested he repeat the work with hawkweed — a more "important" plant. Mendel obeyed. Hawkweed reproduces by apomixis, setting seed without fertilization, and Mendel's ratios failed there entirely. It took him years to understand that he had followed the wrong advice.[7]

In 1868, Mendel was elected abbot of the monastery. Administrative duties and a dispute with the government over taxation consumed the rest of his life, and he published almost no more science. He died in Brno on January 6, 1884, aged 61. At his death he was the abbot of a provincial monastery, not a scientific celebrity. The traditional account says his paper was cited about three times in 35 years; recent historical research puts the true figure higher, but still vanishingly small — small enough to ignore.[6][5]

Chapter Five1900 — Three Men Knocking at Once

In the spring of 1900, three botanists published papers announcing that they had discovered the laws of heredity: Hugo de Vries in the Netherlands, Carl Correns in Germany, and Erich von Tschermak in Austria. De Vries had worked with evening primroses, Correns with maize and peas, Tschermak with peas. Three men, three plants, the same ratios. And then, while surveying the literature, each in turn came upon Mendel's paper of 1866.[4][7]

When Correns read de Vries's paper and found no mention of Mendel in it, he published at once, stating publicly that priority belonged to the friar from Brno, 34 years earlier. Tschermak followed. No one had plagiarized anyone — all three had genuinely arrived at the same answer on their own. What matters is not that three men discovered it at once, but that the age had finally reached the point where the question could be asked: cytology had advanced, microscopes could see chromosomes, and Darwin's theory of evolution had forced everyone to ask how variation is passed on at all.[9][4]

In 1902, Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri proposed the chromosome theory of inheritance: Mendel's "factors" live on chromosomes, which occur in pairs and separate during reproduction — exactly what is needed to explain why factors pair and why they part. What a friar had worked out with arithmetic in a garden found its physical body under the microscope 36 years later.[4]

Histories of science call 1900 the "rediscovery." The word is not quite right. The paper was never lost; it had been sitting in libraries all along. What was lost was the ability to read it. What was rediscovered in 1900 was, in truth, the scientific community itself.

EpilogueWhat Keeps Science Alive

Looking back, Mendel's paper survived thanks to a handful of utterly ordinary facts: a society printed it; libraries shelved it; reprints were mailed; nobody burned it. None of these was a miracle; every one was an institution. Science is most fragile not when it lacks geniuses, but when it lacks the institutions that record, preserve, and let later generations find what was recorded.[7]

But institutions can only preserve an answer; they cannot guarantee that it will be understood. Understanding requires the question to ripen first. In 1866, biology was asking how species change, not how traits are transmitted; by 1900, the question had changed, and the answer was taken down from the shelf. Mendel was defeated not by stupidity, but by timing. And timing is not fate — it is the maturity of a whole discipline, including the microscope, cytology, evolutionary theory, and a new generation willing to trust numbers.

If a correct paper can lie in a library for 34 years, might some answer we cannot yet question be lying on today's shelves too?

Mendel did not live to see that day. Sixteen years after his death, his name entered the textbooks. But what he left behind was more than a 3:1 ratio — it was a demonstration: one person, one garden, eight years, and the plainest of methods can answer a question that an entire age could not. Science survives on luck, but it moves forward on people like him.

Penglai Rice: How One Grain Rewrote an Island's Fate

In 1926, the governor-general named a new rice 'Penglai' — the rice of the fairy isle. Behind half a century of breeding lay a long negotiation over eating, empire, and survival.

PrologueColonisation at the Dinner Table

In 1918, Japan erupted in the Rice Riots. Rice prices soared, fishermen's wives in Toyama took to the streets to seize rice, and the unrest spread across the country until Prime Minister Terauchi Masatake's cabinet fell. The head of an empire lost his office over a bowl of rice. From that year on, the Japanese government learned one thing: rice was not just food, it was the lifeline of the regime. And the empire's granary lay across the sea — in Taiwan.

Taiwanese rice had one problem: it did not taste good. At least, not to Japanese palates. Taiwan grew indica rice, the local "zailai" variety — long grains that cooked up dry and loose, nothing like the short, round, sticky japonica rice the Japanese loved. Japonica, planted in Taiwan, fell to pests and disease and refused to thrive in the climate. The empire wanted Taiwan's rice but could not eat it. That contradiction became one agronomist's lifelong task.[1]

The agronomist was Iso Eikichi. Born in Hiroshima in 1886, he graduated from the agricultural faculty of Tohoku Imperial University in 1911 and came to Taiwan the following year, staying for 46 years. His assignment, put simply, was this: make the rice the Japanese loved grow on Taiwanese soil.[2]

Chapter 1The Impossible Cross

Before 1922, agronomists agreed on one thing: crossing japonica with indica was nearly impossible. The two were too distantly related; their offspring were sterile or split apart into useless traits. Iso chose the hardest path anyway. Working with Suenaga Jin, a technician at the Taichung Agricultural Experiment Station, he crossed the Japanese japonica "Shinriki" with the Taiwanese indica "Kameji" again and again — more than 1,000 combinations.[4]

In 1922, the cross succeeded. The offspring was developed into "Taichung No. 65": it thrived in both the first and second crops, resisted lodging, and yielded markedly more with fertilizer. Farmers called it a dream variety. Its secret lay in its genes — a rare photoperiod-insensitive gene that freed it from the length of daylight, allowing two harvests a year. That trait later became the key to Penglai rice sweeping the whole island.[1][4]

But a variety was only the first step. The greatest enemy of Japanese rice in Taiwan was not genetics but climate. Taiwan was too hot: seedlings grew too fast, headed before they had grown enough, and yields stayed low. In 1923, Suenaga proposed the "young-seedling transplanting method," cutting the nursery period from two months to one so the plants had time to grow strong before heading. Iso found the scientific explanation in the carbon-nitrogen ratio and published it as the "young seedling theory," turning craft into teachable technique.[1][4]

Chapter 2Making Japanese Rice Live in Taiwan

For a variety to move from the experiment station to every paddy on the island, it needed an entire system. To keep the seed pure, the team set up a foundation seed farm at Zhuzihu on Yangmingshan — geographically isolated, so varieties could not mix — and it became the source of all Penglai seed in Taiwan. A three-tier system of foundation, multiplication, and farm seed was established, and Taiwan had a modern seed supply system for the first time.[1]

Promotion could not be done by decree either. The colonial government built irrigation works, set up experiment stations and agricultural warehouses, improved fertilizer, fought pests, and pushed second cropping. Penglai rice yielded about 20% more than zailai rice and sold for 5% to 10% more; farmers could do the arithmetic, and the planted area spread fast. In the end, a new variety wins only when farmers vote with their feet.[3]

Chapter 31926: A Name

In 1926, Governor-General Izawa Takio formally named the japonica rice grown in Taiwan "Penglai rice" at the 19th National Rice Conference. "Penglai" is the mythical isle of the immortals, and also an old poetic name for Taiwan. One name turned a grain of rice into the rice of the fairy isle — and turned a breeding experiment into an achievement of the empire.[1][2]

The naming was not a ceremony; it was policy. From that year on, "Penglai rice" became the official brand, promoted with full force: seed, technique, and purchase, all in one chain. Taiwan's rice economy began to flip. In the 1910s, the island produced less than 5 million koku of rice; by the 1920s, once Penglai rice had spread, output approached 10 million koku at its peak — the same land, twice the harvest.[3]

The bigger flip happened at the ports. Before Penglai rice, Taiwan exported only about 1 million koku, 23% of output; after it spread, exports surged to 5.2 million koku, 53% of output. More than half of Taiwan's rice was shipped to Japan. The island changed from the "island of sugar" into the "island of rice and sugar," and rice became the second lifeline of the empire's food security.[3]

Chapter 4The Island of Rice and Sugar

Penglai rice redrew Taiwan's economic map. In 1901, Nitobe Inazo had proposed his plan for reforming the sugar industry, and for a time Taiwan's economy was a one-crop world of cane sugar. Once Penglai rice spread, rice and sugar stood side by side as the two great commercial crops, and the structure of Taiwan's agricultural production and foreign trade changed completely. Cane and rice — one sweet, one plain — became the two pillars of the colonial economy.[3]

But who stood beneath those pillars? Most of the profit from Penglai rice never reached the farmers. Four great Japanese trading houses — Mitsui Bussan, Mitsubishi Shoji, Kato Shokai, and Sugihara Sangyo — controlled more than 90% of Taiwan's rice prices and output. Farmers harvested 20% more and sold it for 5% to 10% more, but they did not set the price. The rice got better to eat; the seating order at the dinner table did not change.[3]

More subtle still was the Taiwanese dinner table itself. Most Penglai rice was shipped to Japan; Taiwanese people still ate mainly zailai rice. An island grew good rice for the empire and ate second-rate rice itself. That was not the breeder's intention, but it was the colonial norm: the best of everything flows to the centre first.

Chapter 5Whose Rice?

When the war ended, Penglai rice stayed and the empire left. Iso Eikichi did not leave with the Japanese; he remained in Taiwan as a professor of agronomy at National Taiwan University and an adviser to the provincial government, continuing to improve rice varieties until he retired to Japan in 1957. Before he left, the Taiwan Provincial Assembly resolved to supply him with Penglai rice for the rest of his life — a variety bred by a coloniser, returned as a gift by the colonised. He died in 1972, aged 85.[2]

In 46 years in Taiwan, Iso published 73 research papers and bred 214 varieties. Behind the figures lies a stubbornness: one agronomist betting his life on a single idea, that science could solve the problem of eating. The belief was not wrong; what was wrong was thinking that eating was only a scientific problem. It was also a question of power, of markets, of identity.[2][4]

After the war, the breeding of Penglai rice carried on: from solving environmental adaptation, to pursuing stable and abundant harvests, to today's emphasis on quality, character, and resilience. The photoperiod-insensitive gene of Taichung No. 65 still flows in the bloodline of Taiwan's rice varieties. The family tree of a single grain of rice records a whole century of the island.[1]

Questions That Remain Open

In honesty, the following two things have no settled answer, and this essay does not pretend otherwise:

  • How much richer did Penglai rice actually make Taiwanese farmers? The figures of about 20% more yield and 5% to 10% higher prices come from the colonial government's promotion; after fertilizer, seed, and the trading houses' margins, no surviving ledger can say how much farmers actually gained.
  • Would Taiwan still be an island of sugar without Penglai rice? There is no answer to that question. But one thing is certain: what an island eats is never only a matter of taste — it is a question of who has the power to decide what the land grows.

EpilogueEvery Bowl of Rice Has a History

The name given in 1926 is still on Taiwan's rice bags today. Penglai rice has travelled from the product of a colonial policy, to an everyday sight on Taiwanese dinner tables, to a memory of the land itself. When we pick up a bowl of rice, we rarely think of the 1,000 crosses, the 46 years of experiments, a governor-general's naming, and a riot over rice.

But every bowl of rice has a history. The story of Penglai rice is a reminder that the dinner table is the most honest site of history: an empire's anxiety, one agronomist's stubbornness, and the fate of an island, all condensed into a single short, round, sticky, fragrant grain. Eating was never just eating — it is how an island deals with the world.

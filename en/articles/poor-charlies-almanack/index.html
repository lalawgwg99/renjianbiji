Poor Charlie's Almanack: The 99-Year-Old Who Left Behind a Way of Thinking, Not a Fortune

Edited by Peter D. Kaufman, first published in 2005 and reissued by Stripe Press in 2023; the Traditional Chinese commemorative edition came from Business Weekly in 2024. It reached No. 29 on Kingstone's bestseller chart for the first half of 2026. This deep read asks: how does a collection of speeches stay in print for 20 years? What is a latticework of mental models? And what, exactly, is so 'common' about Munger's common sense?

PrologueNovember 28, 2023: A Book Outlives Its Author by Seven Days

On November 28, 2023, Charlie Munger died in Santa Barbara, California, at 99, 34 days short of his 100th birthday.[4] Seven days later, on December 5, Stripe Press published a new edition of Poor Charlie's Almanack — an edition Munger had helped edit and whose cover he had personally approved.[5] The man was gone; the book was back on the shelves within the week. It had first appeared in 2005, when Peter D. Kaufman gathered 20 years of Munger's speeches and talks into a single volume, its title a tribute to Benjamin Franklin's Poor Richard's Almanack.[3][7]

The Traditional Chinese edition kept pace: Business Weekly published the first edition on May 1, 2011, revised it in 2019, and issued a commemorative collector's edition in 2024 — 496 pages, translated by Li Lu, ISBN 9786267366882.[9][10] In the first half of 2026 it reached No. 29 on Kingstone's overall bestseller chart — a collection of speeches, still on the charts 20 years after publication.[2]

This deep read asks three questions: how does a collection of speeches stay in print for 20 years? What exactly is a "latticework of mental models"? And what, precisely, is so common about Munger's "common sense"?

Chapter OneOmaha, 1959: A Dinner

Munger was born in Omaha on January 1, 1924, the son of a local lawyer. As a teenager he clerked at Buffett & Son, the grocery store run by Warren Buffett's grandfather — Buffett worked in the same store as a boy, but the two were six and a half years apart in age and never crossed paths there.[4][6] He studied mathematics at the University of Michigan, served in the Army Air Corps during the Second World War, studied meteorology at Caltech, and graduated from Harvard Law School in 1948 with a J.D., magna cum laude.[4]

In 1959 his father died, and Munger returned to Omaha to wind up his father's law practice. The Davis family arranged a dinner so he could meet Buffett. They talked for hours. Buffett later recalled: "About five minutes into it, Charlie was sort of rolling on the floor laughing at his own jokes — which is exactly the same thing I did. I thought, 'I'm not going to find another guy like this.'"[6][8]

But they did not become partners right away. Through the 1960s each ran his own shop: Buffett his partnership in Omaha, Munger a law practice in Los Angeles, and from 1962 his own investment firm, Wheeler, Munger & Co. From 1962 to 1975 his partnership compounded at 19.8% a year, against 5.0% a year for the Dow.[4] In 1978 he became vice chairman of Berkshire Hathaway, a post he held for 45 years, until his death.[4] Buffett called him the "architect" of modern Berkshire's philosophy.[4]

That history explains the book's tone. It is not theory written by an academic, but notes assembled in hindsight by a man who had practised law, developed real estate, managed money — and lost money, down 32% in 1973 and 31% in 1974.[4] Every talk in the book has a real occasion behind it: USC, Stanford Law School, the Harvard Law class of 1948 at its 50th reunion, a meeting of foundation financial officers.[3]

Chapter TwoThe Lattice and the Hammer

Munger's most famous idea fits in a single sentence: think with a latticework of mental models. His proposal: every major discipline holds a few truly big ideas. Take them out, hang them on a lattice in your head, and when a problem arrives, run the lattice over it.[3] Compound interest from mathematics, incentives from psychology, evolution from biology, redundancy from engineering, opportunity cost from economics — no single discipline can explain the world on its own.

The cautionary tale is the "man with a hammer": give someone only a hammer and every problem looks like a nail. Munger said this is the most common defect of professional training: the economist explains everything with supply and demand, the psychologist with childhood, the lawyer with statutes.[3] The lattice exists to force you to switch tools.

The second idea is the circle of competence: know what you understand, and — more importantly — know what you do not, and only bet inside the circle. Buffett repeats the phrase constantly; the source is Munger's reminder that the size of the circle does not matter, knowing where its edge lies does. In the book it is not a slogan but a discipline: a business you cannot understand is not a bargain at any price.

The third is inversion: "Invert, always invert," after the German mathematician Jacobi. To learn how to succeed, first work out how to fail; to learn how to make money, first work out how to lose it. His favourite example: "All I want to know is where I'm going to die, so I won't go there."[3] Inversion is not pessimism. Turn a hard problem around, and the answer often shows itself.

The fourth is "take a simple idea and take it seriously": compounding, incentives, marginal utility — each is simple on its own. The hard part is actually living by them for 40 years. Munger's lattice is not a collection. It is a set of working drawings.

Chapter ThreeTwenty-Five Ways to Be Wrong

The thickest talk in the book is "The Psychology of Human Misjudgment," delivered at Harvard in 1995 and revised for the book in 2005. In it Munger lists 25 psychological tendencies — not a textbook taxonomy, but items he picked up one by one from investing, law, and life.[3]

The first and most important is incentives: show people the reward and you will see the behaviour. "Show me the incentive and I'll show you the outcome," he said for the rest of his life. Federal Express paid its night shift by the number of packages moved, and the packages arrived on time; pay doctors per operation and operations multiply. If you cannot understand an organisation's behaviour, look at its reward system first.

The others are just as sharp. Liking and disliking blind the eyes: like someone and you cannot see their faults; dislike someone and you cannot see their merits. Envy and deprivation: people react far more violently to losing something than to gaining it. Authority: words from a person in a white coat simply carry more weight. And plain doubt-avoidance: people would rather have a wrong answer than a question left hanging.

The most frightening thing about the 25 is not any one of them alone, but several firing at once. Munger gave that pile-up a name: the Lollapalooza Effect — when multiple biases point in the same direction, people do extreme things.[3] Market bubbles, cults, mass panics all run on the same mechanism: incentives, imitation, envy, and denial, all aligned.

One caution belongs here: Munger's list is a set of "tendencies," not laws. It is his own synthesis, not the consensus of psychology textbooks, and the book does not attach a paper to each item. Used as a checklist, it is close to what Munger intended. Memorised as settled science, it is not.

Chapter FourIt Sells Because It Is Not an Investing Book

First, a counterintuitive fact: this book barely teaches anyone how to buy stocks. There is no stock-picking formula, no lesson in reading financial statements, no tips. Of the 11 talks, only some are about investing at all; the rest are about education, the faults of academic economics, how charitable foundations should invest, and how to live a life.[3] It sells precisely because it does not read like an investing book.

Second, the form. The original is not a square, sober treatise: a large format, illustrations, sidebars, photographs, Munger's handwritten notes — something closer to a scrapbook. A reader can open it at any page, read for five minutes, and put it down. That is what turns it into a gift book and a bedside book, rather than a textbook to be read cover to cover.[3][5]

Third, the people. In the foreword, Buffett credits Munger with transforming his thinking, saying his influence was irreplaceable and that without it he would be far poorer than he is.[1] The richest investor in the world, saying in public that half his achievement belongs to someone else — no advertisement works better than that. The subtitle, "Buffett's wise partner of 50 years," sells the relationship itself.

Fourth, timing. In December 2023 the Stripe Press edition appeared, seven days after Munger's death, with a free online edition, audio narration, and archival photographs released alongside it, bringing a book from 2005 back in front of young readers.[5] Taiwan's commemorative edition followed in 2024 and reached No. 29 on Kingstone's overall chart in the first half of 2026.[2][10]

The last reason is the hardest to copy: Munger's sentences themselves. "Take a simple idea and take it seriously." "I have nothing to add" — his standard line at Berkshire annual meetings, delivered after Buffett had finished speaking, to laughter from the whole room.[3] A book that stays in print for 20 years usually survives on its sentences, not its system.

Chapter FiveWhat It Gets Right, and What It Misses

Three things are right. First, the ideas travel. The lattice, the circle of competence, inversion, incentives — they need no memorising; they surface on their own when a problem arrives. Second, the honesty. Munger does not pretend: he lost money, admitted mistakes, and said plainly how much he did not know. Third, the range. It treats investing as a by-product of thinking, so even readers who never buy a share can use it.

Three things miss. First, the system is scattered. Eleven talks, each standing alone; the lattice is built, but there is no manual: which problem calls for which model, when to switch tools — the book does not say. What the reader gets is a box of tools, not a set of working drawings.

Second, the examples age. The talks run from 1986 to 2007, and many of the companies, regulations, and market conditions in them are gone.[3] The ideas do not expire, but the examples do. Readers have to swap in examples of their own.

Third, survivorship bias. Munger's method worked for Munger, but the book has no control group: people who used the same method and failed do not appear in the talks. That is not Munger's fault — a successful person writing a book can only write about his own road. But readers should remember: the lattice lowers the odds of error; it does not guarantee success. Munger said it himself: opportunity does not come often, so when it does, seize it — and the other side of that sentence is that most of the time, all you can do is wait.[4]

One more reminder: the "common sense" in the title is not common at all. It asks the reader to go and read psychology, mathematics, biology, and history, and then hang the ideas on the lattice one by one. Munger spent 70 years building his. A weekend with the book buys only the catalogue of the lattice. That is not the book's flaw. It is its honesty — it never pretended that thinking could be learned in a hurry.

EpilogueCommon Sense, Not at All Common

Close the book, and what remains is not a method for picking stocks. It is a habit: when a problem arrives, ask how many models you hold; before deciding, ask which side the incentives are on; when stuck, turn the problem around.

Munger died at 99 having written no systematic work and founded no school. What he left is a collection of speeches compiled by someone else, and a habit — and one sentence he repeated for years: "In my whole life, I have known no wise people who didn't read all the time." Every page of this book is evidence for it.

Common sense is common because it sounds like something anyone could understand; it is uncommon because almost no one actually lives by it. Munger spent 70 years building his lattice. Finishing this book does not give you the lattice. It gives you a reason to start building one.

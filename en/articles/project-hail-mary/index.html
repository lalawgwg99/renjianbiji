Project Hail Mary: A Teacher with Amnesia, an Alien Spider, and a Dying Sun

Andy Weir's third novel, after The Martian: published by Ballantine on May 4, 2021, voted Goodreads' Best Science Fiction of 2021, and on the New York Times bestseller list for 57 weeks in total by July 2026. The film opened in March 2026 and sent the novel back onto the charts — number 40 on Kingstone's bestseller chart for the first half of 2026 in Taiwan. This deep read asks: how does hard science fiction keep readers who never read science fiction turning pages? And when one person cannot save the world, where does cooperation begin?

PrologueA Man Wakes Up Remembering Nothing

A man wakes up aboard a spaceship remembering nothing. Beside him are two dead bodies, and a robotic arm keeping watch over him like a nurse. He does not know who he is, why he is here, or that the ship under his feet is bound for a star a dozen light-years away.[4] He is Ryland Grace, a junior high school science teacher and former molecular biologist. And as he wakes, the Sun back on Earth is growing dimmer, little by little.[3][4]

This is Andy Weir's Project Hail Mary. The original was published by Ballantine on May 4, 2021, 496 pages;[1] the Traditional Chinese edition followed on February 25, 2022, from Suncolor, translated by Guo Tingxuan.[2] The film adaptation opened on March 20, 2026, starring Ryan Gosling;[4] the film-tie-in collector's edition reached number 40 on Kingstone's bestseller chart for the first half of 2026. This deep read asks three questions: how does hard science fiction keep readers who never read science fiction turning pages? Is the science in this book background, or is it the hero? And when one person cannot save the world, where does cooperation begin?

Chapter OneFrom Mars to the Sun

Andy Weir was born on June 16, 1972, in Davis, California. His father was a physicist at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory; his mother was an electrical engineer. At 15 he was hired as a computer programmer by Sandia National Laboratories, and as an adult he worked at AOL, Palm, and Blizzard, where he helped develop Warcraft II.[8] He grew up reading Arthur C. Clarke and Isaac Asimov. Before he was a novelist, he was a genuine space nerd and programmer.[8]

The Martian began as a free serial on his own website. Readers asked for a Kindle edition, so he put it up for 99 cents — and it climbed the Kindle bestseller list. Crown bought the rights, and the paperback debuted at number 12 on the New York Times bestseller list in 2014.[8] The film followed in 2015, starring Matt Damon, nominated for seven Academy Awards and grossing 630 million US dollars worldwide.[3] In 2016 Weir won the John W. Campbell Award for Best New Writer.[8] His second novel, Artemis, came out in 2017 and also won that year's Goodreads Choice Award for Best Science Fiction.[5]

Before Project Hail Mary, he started a multi-volume space opera called Zhek and abandoned it after 75,000 words.[4] But Zhek was not wasted: a substance that absorbs radiation and can fuel a starship, and a ruthless bureaucrat with near-unlimited power, both moved into the new book.[4] The title borrows from American football — the Hail Mary, a desperate long pass thrown when the game is nearly lost. The whole novel is one such pass: the Sun is going out, and humanity has one throw left.

Chapter TwoThe Sun Is Going Out

The story begins with an observation: the Sun is dimming at an exponential rate, and a faint infrared line runs from the Sun toward Venus. Its first observer was Dr. Irina Petrova, so it is called the Petrova line.[4] Left alone, it will bring a catastrophic ice age within 30 years;[4] the publisher's own copy puts it more bluntly — crop failure will push more than 3.5 billion people toward death.[3]

Grace is the man brought in to read the sample. He finds the line is made of single-celled organisms: they consume electromagnetic radiation and release it again, breeding on sunlight and carbon dioxide from Venus. He names them Astrophage — star-eaters.[4] The same organism has infected nearby stars, with one exception: Tau Ceti, a dozen light-years away, which has resisted infection.[4]

So there is a plan: build a starship fuelled by Astrophage and fly it to Tau Ceti for answers. There is fuel for one way only; the answers must come home aboard unmanned mini-ships. Grace trains the two chosen astronauts, but they die in an accident shortly before launch. With no time to train replacements, the mission's director, Eva Stratt, forces Grace aboard — sedated, carried onto the ship, and given a temporary amnesia drug so he will not sabotage the mission out of spite.[4] He is not a willing hero. He is a man strapped into a ship. That fact sets the tone of the whole book.

This is how Weir writes: science is not the background, it is the engine of the plot. Every turn grows out of a question in physics or biology — what Astrophage eats, how it breeds, why it spared Tau Ceti — and each answer Grace works out moves the story one step forward. Readers need no physics degree, because Grace works every step out on the blackboard for them. He is, after all, a teacher.

Chapter ThreeRocky

At Tau Ceti, Grace meets another ship. Its default radar name is Blip-A. It sends over a star-map sculpture showing it came from 40 Eridani, and once the ships dock, Grace meets its captain: an eyeless, spider-like, five-legged, rock-textured alien engineer. He names him Rocky.[4] Rocky has been at Tau Ceti for 46 Earth years; his crew died of radiation sickness, and he is the last one left.[4]

The two share no language and no biology. Even "hello" has to be invented from scratch. Weir spends long pages on how they learn to talk: mathematics first, then units, then names. It reads slowly, but it is the spine of the book — because the real hero of Project Hail Mary is neither Grace nor Rocky. It is communication itself.

Cooperation grows from there. The Eridians do not know relativity, so Blip-A carries enough fuel for both ships to go home;[4] what humans do not know, Rocky knows; what Rocky lacks, Grace supplies. Together they find, on a planet of Tau Ceti, a microbe that preys on Astrophage — a predator, and with it, the shape of a solution.[4] But solutions are never free: every step means touching alien physics with human tools, and repairing a human ship with alien materials.

Set it against The Martian and the difference is plain. Mark Watney is one man against one planet, surviving on knowledge, humour, and stubbornness. Grace starts out not even knowing who he is, and survives by asking another species for help. Weir has rewritten "going it alone" as "surviving together": one person can stay alive, but only two people together can save two worlds.

Chapter FourWhy It Took Off

First, the order of events: the book came five years before the film. The original was published on May 4, 2021, and debuted at number three on the New York Times bestseller list;[4] by the week ending July 11, 2026, it had spent 57 weeks on the list in total.[4] In 2021 it won the Goodreads Choice Award for Best Science Fiction with 92,831 votes — Weir's third win in a row in that category, after The Martian and Artemis.[5] The same year it won the Dragon Award for Best Science Fiction Novel; the next year it was a finalist for the Hugo Award for Best Novel, and the audiobook, read by Ray Porter, won the 2022 Audie Award for Audiobook of the Year.[4]

The reading lists and famous fans came early too: the 2021 Obama summer reading list and the Bill Gates holiday reading list.[3] Gates' recommendation is precise: "I'd recommend this book to anyone looking for a fun read. Even if you're not a science fiction fan, Project Hail Mary is a great story about two friends using science and engineering to save the world."[7] Reader numbers climbed with it: the 2022 Traditional Chinese edition cites 190,000 Goodreads ratings; by the 2026 collector's edition that figure had become nearly 850,000, with 150,000 ratings on Amazon.[3][6]

The film was the second wave. It opened on March 20, 2026: Ryan Gosling starring and producing, Sandra Hüller alongside him, James Ortiz voicing Rocky, Drew Goddard writing the screenplay, Phil Lord and Christopher Miller directing.[4][9] Once the film opened, sales of the novel climbed again, reaching the top 10 in Australia;[10] in Taiwan, a bundle of the film-tie-in collector's edition with The Martian was published on February 5, 2026,[6] and the collector's edition reached number 40 on Kingstone's bestseller chart for the first half of the year.

So it did not go viral on a single event. The book held its ground first on word of mouth and reading lists, prizes and long sales followed, and the film brought the same readers — plus new ones — back to the bookshop. Get the order right, and the amplifier works.

Chapter FiveWhat It Gets Right, What It Misses

Three things are right. First, the science is the plot, not the decoration. Every property of Astrophage — what it eats, how it breeds, what it fears — decides directly what the hero can and cannot do next, and readers calculate along with him, get it wrong with him, and recalculate. Second, the hero is no hero. Grace is afraid, he refuses, he works out a way to live while shaking at the blackboard. He was forced aboard; he did not volunteer to die. That gives weight to every choice he makes. Third, Rocky is genuinely alien, not a human in a costume. His body, his language, his physical intuition are all different from ours, and the reasons the two need each other are written into their physiology, not just their dialogue.

Some things miss. First, the science is dense. The derivations link one to the next, and midway through, readers in a hurry start skipping pages — but this is a book that does not allow skipping; skip, and you lose the thread. Second, the double track of amnesia and flashback means that by the middle, careful readers can roughly guess why Grace is on the ship, and the suspense thins. Third, the Washington Post review put it plainly: some of the crises in the book could have been avoided with common sense and checklists — and spaceflight is, after all, an industry that stays alive on checklists.[4]

One more note: watching the film before reading the book, and reading the book before watching the film, are two different experiences. A film can only take the novel's skeleton; 496 pages hold the derivations and the conversations.[1] Those who saw the film will keep putting Gosling's face on Grace; those who read first will keep comparing the screen with the Rocky they imagined. Neither is better. But they are different.

EpilogueWhat Coming Home Means

The title carries two meanings. A Hail Mary is the desperate long pass in American football: the game nearly over, no way back, the ball thrown forward on a prayer that it lands in the right hands. The Hail Mary mission is exactly that pass — fuel for one way, answers to come home aboard small ships, and once thrown, no taking it back.

But "coming home" carries a second meaning. When Grace sets out, home means Earth. By the end, the question is not whether he can get home, but what he brings home with him — and what he is willing to pay to bring it. The book never answers that in a single sentence. It lets Grace answer it with a whole voyage.

Close the book, and what remains is not an answer but a feeling: science can be cold and the calculations hard, but what carries a reader through 496 pages is two people — a human and an alien — teaching each other how to stay alive in the dark.[1] Whether the Sun goes out is a question of physics. Whether to risk everything for another world is a question of being human. This book makes the two questions into one story.

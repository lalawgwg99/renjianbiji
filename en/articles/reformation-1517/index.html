The Reformation: How One Sheet of Paper Tore a World Apart

On October 31, 1517, a 34-year-old monk posted 95 theses in Wittenberg. Nobody expected the printing press to turn an academic debate into a revolution that split Europe.

SlowFolio Editorial Team · Reading time 12 min

The Same Era · 1517

  • 1517In the same year, the Ottoman Empire captured Cairo, ended the Mamluk Sultanate, and absorbed Egypt and Syria. In the very year Luther posted his theses, the greatest power of the Islamic world pushed one great step further west.

PrologueA Door

October 31, 1517, the eve of All Saints' Day. Crowds moved through the doors of the Castle Church in Wittenberg — tomorrow the church's great collection of relics would go on display, and pilgrims had come from everywhere. A 34-year-old professor of theology walked up and pinned several sheets of Latin to the door. It was the university's noticeboard; professors posted topics for debate there all the time, inviting colleagues to argue. His name was Martin Luther. Nobody thought the day was special.[1]

The sheets were the Ninety-five Theses, and the subject was indulgences. Luther was, in fact, restrained: he did not deny the pope, he did not call for revolt. He asked a series of questions — if the pope truly had the power to release souls from purgatory, why not release them all out of love? Is something a poor man buys with money really more reliable than repentance itself?[1][2]

Within weeks the Latin academic document had been translated into German, printed as a pamphlet, and spread across Germany; within months, across Europe.[2] A debate became a revolution. Later generations made October 31, 1517 the birthday of the Reformation. But all Luther wanted that day was a debate. What really changed the world was not the paper — it was two things behind it: a machine, and an age that could not go back.

Chapter 1The Price of an Indulgence

To understand the 95 theses, first understand how the price of an indulgence was calculated.

The Church's doctrine went like this: after death, even forgiven sins still had to be punished in purgatory; buying an indulgence could shorten that punishment for yourself or for a dead relative. By 1517 the doctrine had become a business. Rome was rebuilding St. Peter's Basilica — begun under the previous pope, Julius II, and continued under the reigning pope, Leo X — and the money was never enough.[7]

The sales agent for Germany was Albrecht, Archbishop of Mainz. When he took the post in 1514, he had borrowed a large sum from the Fugger banking family to pay Rome for the appointment. The way to repay it was to sell indulgences: half the revenue went back to Rome, half went to the Fuggers. Every friar hawking indulgences had a Fugger collection agent walking beside him.[7] Pope, archbishop, bankers — the split was clear and agreed.

On the front line was the Dominican friar Johann Tetzel. He is said to have had a slogan he shouted wherever he went: "As soon as the coin in the coffer rings, the soul from purgatory springs." Whether he actually said it, no one can now prove; but it was accurate in a terrible way — grace had a price list, and salvation could be bought on the spot.[2]

Germans had felt something was wrong for a long time. Germany was not yet a country, but hundreds of territories under the Holy Roman Empire, and Rome kept drawing money out of them. Some called Germany "the milk cow of Rome" — an animal kept only to be milked.[7] Luther's anger was half theology, half this.

More importantly, Luther sat in the confessional every day. He heard parishioners ask: if I buy an indulgence, do I still need to repent? He saw a piece of paper replacing a human heart. Thesis 1 says exactly this: "When our Lord and Master Jesus Christ said 'Repent', he willed that the whole life of believers should be repentance."[1] Thesis 1 was not aimed at the pope. It was aimed at the whole logic of the transaction: if repentance is a lifelong matter, it cannot be bought outright with a piece of paper.

Chapter 2The Press Ran Faster Than Theology

Luther posted the theses and went home to wait for a debating opponent. The opponent never came. The printers did.

By 1517 Gutenberg's movable type had been in use for more than 60 years, and print shops stood all over Germany. The theses were written in Latin, which few people read; but someone translated them into German, printed them as a thin pamphlet, and sold them for pennies. People who could read bought copies and read them aloud in taverns and marketplaces to those who could not. Within weeks the theses had spread across Germany; within months, across Europe.[2] An authority built over centuries met, for the first time, a speed it could not catch: the speed at which ideas could be copied.

Rome's response was not slow — it just backfired at every step. In June 1520 the pope issued the bull Exsurge Domine, giving Luther 60 days to retract or face excommunication. Luther answered on December 10, 1520, by burning the bull, together with volumes of canon law, in front of the whole university at Wittenberg. In January 1521 the pope formally excommunicated him. Every condemnation became advertising for the next batch of pamphlets.[4]

In between came the Leipzig Debate of 1519. His opponents forced Luther to answer: can a Church council err? Luther said yes. Once spoken, there was no going back — if councils can err and popes can err, then the only authority that cannot err is Scripture itself. Later generations called this position sola scriptura, Scripture alone. Luther had not set out to reach this point; the debate pushed him there.[3]

In 1520 Luther wrote three pamphlets in a single burst: To the Christian Nobility of the German Nation, The Babylonian Captivity of the Church, and The Freedom of a Christian. Together they stated his full case: every believer is a priest, able to face God without going through the clergy; a person is justified by faith alone, not by good works or indulgences; and the standard of right and wrong is Scripture alone, not pope or council.[3] Each of those three claims knocked out one pillar of the Church's authority.

The printing press changed more than speed; it changed the shape of power. Before, the Church monopolised the interpretation of what was true, because books were scarce, few could read, and the channels of interpretation belonged to the clergy. The press made books cheap, readers multiplied, and interpretation acquired competitors for the first time. Luther did not invent the printing press, but he was the first to truly understand how to use it.

Chapter 3The Words at Worms

April 1521, Worms. The new Holy Roman Emperor, Charles V, summoned an imperial diet and called Luther before it to ask him one question: will you retract?

Luther stood before the emperor, the electors and the archbishops, his books piled on a table. The chairman told him to answer only yes or no — no debate. Luther asked for a day to consider, came back the next day, and said, in essence: unless I am proven wrong by Scripture or by clear reason, I cannot and will not retract a single word.[4]

Tradition adds one more line, supposedly spoken there: "Here I stand, I can do no other." Whether those were his actual words on the day is disputed by historians — the contemporary record of the diet does not contain them; they appeared later. But with or without the line, the outcome was the same: in May 1521 the emperor issued the Edict of Worms, declaring Luther an outlaw of the empire, banning his writings, and forbidding anyone to shelter him on pain of the same punishment.[4]

On the way home, an "accident" happened: a troop of soldiers "kidnapped" him. It was a staged abduction arranged by Frederick, Elector of Saxony, to hide him. The soldiers took him to the Wartburg, a castle on a hill, where Luther lived for a year under the false name "Knight George", growing a beard, in a small room in the castle.[4]

He did not waste the year. At the Wartburg he translated the New Testament from Greek into German, published in September 1522 as the "September Testament". For the first time, Germans could read the Bible in their own language — no need to wait for a priest to explain it, no need to know Latin. A farmer who knew a few letters could judge for himself whether the priest was right. The monopoly on authority began to leak.[4]

Chapter 4After the Fire Caught

Luther wanted to repair the Church. The fire went somewhere he had not expected.

In 1524 the German peasants rose. They had read Luther's "freedom of a Christian" and understood it to mean: if all are free before God, why must we still be serfs on earth, paying crushing taxes? In 1525 the revolt swept southern Germany, and the peasants issued the Twelve Articles, demanding lower taxes and the end of serfdom. Luther first sympathised, then publicly condemned them in Against the Murderous, Thieving Hordes of Peasants, siding with the princes. When the war was put down, tens of thousands of peasants were dead, and reform and revolution parted ways for good.[3]

The turn exposed the other face of the Reformation: the princes' support was never only about faith. A prince who backed Luther could seize Church property, escape Rome's jurisdiction, and turn the Church into his own instrument — all with a clear conscience. Faith gave politics a just cause; politics gave faith a shield. Each side got what it needed. That is why the reform survived, and also why it soured.

In 1530 Luther's colleague Philip Melanchthon drafted the Augsburg Confession and read it before the imperial diet — the first systematic statement of what the Lutherans believed. The emperor rejected it, and both sides built military alliances. More than 20 years of conflict followed, until in 1555 both sides signed the Peace of Augsburg, agreeing on one rule: cuius regio, eius religio — the ruler's religion is the religion of the land.[3][4] In a single sentence, Christendom was formally split in two, and religion became an attribute of the state. What a person believed was no longer decided by his conscience, but by his prince.

The Catholic side was not idle either. In 1545 the Council of Trent convened and sat, with interruptions, for 18 years — reaffirming Catholic doctrine while cleaning up corruption inside the Church. In 1540 the Society of Jesus was approved by the pope and became the most effective force of the Counter-Reformation.[3] Europe now had two Christian worlds, hostile to each other, competing, neither willing to blink first.

Chapter 5The World After the Split

The deepest thing the Reformation left the world was not Protestantism. It was the split itself.

The first thing was the individual conscience. Before the reform, faith was collective: you were born into a parish, entered its church, and lived by the same rites. Luther said every believer is a priest, each person able to face God directly. Faith became a personal matter — you must read for yourself, think for yourself, answer for yourself. It took centuries for the full force of that sentence to be released: the modern habit of "I will judge for myself" starts here.

The second thing was literacy. If everyone must read the Bible, everyone must learn to read. Protestant regions built schools, printed textbooks, and taught children to read and write, and literacy rates slowly climbed. A society that can read handles problems very differently from one that cannot. The printing press gave people books; the Reformation gave them a reason to read.

The third thing was the state. Cuius regio, eius religio handed the power to decide religion to the monarch, who for the first time fully owned the principle of "my land, my rule". In 1648 the Peace of Westphalia ended the Thirty Years' War and wrote that rule into the international order: sovereign states, no interference in each other's domestic affairs. Many of the borders on the modern map of the world began to be drawn from here.[3]

But one thing must be said plainly: the Reformation did not bring tolerance. In 16th-century Europe, Protestants persecuted Catholics, Catholics persecuted Protestants, and both persecuted the Anabaptists. Tolerance was not a product of the reform; it was a product of its failure — after more than a hundred years of war, nobody could win, and people slowly learned to live together. The split itself first destroyed the idea that there could be only one truth and one authority; tolerance grew out of the ruins centuries later.

EpilogueThe Door Is Still There

The original wooden doors of the Castle Church in Wittenberg burned in the fires of war in 1760. In 1858 they were replaced with bronze doors, engraved with the full Latin text of the Ninety-five Theses. The door is still there, and the theses are still engraved on it.[2]

Luther died in 1546 and never saw the Peace of Augsburg. All his life he said he only wanted to repair the Church; instead he broke it apart. In his later years he wrote vicious attacks on the Jews — the other side of the man, which no history of the reform can hide for him. One person can advance liberation and spread hatred at the same time; history never undertakes to wash anyone clean.

But on October 31, 1517, the 34-year-old monk standing at the door had something simple in mind: some things, if never said aloud, will never be known to be wrong. What he posted was not a manifesto; it was a poster inviting a debate. The debate never happened. The revolution did. More than 500 years later, the door is still there, and so is the question: when authority demands that you take back a true sentence, will you stand?

If Luther had not posted that paper that day, would the Reformation still have happened? Or had the printing press, the Fugger ledgers, and the German princes' resentment already piled up the gunpowder, waiting for someone to light it?

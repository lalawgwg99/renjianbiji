The Renaissance: How a City That Lost Half Its People Invented the Future

The Black Death of 1348, a competition in 1401, a dome in 1436 — 'rebirth' was not a return to the past, but a group of people deciding to stop waiting for God to save them

PrologueA City Dies by Half First

In 1348, the Black Death walked into Florence. Ten years earlier, the city had held some 90,000 to 120,000 people, one of the richest cities in Europe; after the plague, its population had fallen by more than half, and a tax survey in 1352 counted only about 25,000.[6] The chronicler Matteo Villani wrote that between April and September of 1348, three in every five people died.[6]

The plague did not bring the Renaissance. It brought empty houses, inheritances with no heirs, and a feeling that was hard to say out loud: God had not come to save anyone. The Church called it divine punishment and told people to repent; but the repentant died anyway, the priests died anyway, and so did the people who prayed for the dead. When an explanation can no longer explain what is happening in front of you, people start looking for their own answers.

The Renaissance did not fall from the sky. It was built, brick by brick, out of the ruins of a city. This is not the "rebirth" of the art-history textbook. It is about three questions underneath it: what must a society lose before it starts believing in itself? Whose hands must the money reach before art walks down off the church walls? And when people discover for the first time that they can decide their own future, what is the first thing they want to do?

Chapter OneTen Young People, One Hundred Stories

In 1348, at the height of the plague, Giovanni Boccaccio began writing the Decameron. The premise is simple: seven ladies and three young men flee Florence for a country villa, and over ten days they tell ten stories a day — one hundred stories in all.[5]

These stories are not about God or the afterlife. They are about merchants, monks, wives, tricksters and lovers — about people. Boccaccio's friend Petrarch (1304–1374) went further: from the manuscripts of ancient Rome he recovered an attitude that had been forgotten — that a person is worth taking seriously, not because he is God's creature, but because he is a person.[5] Later generations called this attitude humanism. It did not begin in a university. It began after a plague, when ten young people decided to survive first, and then to think about how to live.

The Decameron was written around 1349 and completed in 1353.[5] It is not a manifesto of the Renaissance, but it is the first piece of evidence: when the old explanations fail, people write a new story to explain the world themselves.

Chapter TwoThe Competition for a Door

In the winter of 1401, Florence's cloth merchants' guild announced a competition: a new pair of bronze doors for the Baptistery.[6] Seven sculptors entered, all given the same subject: a relief of the Sacrifice of Isaac. Lorenzo Ghiberti won. The man he beat was Filippo Brunelleschi.[11]

Two things about this competition are worth remembering. First, the winner was judged by merchants of the guild, not by the Church and not by a king — art had a market for the first time, and buyers had a choice for the first time. Second, Brunelleschi, the loser, did not leave Florence. He turned to study something nobody had yet managed to do: a dome that could not be built.

Ghiberti's north doors took more than twenty years; they were hung in 1424, twenty-eight panels telling stories from the New Testament.[11][12] As soon as they were finished, the guild gave him the east doors. Those took twenty-seven years, from 1425 to 1452: ten square panels from the Old Testament, with a perspective and depth no one had seen before.[8]

"They are worthy to be the gates of Paradise."
— Michelangelo, on Ghiberti's east doors

When Michelangelo saw the doors, he said they were worthy to be the gates of Paradise — and the name "Gates of Paradise" stuck.[7] One pair of doors took nearly half a century. That looks inefficient today; at the time it was normal: Florence's rich did not buy ready-made things. They bought time — a person's willingness to spend a lifetime on a single task.

Chapter ThreeA Dome Nobody Could Build

Florence Cathedral had been under construction since 1296. After more than a century of work, by 1418 only one problem remained: the octagonal hole above the crossing, 45.5 metres across — who could put a roof on it?[1] Nobody in Europe knew the answer. The hole was too wide; traditional wooden scaffolding could not hold the weight, and the bricks would collapse before the dome was finished.

In the competition of 1418, Brunelleschi offered an answer: a double-shelled dome, bricks laid in a herringbone pattern, iron chains and stone rings hidden inside, so that the dome supported itself as it rose, with no scaffolding at all.[1] He was a goldsmith by training, with no formal education in architecture; what he had was a study of ancient Roman building.[1] Work began on August 7, 1420, and finished on August 30, 1436; on March 25 of that year, Pope Eugene IV consecrated the cathedral himself, and the Flemish composer Guillaume Dufay wrote a motet for the occasion, performed at the ceremony.[2]

The dome is still the largest brick dome ever built, about 116 metres high.[1][2] But its real meaning was not its size. For more than a century, the people of Florence had looked up every day at an unfinished hole — a public failure that everyone knew about. Putting a dome on it was a announcement to the whole city: something no one has ever done is not the same as something that cannot be done.

Brunelleschi did not live to see the lantern finished on top — it was installed in 1471, and he died in 1446.[2] He did not need to. On March 25, 1436, the whole city had already seen it.

Chapter FourThe Banker's Ledger

The Renaissance was not started by the poor. It was bought by bankers. In 1397, Giovanni di Bicci de' Medici founded the Medici Bank in Florence, built on the papacy's business of moving money — bishops all over Europe paid their fees to Rome through his branches.[3]

His son Cosimo turned the money into power. In September 1433, his rivals had him arrested and exiled; in October 1434, he came back, and from then on he was the effective ruler of Florence until his death on August 1, 1464.[3][4]

Cosimo spent more than 600,000 gold florins on art and learning in his lifetime.[4] Donatello's bronze David — the first free-standing male nude since antiquity — was his commission, made around 1440.[4] He paid to restore churches, build libraries and keep scholars, not because he was especially noble, but because his arithmetic was better than other people's: in a republic, money cannot buy a throne, but it can buy a reputation — and a reputation is another kind of power.

This is the least romantic, and most honest, side of the Renaissance: great art needs great buyers. Without the Medici Bank, there would be no frescoes in the Brancacci Chapel, no David, no golden orb on top of the dome. Behind the history of art, there is a ledger.

Ledgers, of course, can be miscalculated. In 1494, King Charles VIII of France marched south into Italy, and the Florentines used the moment to throw the Medici out of the city; ninety-seven years of banking collapsed with them.[3] But the money scattered and the things remained: the paintings, the statues, the dome — still standing over Florence today.

Chapter FiveFitting the World into One Eye

In 1435, Leon Battista Alberti wrote Brunelleschi's method down as a book: De pictura (On Painting). It was the first surviving European treatise on painting, and its first book is the geometry of perspective: how to use a single vanishing point to fit a three-dimensional world onto a flat wall.[12]

Masaccio was the first to understand it. Around 1425, in the Brancacci Chapel, he painted the Tribute Money: Christ and the apostles standing in a real space, mountains, buildings and people each in their place. For the first time, a picture had depth — not symbolic depth, but depth the eye could see.

Perspective changed more than painting. It changed how people saw the world: the world was no longer a flat plane seen from above by God, but a scene seen by a person standing somewhere, with their own eyes. For the first time, the painter left a place in the picture — the viewer's place. Where you stand is where the world begins.

By the middle of the fifteenth century, Gutenberg's movable type (invented around 1440; the first Bible printed around 1455 in Mainz) let books be reproduced in batches for the first time.[13] Ideas no longer belonged only to the monks who copied them. The Renaissance stopped being one city's affair and became Europe's.

EpilogueRebirth Is Not a Return

In 1550, Giorgio Vasari gave this movement a name in his Lives of the Artists: rinascita — rebirth.[14] The word is a little misleading.

The people of the Renaissance did not want to go back to the past. They read ancient Rome not to copy it, but to prove something: that people had done it once, and people could do it again. The ancients were evidence, not a destination.

After 1494, Florence's good days ended: the Medici were expelled, the friar Savonarola took power and burned luxuries and paintings in the "Bonfire of the Vanities"; within a few years, he was executed himself. The Renaissance did not end well. It never promised to.

Questions Without Answers Yet

To be honest, the following two things still have no reliable answer, and this article will not pretend otherwise:

  • How exactly did Brunelleschi build the dome? He left no complete working drawings or calculations; later generations can only work backwards from scattered records and the dome itself, and the details are still disputed.
  • The exact year Donatello's bronze David was cast is still unsettled — scholarly dates range from the 1430s to the 1450s, and the "around 1440" used here is only one common estimate.

But it left behind a habit: when the old answers fail, build new ones by hand. On March 25, 1436, the people of Florence looked up and saw a finished dome — not a miracle, but sixteen years of work, one brick at a time. From that day on, the word "impossible" lost a little of its magic in Europe. And that little was enough, in time, to change everything.

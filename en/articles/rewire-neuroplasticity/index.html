Rewire: Your Brain Is Not Concrete — It Is a Tangle of Wires That Can Be Reconnected

A 2024 British neuroscience bestseller won Taiwan over with one sentence: your brain can be rewired. This deep read takes apart its three-phase programme — and asks how much of it is actually new.

PrologueA Book That Begins with an Anxious Age

On May 9, 2024, the British publisher Michael Joseph released a neuroscience self-help book called Rewire, by a neuroscientist then little known to the general public, Nicole Vignola.[2] A month later, the Traditional Chinese edition appeared from Rye Field Publishing, translated by Liang Yung-an, the paperback released on June 1, 2024, under the title Rewire: Neuroplasticity.[4] Two years later the book ranked number 3 on Eslite's 2026 annual bestseller list,[8] and as of the end of October 2026 it was still on Books.com.tw's bestseller chart — the bookstore's 2025 annual reading report said its sales had been driven by recommendations from readers on Threads.[11]

Its timing was no accident. Readers in Taiwan in the 2020s are collectively learning a new word: neihau — the exhaustion of burning energy inward. Work, family, and social feeds, all burning at once; anxiety, panic, and depression, become daily realities many dare not name. This book's promise lands exactly on that pain: your brain is not concrete but a tangle of wires that can be reconnected — and science has already worked out how.

Chapter OneThe Brain Is Hardware; Thoughts Are Software

The book's central metaphor is a single sentence: the brain is hardware; thoughts, memories, and habits are software. The hardware ships as it is, but the software can be rewritten. Nicole Vignola holds a BSc in neuroscience from the University of Bristol and an MSc in organisational psychology from the University of the West of England; her undergraduate research focused on synaptic plasticity — the ability of neurons to remodel their connections.[4] She later became an organisational consultant, and explains brain science to the public on Instagram and Substack under the name nicolesneuroscience.

The crucial point about plasticity is that it runs both ways. The brain can grow new connections and shed old ones — you can learn new responses and unlearn old ones. The book cites a well-known piano imagery experiment: two groups of volunteers, one actually practising the piano and one only imagining playing it, showed similar patterns of brain activity.[3] Imagination is not daydreaming; imagining itself rewires the brain. That is why the author dares to write: "You can, if you so wish, create yourself. Whoever you want to be."[5]

But that sentence carries a condition, which the book states honestly: rewiring is not a miracle, it is practice. Plasticity gives you the possibility of change, not a guarantee. The three phases that follow are the training schedule the book prescribes.

Chapter TwoWhy the Brain Always Expects the Worst

Phase one is called "Ditch the Negative." It starts from an uncomfortable fact: the brain is built to prefer negative information. This is the negativity bias, a survival mechanism left by evolution — on the savanna, missing one danger could be fatal, while imagining one bad outcome too many merely costs sleep. The problem is that modern brains still run on savanna settings while living office lives: a supervisor's remark, a message left on read, someone else's polished life on social media all get processed as threats to survival.

The author's solution is not "think positive" but concrete tools. The book offers a "neuroscience toolkit": noticing when the negativity bias is running, interrupting the loop that rehearses the worst outcome, and processing loss and grief instead of walking around them. The toolkit also includes the physiological sigh — a breathing method introduced in the book, described here only as a tool the book presents, without endorsing its efficacy. What matters is not any single tool but the sequence: you can only break a loop you have first seen.

Most worth noting in this chapter is the author's attitude toward "the power of thought." She does not deny that positive thinking helps, but she grounds it in mechanism: thoughts have power not because the universe answers your wishes, but because thoughts that recur strengthen the neural connections behind them. In other words, you do not change the world with intention; you change the brain with repetition.

Chapter ThreeWhat Is Inside the Toolbox

Phase two, "Shift Your Narrative," is the longest part of the book. The author's view is that we are shaped not only by events but by the stories we tell ourselves: "That is just who I am," "I will never manage it," "Everyone is better than me." Once those sentences are repeated long enough, they become the brain's default pathways. Shifting the narrative means taking a different road.

The seven steps of this phase run in order: rewiring the subconscious, putting down the phone, imagination and attention, repetition, leaving blank space, pushing past boundaries, strategy and setbacks, and moving through fear and self-sabotage.[3] The most interesting among them are "putting down the phone" and "leaving blank space": the author argues that a brain without rest and empty time cannot lay down new wiring — it needs quiet hours to consolidate new connections. Imagination and attention echo the piano experiment from chapter one: where you place your attention, the connections grow.

Phase three, "Boost the Positive," covers psychological resilience, a growth mindset, sleep, dopamine, and building confidence. The author ranks sleep as the number one tool to optimise — not meditation, not exercise, but sleep. That ranking is itself a statement: the most effective way to rewire the brain is often not learning one more technique but doing the most basic things well.

The book also says that walking in a neuroscience-informed way can reduce anxiety, and that being in nature and open space can help shrink the amygdala.[3] The attribution matters here: this is the book's claim, and this article does not independently vouch for the research behind it.

Chapter FourWhy It Became a Hit

The numbers first. The English edition was first published on May 9, 2024, with the HarperOne ebook following on June 4, 2024;[2] the Traditional Chinese paperback appeared on June 1, 2024, and the ebook on May 30, 2024, priced at NT$420.[4] The book reached number 1 on Amazon UK's neuropsychology chart, launched simultaneously in Taiwan and the United States,[4] and has been licensed in more than a dozen languages.[7] On Goodreads it holds 4.17 stars from more than 2,400 ratings.[2] By 2026 it was number 3 on Eslite's annual bestseller list.[8]

Why did it sell? First, it translates difficult brain science into plain language. The author built her audience explaining neuroscience on Instagram, so she knows how to make synaptic plasticity intelligible to non-specialists. One Goodreads reader writes that the book's greatest strength is how it bridges science and everyday life without reading like a textbook, in a way that makes you stop and think, "So that is what that is."[10]

Second, it landed on the era's mood of neihau. The November 2026 issue of Manager Today monthly built its cover story around the book, saying it answers readers' need to "repair burnout and settle their emotions," and that its sales show the subject touches readers' hearts.[11] In an anxious age, a book that tells you "the brain can change" is itself a form of comfort.

Third, the author brought her own audience. With large followings on Instagram and Substack, her readers were the book's first buyers. The authority of neuroscience plus the approachability of social media gave the book the two things a bestseller most wants: credibility, and shareability.

Chapter FiveWhere It Promises Too Much

But the more popular the book, the fairer it is to ask: how much of it is actually new? The most common criticism on Goodreads is a single sentence: "nothing really novel."[10] Sleep, meditation, exercise, putting down the phone — all of it has been written in earlier self-help books; this one merely dresses the same advice in neuroscience. One reader put it more bluntly: the book feels rushed, as if written quickly to ride the hype of a growing Instagram following, loosely organised and under-edited.

A criticism that deserves to be taken more seriously comes from neurodivergent readers. One writes that the author repeats "you have more choice than you think," yet for neurodivergent people many of the book's first-half recommendations — controlling emotional reactions, rewriting narratives by will — are precisely the hardest things to do. Treating "you have a choice" as a universal rule, while ignoring differences between brains, is not a scientific conclusion but an optimistic inference.

Two details are also worth knowing. First, the book contains a passage on self-hypnosis that many readers found jarring — one writes that she has never believed in hypnosis and found that section simply baffling. Second, the author's degrees are a bachelor's plus a master's, not a doctorate, yet some promotional copy calls her "Dr."[2] That is not the author's fault, but readers should know it: her strength is translation and communication, not an academic title.

None of these criticisms makes the book useless, but they draw a line: this is an excellent popular-science self-help book, not a neuroscience monograph. Its value lies in getting people to start, not in every sentence carrying an experiment behind it.

Questions Without Answers Yet

To be honest, the following two things still have no reliable answers, and this article will not pretend otherwise:

  • Actual sales of the Traditional Chinese edition: the Eslite chart publishes rankings, not numbers, and the publisher has not disclosed how many copies sold in Taiwan, so the book's real sales in Taiwan cannot be checked.
  • The original details of individual experiments in the book: for example, the sample size, design, and publication source of the piano imagery experiment have not been checked against the original papers here; this article reports them as the book and the author's public writing present them.

EpilogueRewiring Is a Slow Business

When the book is closed, what should remain is not any single tool but a quieter idea: the brain is not concrete, but rewiring is not magic either. New connections need repetition, sleep, blank space, and time. Methods that promise speed sell comfort; real change is slow enough to try your patience.

The most honest sentence in the book is hidden in its subtitle: break the cycle, alter your thoughts, and create lasting change. The weight falls on the last word: lasting. Not one epiphany, not an overnight rebirth, but returning your attention, again and again, to the person you want to become. The brain will catch up — if you give it enough time.

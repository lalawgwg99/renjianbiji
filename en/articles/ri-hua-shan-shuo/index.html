Sunlight in Bloom: One Hundred Taiwanese Words That Name Light, Home, and Farewell

Wen Jo-chiao's collection, published by China Times on January 6, 2026: 100 Taiwanese words, written into 100 poems. It went viral on Threads, reached the top 30 of the 2026 first-half bestseller lists, and was read aloud by the President on International Mother Language Day. This deep read asks: how did a language dismissed as vulgar produce poetry this elegant — and after reading it, will we start speaking Taiwanese?

PrologueA Word That Fell Through the Leaves

On January 6, 2026, China Times Publishing released a 288-page collection of poetry: Sunlight in Bloom: A Hundred Beautiful Taiwanese Words & One Hundred Poems, by Wen Jo-chiao, born in 1999 in Tamsui.[1][2] The word in the title, ji̍t-hue, is a Taiwanese word for the light that falls through gaps in clouds or leaves and lands on the ground like scattered petals. Older generations used to say "chhut ji̍t-hue-á" — the sun is flowering. It was this island's beautiful name for a gift of light.[2]

The book began with a trip. Walking in a park in Japan, Wen saw afternoon sunlight filtering through leaves and falling on grey pavement in flickers. The Japanese word komorebi came to mind. Only later did she learn that Taiwanese has its own word for this — ji̍t-hue — and that its meaning is broader and more concrete: not only leaves, but gaps in clouds, gaps between coloured panes of glass, light falling on a tabletop. All of it is sunlight in bloom.[3] From that day on, her world had one more kind of light in it.

What she did next was simple and stubborn: from the 1931 Taiwanese-Japanese Dictionary, the Ministry of Education's Taiwanese dictionary, and everyday speech, she chose 100 Taiwanese words and wrote one poem for each, arranged into 11 sections. Every poem appears in three parallel versions — recommended characters, Tâi-lô romanisation, and a Mandarin translation — and every poem comes with a recording of the author reading it aloud.[2] This deep read asks: how did a language dismissed as "vulgar" produce poetry this elegant? And after reading it, will we start speaking Taiwanese?

Chapter OneIt Went Viral Because It Gave a Mother Tongue Back to Daily Life

This book did not become famous in bookstores. It became famous on phone screens. Wen runs the Taiwanese-language community "ohtaigi" on Instagram and Threads, with 100,000 Instagram followers; her Threads series on "beautiful words found only in Taiwanese" gathered readers one post at a time before being collected into a book.[2] A poem is short. One word, one poem — exactly the length a social feed can finish, share, and save.

The numbers are clear. In the first half of 2026, among the 34 titles on Eslite and Books.com.tw's top-30 bestseller lists, Sunlight in Bloom ranked third in online buzz, with 4,484 mentions — behind only Taiwan Travelogue and The Almanack of Naval Ravikant.[4] Around International Mother Language Day on February 21, 2026, discussion surged: that day, President Lai Ching-te shared the book on social media and read one of its poems aloud, saying he had bought it at the Taipei International Book Exhibition, and the post was widely shared.[4]

The first reason it caught on is timing. Taiwan has talked about mother tongues for years, but mostly as policy, curriculum, and preservation — serious topics, far from daily life. This book pulls the mother tongue out of policy and back into life: one word is an afternoon's light, a grandmother's back, a sentence never spoken. Readers do not need to agree with any position first. They only need to recognise the light.

The second reason is form. Poetry collections usually sell poorly, and Taiwanese poetry collections even more so. But this book removes the barriers: if you cannot read Taiwanese script, there is a Mandarin translation; if you cannot pronounce it, there is an audio recording; if you wonder where a word comes from, the source is spelled out. Someone who has never read a Taiwanese poem can start on page one.

Chapter TwoOne Hundred Words, One Hundred Poems

The 100 poems are arranged into 11 sections: Scenery, Home, Parting, Companionship, Regret, Longing, Gratitude, Hope, Realisation, Bitterness, and Sweetness. From landscape to home, from farewell to companionship, from regret to sweetness — 11 words that hold the folds of a 26-year-old life, felt or once felt.[2][3]

Every poem works the same way: first a word, then a poem. The words come from three places: old dictionaries, the Ministry of Education's Taiwanese dictionary, and everyday words whose beauty only shows itself when you stop and think about them.[3] Take the first poem in the Scenery section, "Thiah-ha̍h-kng" — the light of split bamboo: the morning sky peeling away the night like a bamboo sheath, the faint dawn breaking out of the dark.[3] One word, one short poem, three scripts, one voice. What the reader gets is not only a poem but the whole history of a word.

The cleverest thing about this design is that it does two jobs at once. For people who already speak Taiwanese, this is a poetry collection: words heard since childhood, never imagined as poetry, suddenly given literary shape. For people who do not speak Taiwanese, it is a beginner's book: three parallel versions let readers learn by comparison — the Mandarin translation is a bridge, Tâi-lô romanisation a crutch for pronunciation, the audio recording the final check. One book, two kinds of readers, both welcome.

Even the author's preface comes in three versions: Taiwanese characters, Mandarin, and Tâi-lô. That is not decoration. It is a statement: for a language to live, it cannot have only one written form. It must be readable, speakable, and passable-on by people at different levels.

Chapter ThreeWhy Was Taiwanese Called "Vulgar"?

In her preface, Wen tells her own story. Born in 1999 in the north of Taiwan, she grew up living with her grandparents. Taiwanese and Mandarin were both languages she heard from childhood. But no one at home required her to speak Taiwanese, and school did not encourage it, so her Taiwanese never became more than a tenth as fluent as her Mandarin.[3] Her question is direct: why should languages have different ranks? Why should the language her grandmother spoke all her life be treated by the younger generation as less worth learning than a foreign language? Some say Taiwanese is by nature coarse and vulgar. Is that true?[3]

People in Taiwan know this question well. For decades after the war, schools and public life promoted Mandarin, and local languages were pushed into corners. A whole generation grew up with Taiwanese as "the language you speak to Grandma"; the next generation could barely understand it. A language does not disappear overnight. It disappears one unspoken sentence at a time, one switch to Mandarin at a time, until someone turns around and finds that many words are no longer used by anyone, surviving only as dictionary examples.

Wen's answer is not protest. It is poetry. She did not write a long essay arguing whether Taiwanese is elegant. She chose 100 words and showed you, one by one: the delicacy and elegance, the colour and vividness of Taiwanese live in its rich vocabulary and its sounds.[2] It is a quiet kind of rebuttal. No argument. Just look.

She is herself the best evidence. Born in 1999, a sociology graduate of National Taiwan University who went on to study clinical psychology, now a freelance writer and clinical psychologist. In 2019 she won an honourable mention in the Taiwanese poetry division of the 8th Taichung Literature Awards; in 2022 she won first prize in the same division; in 2024 she won a runner-up prize in the 30th Maeda Junko Prize student tanka competition; she has also translated Tagore's Stray Birds into written Taiwanese.[2] A 26-year-old proving with one collection that Taiwanese is not a language of the past. It is a language in the present tense.

Chapter FourJi̍t-hue and Komorebi

One comparison in this book deserves a slow look: the Japanese komorebi and the Taiwanese ji̍t-hue. The two words describe a similar scene, but their ranges differ. Komorebi refers specifically to sunlight through tree leaves. Ji̍t-hue is broader: gaps in clouds, gaps in leaves, gaps between coloured windowpanes, light landing on a table — all of it is sunlight in bloom.[3] The words a language has decide what its speakers can see. Without a name, a scene is only a scene. With a name, the same light becomes something to talk about, to write poems about, to remember.

This is also why Wen says that writing poetry is, for her, the process of flattening out the folds of a life. When life moves too fast, there is no time to look closely at those marks; only by spreading them out do you find, under fold after fold, the things a hurried self let slip — an unremarkable view, an untidy story, a feeling too fine to hold. Writing poetry is taking those things out carefully and giving each a name of its own length.[3]

The word ji̍t-hue itself is the best demonstration. It is not a new coinage. It is an old word, found in dictionaries, once on the lips of grandparents. What Wen did was take it out of the dictionary, dust it off, hold it up to the light, and let a new generation of readers see it again. Sometimes reviving a language needs no new words. It only needs the old words spoken once more.

Chapter FiveWhat It Gets Right, What It Misses

Three things are right. First, the barrier really is low. Three parallel versions plus audio recordings make this book a poetry collection, a dictionary, and an audiobook at once. A reader who knows no Taiwanese can enter through the Mandarin translation, follow the romanisation, and check against the author's voice. Second, the word choices are documented. The 100 words were not picked by feel; their sources are stated — the 1931 Taiwanese-Japanese Dictionary, the Ministry of Education's Taiwanese dictionary, everyday speech.[2][5][6] Readers can trust that these words are real, not invented. Third, the poems are restrained. None is long, none shows off. The word stands in front; the poem stands behind it.

Three things should also be said plainly. First, 100 short poems make a walk, not a climb. Readers get 100 brief surprises, not one long immersion. Those who like long poems and narrative may find it leaves them wanting more. Second, the selection leans toward beauty. Scenery, longing, sweetness, and hope take up most of the book; the rough, funny, sharp-edged side of everyday Taiwanese appears less. That is a choice of theme, not an error — but readers should know that the Taiwanese in this book is Taiwanese at its most beautiful. Third, reading poems is not speaking. Recognising characters and reading aloud is not the same as bargaining at a market or talking to a nurse at a hospital. Tâi-lô romanisation is still a hurdle for complete beginners. This book can make people fall in love with Taiwanese. Speaking it fluently will take more practice.

EpilogueGiving the Name Back to the Light

Close the book, and what remains is not 100 poems but a simple thought: a language is not lost because dictionaries disappear. It is lost because no one in daily life calls things by their names any more. Sunlight falls through leaves every day. But if no one says "chhut ji̍t-hue-á," that light is only light. It never becomes a sentence, never becomes a poem, never becomes a secret code between two generations.

At the end of her preface, Wen thanks beautiful Taiwanese for giving her so many words to shape a life with, and thanks her folded, wrinkled life for giving her reasons to draw close to herself and to the roots of her family's tongues and hearts.[3] What this book does is small: it brings back the names of 100 things. But small has its advantage. Once the names are back, the light has somewhere to go, and a conversation has somewhere to start. Tomorrow, walking past a tree, when sunlight leaks through the leaves, you will know what it is called. That is enough.

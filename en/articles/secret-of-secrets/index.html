The Secret of Secrets: A Novel Eight Years in the Making Bets on Where We Go When We Die

Dan Brown's sixth Robert Langdon novel, eight years after the last, was published in the United States in September 2025 and reached Taiwan in March 2026. This time Langdon is not chasing an ancient artefact in Prague, but a secret about human consciousness and what happens after death. This deep read asks how much of the book's science is real, where the Golem comes from, and why the reviews are so divided.

PrologueA Book Eight Years in the Making

On September 9, 2025, Doubleday in the United States published a 688-page novel: The Secret of Secrets.[3][5] It was Dan Brown's first new book in eight years, the sixth Robert Langdon novel, following Origin in 2017.[2][3] The first printing ran to 1.5 million copies, with rights sold in 17 countries — both figures are the publisher's claims.[2] In its first week it reached No. 1 on the New York Times bestseller list, and by January 2026 it had spent 17 consecutive weeks on the list.[3][4]

The Traditional Chinese edition did not take long: China Times Publishing released it on March 31, 2026, in 672 pages, priced at NT$680, translated by Lee Chien-hsing, ISBN 9786264443258.[2] In November 2026 it reached No. 7 on Eslite's annual new-books bestseller chart.[12] Netflix had already announced a series adaptation in May 2025 — the show was on its way before the book was even out.[3]

This time, Langdon is not chasing an ancient artefact or a code. He is chasing a book — a book about human consciousness, a book someone wants never to be published. This deep read asks three questions: how much of the book's science is real? Where does the Golem come from? And why are the reviews so divided?

Chapter OneIn Prague, Langdon Falls in Love

The story opens on Charles Bridge. Chapter 3 has Langdon jogging along Křižovnická Street and across the snow-covered bridge, past its 30 statues of saints, above the Vltava River — the serialised chapter is free to read on Books.com.tw.[2] Langdon has not come to Prague alone. He is with his girlfriend, Katherine Solomon. Katherine is a cognitive scientist who first appeared in The Lost Symbol in 2009; this time she has come to give a lecture on her new book — an explosive discovery about the nature of human consciousness.[2][3]

Then comes something that has never happened in this series: Langdon falls in love. Six novels in, the symbology professor finally has a steady partner. Brown himself calls this his "most intricately plotted and ambitious novel yet".[2] The romance is not decoration; it is the engine of the plot. Katherine disappears, the manuscript of her new book is destroyed, and Langdon must cross Prague, pursued by a formidably powerful organisation, to find her.[2]

Prague is not just the setting here; it is one of the main characters. The novel's real locations include Charles Bridge, the Klementinum, Old Town Square, Prague Castle, Petřín Hill, and the Cold War bunker beneath Folimanka Park; Prague City Tourism worked with Argo Publishers on an official map marking 15 real places from the novel.[3] The Prague Astronomical Clock on the cover is one of the city's landmarks in the novel too — in this city, time has never been mere background.

Chapter TwoConsciousness: What Science Has Not Finished Saying

Katherine's new book is about "non-local consciousness": the mind may become completely open immediately before death, while individual consciousness may persist afterwards as part of a universal whole.[3] In other words, consciousness may not be a product manufactured by the brain; it may exist independently, with the brain serving only as a receiver. That is the secret of the whole book — and its gamble.

What does mainstream science say? In an OKAPI essay, Hsieh Po-jang writes it plainly: the mainstream position on consciousness is still physicalism — consciousness is a product of brain activity. When the neurosurgeon Wilder Penfield stimulated the cortex of awake patients during surgery, they reported vivid, specific experiences, which counts as strong evidence that consciousness comes from the brain.[8] Katherine's hypothesis runs exactly against that mainstream view.

"Noetic science" is a real field. The Institute of Noetic Sciences (IONS) was founded in 1973 by Apollo 14 astronaut Edgar Mitchell to study the relationship between consciousness, mind, and matter.[11] Dan Brown really did study brain science for this book; the Los Angeles Times covered his preparation.[9] The novel's Threshold programme — placing people in near-death states and monitoring and controlling their experiences with brain implants — is real research pushed to a fictional extreme: research into near-death experiences is real, research into brain–computer interfaces is real, but stitching them into an underground facility and using them for espionage is fiction.

A line has to be drawn here: non-local consciousness is a hypothesis, not an established fact. The novel writes it as if it were settled, and readers can easily mistake it for truth. Hsieh's warning in OKAPI is that science still cannot explain the secret of consciousness — and that sentence cuts two ways: science has not explained it, which does not mean the novel has.[8]

Chapter ThreeThe Golem: Prague's Oldest Guardian

Besides the people hunting Langdon, there is a killer who calls himself the Golem.[3] The Golem is Prague's oldest legend: in the 16th century, Rabbi Judah Loew ben Bezalel is said to have shaped a giant from clay to protect the residents of Prague's Jewish quarter. The Golem had no will of its own and obeyed only its master; in the legend, Loew finally took back the life he had given it, and it turned to clay again.

The novel moves that legend into Prague in 2025: the Golem acts in the dark, protecting those who should be protected and killing those who should be killed. What that means only becomes clear at the end — the Golem's identity is the book's biggest twist, and this article will not spoil it. One thing can be said in advance: the Golem is not a monster; it is a guardian. Brown borrows the legend to ask a question: if something made to protect people starts deciding for itself who deserves to die, is it still a guardian?

A review in The Forward notes that the novel's handling of Jewish material is controversial: the Golem story is borrowed as a thriller device, while Jewish tradition itself barely gets a voice in the book.[10] The criticism is worth recording. Brown has always been good at borrowing from religion and legend — but when he borrows, he often takes only the most dramatic part.

Prague itself is the vessel of the Golem legend. The Jewish quarter, the Old Town, the castle, Charles Bridge — every place in the novel is real.[3] It is Brown's old method: The Da Vinci Code used the Louvre and Paris, Angels & Demons used the Vatican, and this time it is Prague. The city is real, the legend is real, and the story built from them is invented — the mix of true and false is exactly what readers call "the most fascinating thing about Dan Brown".[2]

Chapter FourIt Sells Because It Asks the Biggest Question

The first reason it sells is the wait. Eight years. The previous book, Origin, came out in 2017, and readers waited eight years for the next one.[2][3] For a series novel, eight years is a long gap — long enough for anticipation to become an event.

The second reason is the size of the question. What happens after death is humanity's oldest question and its hardest to answer. Brown packages it as a thriller: a secret about consciousness, a hunted manuscript, an underground facility. Readers are not only buying a story; they are buying the imagination of "if there is something after death, what could it be?"

The third reason is the moment. In 2025, AI and brain–computer interfaces are in the news every day, and the question of consciousness is no longer the philosophy department's property. Katherine's research — consciousness may not be in the brain — lands exactly on the anxiety of this era: machines are becoming more like people, and people are becoming less sure what they are.

The fourth reason is the brand. Dan Brown's novels have been translated into 56 languages, with total print runs of 250 million copies — the publisher's figures; The Da Vinci Code, published in 2003, is one of the best-selling novels in history.[2] A new book from an author who has sold bestsellers for more than 20 years is news in itself. Netflix announced the series adaptation in May 2025; the show was on its way before the book was even out.[3]

Chapter FiveWhat It Gets Right, and What It Misses

Three things deserve credit. First, it brings unpopular science into a mainstream novel. Consciousness research, noetic science, near-death experiences — subjects that used to circulate only in academic circles and niche readerships are now inside a thriller with a first printing of 1.5 million copies.[2] Second, Prague is written like a main character. The city is not a backdrop; it is part of the plot: a morning on Charles Bridge, the library of the Klementinum, a Cold War bunker — every place moves the story.[3] Third, it dares to write about love. Six books in, the hero finally falls in love — a human touch the earlier Langdon never had.

Three things are missing. First, the reviews are divided. The New York Times called the book "a wistful testament to the power of the printed word", while also treating it as a work of nostalgia that falls short of The Da Vinci Code's cultural impact.[6][3] The Guardian's review was blunter; its headline reads "weapons-grade nonsense from beginning to end".[7] Reader ratings on Books.com.tw are just as split: some give 5 stars, others 0.5, calling it "shockingly mediocre".[2] Second, the line between science and fiction. Non-local consciousness is written too much like an established fact; readers with no background in the science can easily take it as true. Third, the formula. Codes, chases, secret organisations, a mystery solved within 24 hours — the sixth time around. A familiar recipe, a familiar taste, familiar enough to predict what the next chapter will do.

One more thing: the English original runs 688 pages, the Chinese edition 672 — a thick book.[2][3] Thickness is not the problem; pace is. Brown's method is to end every chapter on a hook, and 688 pages means hundreds of hooks. With that many hooks, readers turn the pages fast — and just as easily forget what they have just read.

Questions Without Answers Yet

To be honest, the following three things still have no reliable answers, and this article will not pretend otherwise:

  • The Chinese edition's actual sales in Taiwan: the publisher has not released a Taiwan sales figure; this article found no reliable number and will not estimate one.
  • How far the brain-implant technology in the book has progressed in reality: the Threshold programme is a fictional extrapolation, and comparable research in the real world is still developing; this article does not vouch for the book's depiction of the technology.
  • When the Netflix series will be released: the adaptation was announced in May 2025, and no release date had been announced when this article was written.

EpilogueWhat Is the Secret of Secrets?

The title works on two levels. The first is Katherine's book: the secret of consciousness. The second is Brown's book: the secret of death. The book borrows a line from the Czech psychiatrist Stanislav Grof: "Eliminating the fear of death can transform the way a person lives in the world."[1][2] That sentence is the key to the whole book.

What Langdon burns in the book is supposedly the last copy of Katherine's manuscript — but what he actually burns is only its bibliography; the manuscript survives.[3] That ending is Brown's love letter to the printed book, and the source of the New York Times review's phrase, "a wistful testament to the power of the printed word".[6] A book can be hunted, destroyed, and digitised — but as long as one copy printed on paper remains, the secret remains.

So what is the secret of secrets? Not an answer, but an attitude: death is not an end; it is the start of the next question. Brown spent 688 pages saying one thing: people fear death because they do not know what happens afterwards — and people live because that question still has no answer.

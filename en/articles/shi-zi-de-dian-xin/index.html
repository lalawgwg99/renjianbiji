The Lion's Snack: On the Last Mile of Life, Order the One Treat You Want to Taste Again

Ito Ogawa's 2019 novel took second place in the 2020 Japan Booksellers' Award, became an 8-episode NHK drama in 2021, and was published in Taiwan on July 5, 2021, translated by Wang Yun-jie; in 2026 it reached number 10 on Books.com.tw's literary fiction chart. Shizuku Umino, 33, has been told her cancer is terminal, and moves into the Lion's House, a hospice on a lemon island in the Seto Inland Sea, where every Sunday at 3 p.m. residents may order the one snack they most want to taste again. This deep read asks: why does a novel about dying make readers want to live more carefully — and has its gentleness made death look too clean?

PrologueA Snack She Could Not Choose

Near the end of December, a 33-year-old woman took a ferry to a small island covered in lemon trees. Her name was Shizuku Umino. A doctor had just told her that her cancer was terminal and that she did not have long left. She did not stay in a hospital, wired up to machines, fighting to the end. Instead she chose a hospice on an island in the Seto Inland Sea called the Lion's House, where she would spend her last days.[3][5]

The Lion's House has one rule, and only one: every Sunday at 3 in the afternoon, it is snack time. Each resident may write down the one snack they most want to taste again, together with the story behind it, and drop it into a special box. Madonna, who runs the house, draws lots to decide whose wish comes true, and the winner is only revealed on the day itself.[3] But Shizuku thought and thought, and could not decide. She could not think of a single snack she wanted most.

This is Ito Ogawa's The Lion's Snack. The Japanese original, Raion no Oyatsu, was published by Poplar on October 10, 2019, 255 pages; the Traditional Chinese edition, translated by Wang Yun-jie, was published in Taiwan by Yuezhi Culture on July 5, 2021, 352 pages.[1][2] It took second place in the 2020 Japan Booksellers' Award, was adapted by NHK into an 8-episode television drama in 2021, and in 2026 reached number 10 on Books.com.tw's literary fiction chart.[3][8] This deep read asks two questions: why does a novel about dying make its readers want to live more carefully? And has its gentleness made death look too clean?

Chapter OneIt Went Viral Because It Made Death Something You Can Eat

The Lion's Snack became famous for good reasons. First in Japan: second place in the 17th Japan Booksellers' Award in 2020, a prize voted on by booksellers across the country, one person one vote, where what wins is not a jury's taste but the instinct of people who stand in front of the shelves every day.[3] By April 2021, more than 200,000 copies were in print; the publisher's blurb said sales had passed 150,000 within half a year of publication.[2][4] That same year NHK turned it into an 8-episode drama, airing Sunday nights on BS Premium from June 27 to August 15, 2021, with Kaho Tsuchimura as Shizuku and Kyoka Suzuki as Madonna.[3]

Its fame in Taiwan came more slowly, and lasted longer. The Chinese edition came out in July 2021, and five years later, in November 2026, a roundup of Books.com.tw's literary fiction chart still listed it at number 10 — a book from 2019, still selling on a 2026 chart.[8] As of November 27, 2026, 60 readers on Books.com.tw had given it a perfect 5 out of 5, and 58 reviews said much the same thing: "I cried so hard," "I couldn't bear to turn the pages too fast."[2] A novel about dying, still making people cry seven years on — that is part of the answer.

The first reason it caught on is the question it asks. "At the last moment of your life, what would you like to eat?" Nobody is stuck for an answer, and nobody can answer it lightly. A last supper is too grand; it sounds like a farewell ceremony. A last snack is just right — light as a joke, heavy as a whole life. Ogawa has said in interviews that when you ask people what they would want for their last meal, they answer surprisingly fast; ask them about their last snack, and everyone stalls — because snack memories are wrapped in joy, and to recall one, you have to turn over your entire life.[6]

The second reason is the author herself. Born in Yamagata City in 1973, Ito Ogawa debuted in 2008 with The Restaurant of Love Regained, which was made into a film in 2010, won Italy's Premio Bancarella in 2011, and took France's Prix Eugénie Brazier in 2013; Tsubaki Stationery Store took fourth place in the 2017 Booksellers' Award and was also adapted by NHK.[2] She is one of contemporary Japan's finest writers about food, and this time she wrote eating all the way to its end: at the end, what you eat is not flavour. It is memory.

Chapter TwoSnack Time: A Ritual at 3 P.M. Every Sunday

The rules of snack time are almost childishly simple: write down what you want, drop it in the box, wait for the draw. But that childish rule carries the whole novel. Every Sunday at 3 p.m., everyone in the house gathers to eat one snack and hear the story behind it. Douhua, canelé, apple pie, botan mochi — each snack is a slice cut out of somebody's life.[2][3] One person remembers childhood; another, a late wife; another, a mistake made long ago. When the snack is gone, the story stays, and the storyteller stays too — at least in everyone else's memory.

Shizuku cannot write hers down. Her problem is not that she has no favourite snack. It is that her whole life has been lived by putting other people first. Her parents died in a car accident when she was small; she was raised by her mother's twin brother, her foster father, until she finished middle school; when he remarried, she began living alone at 16; the last time she saw him was five years ago.[11] She is so used to pushing her own feelings aside that she can no longer remember what she wants. Snack time asks her to do just one thing: for once, choose for herself.

What she finally chooses is mille crêpe. Not a famous patisserie's signature, but the cake she made with her own hands for her foster father's birthday, when she was in the second or third grade. The cake she baked that year was far from perfect, but her father ate it happily. The choice is small, almost too small for a novel's climax, yet it fits the book's logic exactly: the snack you most want to taste again is never the tastiest one. It is the one someone was waiting for you to serve.[11]

The other details of the house all point the same way. It is called the Lion's House because a lion is the king of beasts: it need fear no enemy, and can simply eat and sleep in peace. Residents go by nicknames, and every nickname has a story behind it. Every morning the kitchen cooks a different porridge, 365 days without repeating. Rokka, the white dog, was left behind by a previous resident and is now raised by the whole house.[2][7] Put together, these details draw a hospice that defies every expectation: it does not look like a hospital. It looks like a home. Or more precisely, like a home where a person, for the first time before dying, finally gets to decide how to live.

Chapter ThreeWhere the Idea Came From: A Mother's Words, "I'm Afraid to Die"

Ogawa did not write this book because she had a theory about death. She wrote it because of her mother. Her mother had cancer, had been told how long she had left, and said to her on the phone: "I'm afraid to die." In an Asahi Shimbun interview, Ogawa said those words surprised her: even her mother was afraid of dying. Death, she realised, was not somebody else's business. She wanted to write a book that would make death less frightening, and the image she held in mind while writing was a bowl of porridge — she hoped the story would be like porridge, warming the stomach and warming the heart.[5]

To make it feel true, she went to talk to Shigeko Okuno, who works in terminal care. She had assumed a hospice was a place where everyone calmly accepted death and slipped away quietly. She learned it was nothing of the kind: some people struggle desperately against fate, some lose their temper, some make a scene, some laugh. Sadness and joy are both there; the everyday ups and downs of life are all there.[6] That interview set the tone of the whole book: dying is not a ceremony. It is a stretch of days that are still being lived.

The most personal part of the book is hidden in the snacks. Ogawa's own last snack would be the hotcakes her grandmother made. When she was small, her grandmother always made unfashionable snacks — fried dried rice cakes, tempura manjū — and she complained they were not glamorous enough. Then one early winter day, her grandmother set a frying pan on the stove and, with trembling hands, cooked the first thing in her life that had the word "cake" in its name, just for her. She has remembered that taste ever since.[5][6] So while the book appears to ask its readers, "What would you eat?", the author had already answered first: she would eat the one someone broke the rules to make for her.

The book is also a turning point in Ogawa's writing. From "eating is living" in The Restaurant of Love Regained, to "writing letters is living" in Tsubaki Stationery Store, to "walking toward death is living" here — she has written three ways of being alive. The difference is that the heroines of the first two books were repairing their lives, while this one is finishing hers. And finishing is harder to write than repairing, because it cannot lean on hope. It can only lean on honesty.

Chapter FourThe Hospice Care Is Real; the Lion's House Is Invented

To feel the full weight of this book, you need to know where hospice care comes from. The modern hospice movement began in 1967, when Cicely Saunders founded St Christopher's Hospice in south London — the first institution to put pain control, psychological support, social care, and spiritual companionship under one roof, caring not only for the body but for the whole person: body, mind, society, and spirit.[9] Taiwan came to it later: in 1982, Dr. Chung Chang-hong of Mackay Memorial Hospital introduced hospice care to Taiwan; in 1990, Mackay's Tamsui branch opened the first 18-bed hospice ward; in 2000, the Hospice Palliative Care Act passed, making Taiwan the first place in Asia to protect the right to a natural death by law.[10]

Against that background, you can see what the novel chose to keep and what it chose to leave out. Real hospice care is, at its core, symptom control: pain, breathlessness, nausea, sleeplessness, each to be managed. In the Lion's House, medical treatment is barely mentioned at all; what is talked about is porridge, snacks, walks, and the sea. In a real hospice, the family's opinions, the cost, the speed at which a condition worsens — all of these are daily realities. In the novel's house there are no bills, no family arguments, only Madonna saying, "There are no rules here." This is not the author failing to understand. She deliberately pushed the medicine into the background so that the people could stand in front.

A nurse at Pingtung Christian Hospital, writing after she finished the book, said the part that moved her most was precisely that Shizuku had not really accepted death at first: she moved into the hospice only because it seemed like the better option for her, while in her heart she was still thinking, "I want to live." Only when she admitted that — and the anger and grief came flooding back — did she truly begin to face what was happening. Wanting to live and accepting death are not opposites in this book. They are two sides of the same thing.[7] That sentence comes closer to the reality of hospice care than many textbooks: nobody walks in willingly. Everyone learns to accept it as they go.

The book also borrows a phrase often heard in Taiwan's hospice circles: the five farewells — to give thanks, to express love, to apologise, to forgive, and to say goodbye. Every day Shizuku spends on the island is practice for those five things: thanking her foster father, loving Tahiti, apologising to her father, forgiving herself, and finally saying goodbye properly.[7] The novel never turns those five words into a slogan. It lets readers recognise them for themselves inside the story. In that, it is cleverer than most self-help books.

Chapter FiveThe Critical Frame: Is It Too Gentle — a Beautification of Death?

But the praise has to stop here for a moment. The book's greatest strength is also its biggest problem: it is too gentle, so gentle that you start to wonder whether death can really be like this.

First, where did the pain go? Shizuku has terminal cancer. The book writes about her body weakening day by day, about not being able to walk or eat, and about Madonna easing her with painkillers and aromatherapy massage. But compared with a real deathbed, this pain is written with too much restraint. In real terminal cancer, pain often runs out of control, the drugs often cannot keep up, and incontinence, bedsores, delirium, and family members breaking down at the bedside are everyday sights in a hospice ward. The novel gently pushes all of that aside and keeps only the parts that can be written as prose. That is not fabrication. It is a choice — but readers should know that the choice makes death look far more dignified than it usually is.

Second, snack time itself is a romanticisation. Every Sunday at 3 p.m., everyone gathers, eats a snack, listens to a story — a tea party that never fails to happen. In reality, many terminal patients cannot eat at all: swallowing becomes difficult, taste changes, and even a favourite food will not go down. And some people have no family, no story to tell, or a story too painful to speak aloud. The novel assumes that everyone has a snack worth remembering and a memory worth sharing. That assumption is beautiful, but it is not everyone's reality. For those without such memories, the book's gentleness may sting a little.

Third, the plot's coincidences arrive too neatly. The snack Shizuku could not choose for an entire book suddenly becomes clear on the very day her foster father turns up with a half-sister she never knew she had; the promise she asks of Tahiti — to stand on the coast by the vineyard on the evening of the third day after her death and wave at the sky — is beautiful like the final shot of a film.[11] All of it is moving, and all of it is too perfectly timed. Real endings rarely arrive with a surprise delivered on schedule; more often, people leave with things unsaid. Ogawa has said in interviews that she did not want to write a heavy, sorrowful book; she wanted to write about how lovely and warm being alive can feel.[6] That is her right. But readers should keep their own judgement: this book does not show all of death. It shows death at its gentlest.

Fourth, Madonna is too much like a prophet. She always knows what to say, never panics, is always gentle, and even has a complete philosophy for facing death. In a real hospice team, nurses get tired too, say the wrong thing, and hide away to cry after a patient dies. The novel makes Madonna a person who never makes a mistake, which makes her enchanting — and a little unreal. Then again, perhaps that is exactly what Shizuku needed: someone who would not make a mistake, someone whose hand she could hold when she was most afraid.

Questions Without Answers Yet

To be honest, four things still have no reliable answer, and this piece will not pretend otherwise:

  • Actual sales of the Traditional Chinese edition: the publisher's blurb only says the Japanese edition passed 150,000 copies within half a year, and that total printings passed 200,000 by April 2021. How many copies the Taiwan edition sold has never been made public.
  • Whether the island in the novel has a real model: some readers guess it is Ōmishima in Ehime, but the author has never confirmed it, and the novel only calls it "a beautiful island" in the Seto Inland Sea.
  • The protagonist's age: the Asahi Shimbun, Pia, and most introductions to the novel say 33, but a MANTANWEB report's headline says 29. This piece follows 33; the difference has not been checked against the original book.
  • Whether the Lion's House has a real-life model: the author interviewed Shigeko Okuno, an expert in terminal care, but never said which hospice, if any, she drew on.

EpilogueWhich Snack Would You Order?

At the end of the book, Shizuku dies. Before she goes, she leaves a promise: on the evening of the third day after her death, Tahiti should take Rokka to the coast by the vineyard and wave at the sky. She believes she will try her hardest to wave back. The ending writes no heaven and no reincarnation, only a promise to wave — the living are still here, and the dead have not really gone far, only moved somewhere else, still watching the same sea.[11]

Close the book, and the question comes back to the reader. Not "Are you afraid to die?" but "If you had one snack left, which would you order?" Ogawa's own answer is the hotcake her grandmother cooked on the stove; Shizuku's is the mille crêpe she made for her foster father as a schoolgirl. Both answers share one thing: neither is the tastiest snack. Both are the ones someone was waiting for.

That is where this book is truly clever. It does not ask you to learn how to face death. It simply sets death down on a snack table and lets you discover something: that every day, when we eat, when we say "see you tomorrow," we are quietly assuming that tomorrow will come. To be able to take tomorrow for granted is, in itself, a very happy thing. After finishing this book, you will want to eat your next meal properly, answer the message you have been putting off, and say one honest thing to the people beside you. Not because death is near, but because the snacks are good — and there really is not much time left.

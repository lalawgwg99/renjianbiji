Sunrise on the Reaping: What Can Still Be Written When Everyone Already Knows the Ending

Suzanne Collins, published by Scholastic on March 18, 2025, 400 pages; more than 1.5 million English-language copies sold in its first week, number 1 on bestseller lists in the US, UK, Canada, Australia and New Zealand. The Traditional Chinese edition, translated by Wang Xinying, was published by Locus Publishing on June 27, 2026, 512 pages. This deep read asks: how did a story whose ending was written 17 years ago become the bestselling young adult novel of 2025?

PrologueReaped on His Birthday

On March 18, 2025, Sunrise on the Reaping went on sale simultaneously in the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia and New Zealand, and sold more than 1.5 million English-language copies in its first week — over 1.2 million of them in the US alone, twice the first-week American sales of its predecessor The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes and three times those of Mockingjay in 2010.[2] It debuted at number 1 on the New York Times, USA Today, Publishers Weekly and independent bookstore bestseller lists, and was named one of the New York Times 100 Notable Books of 2025.[3] But the book has one built-in problem: it tells the story of a contest whose outcome everyone already knows.

Its hero, Haymitch Abernathy, appears in the original trilogy as the mentor of District 12 — a middle-aged man who reeks of drink, speaks with a poison tongue and is permanently hung over. Readers have known since 2008 that he won the 50th Hunger Games at 16, and have known just as long that winning ruined him: everyone he loved died, and he has drunk alone ever since. What can still be written about a story whose ending was fixed 17 years ago? This deep read asks exactly that.

Chapter OneWho Haymitch Is

The story opens on the morning of the reaping for the 50th Hunger Games, which happens to be Haymitch's 16th birthday.[7] This year is the Second Quarter Quell, and to mark the anniversary of the rebellion's defeat the Capitol doubles the tributes: each district must send 4 children, 48 enter the arena, and only 1 comes out alive.[1][7] Haymitch wants only to survive the day and spend it with his girl, Lenore Dove Baird. She is Covey — she plays the guitar and sings — the one soft thing in his life in the Seam.[7]

Then the reaping goes wrong. Woodbine Chance, one of the boys drawn, rebels on the spot and is shot dead; in the chaos Haymitch steps in to shield Lenore Dove and is taken as a tribute instead.[7] Three others from District 12 go with him to the Capitol: Maysilee Donner, a merchant's daughter; Wyatt Callow, an oddsmaker obsessed with the odds; and Louella McCoy, 13 years old, a little neighbour like a sister to him.[7] Before they even reach the Capitol, all four understand one thing: these Games were never designed for them to win. They were designed for them to die beautifully.

First, the connection to Katniss has to be made plain. Katniss's father, Burdock Everdeen, is Haymitch's best friend; Katniss's mother, Asterid, is Maysilee's best friend; and the mockingjay pin on Maysilee's neck will become the emblem of the whole series.[7] Collins is not writing a standalone story. She is picking up names the trilogy mentioned in passing, one by one, and giving them flesh and blood.

Chapter TwoWhy It Took Off

Start with the numbers. The World English first printing was 2.5 million copies; first-week sales passed 1.5 million English-language copies worldwide, more than 1.2 million in the US; Scholastic called it the best first week of any book in the series.[2] It launched in 25 languages at once,[2] and the Traditional Chinese edition, translated by Wang Xinying, was published by Locus Publishing on June 27, 2026 — 512 pages, NT$450.[1][5]

Those numbers only make sense at the scale of the series. The trilogy — The Hunger Games, Catching Fire and Mockingjay — appeared in 2008, 2009 and 2010, and the first volume spent more than 260 consecutive weeks on the New York Times bestseller list[13]; all five books in the series now number more than 100 million copies in print and digital formats worldwide, with rights sold in 55 languages.[2] Sunrise on the Reaping did not become a hit out of nowhere. It stands on top of a pyramid 17 years in the building.

The first reason it took off is timing. Five years had passed since The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes, 15 years since the trilogy ended, and the generation that grew up reading these books is now old enough to buy books for itself — and to take its own children into bookshops. Nostalgia is not this book's selling point. It is its ticket of admission.

The second reason is that the film was already lined up. Lionsgate announced the adaptation before the book was even published; the film opened on November 20, 2026, directed by Francis Lawrence, with Joseph Zada as Haymitch, Ralph Fiennes as President Snow and Elle Fanning as a young Effie Trinket.[10] The book sells the story, the film sells the anticipation, and each lifts the other. The film took about US$225 million worldwide in its opening weekend, US$120 million of it in the United States and Canada.[11]

The third reason is the hero himself. Haymitch is the trilogy's most beloved supporting character: readers have loved that sharp-tongued, soft-hearted, permanently hung-over mentor for 17 years. This time Collins is not introducing a new hero. She is taking the person readers most wanted to know about and writing him from the beginning.

Chapter ThreeWhere the Ideas Come From

Collins dedicates the book to George Orwell, William Blake and David Hume.[7] Hume is the key: in interviews she has said that his theory of implicit submission is where the book begins — the idea that people quietly hand their feelings and their judgment over to their rulers.[7] This is not armchair philosophy. It is the engine of the whole novel: a regime does not need everyone to believe in it sincerely. It only needs everyone to submit by default.

Put into the story, that idea becomes propaganda. The Capitol does not merely stage a contest; it stages a narrative. Tributes are dressed, interviewed and scored; the audience falls in love with them first, and only then watches them die. Plutarch Heavensbee appears here as a young propaganda officer, charged with packaging the 50th Games as a celebration;[7] the New York Times review said Collins paints a shrewd portrait of how the machinery of propaganda works and how authoritarianism takes root.[3] That is not a news commentary from 2025. It is a review of a young adult novel.

The other idea comes from the arena itself. This arena is absurdly beautiful: yellow flowers everywhere, a clear lake, fruit trees heavy with fruit — but the pollen is poison, the water burns the throat, the fruit is lethal.[7] Beauty itself is the trap. Here Collins plays her oldest trick: she writes temptation and danger as the same thing, so that the more comfortable the reader feels, the more afraid they should be.

Chapter FourWhat It Gets Right

The first thing it gets right is that it does not flinch from the cruelty. Louella is crushed to death in an accident at the chariot parade, and the Capitol immediately replaces her with a brainwashed body double called Lou Lou, as if a 13-year-old child were a part that could be swapped.[7] Maysilee dies in Haymitch's arms, her throat torn open by a flock of pink bird mutts;[7] Haymitch wins the final fight by turning the arena's force field against his opponent, ducking the axe she throws so that it rebounds off the invisible barrier and kills her.[7] The New York Times called the book propulsive and brutal,[3] and Publishers Weekly gave it a starred review, calling it a brutal tale of compassion and rage and a frank examination of propaganda and tragedy.[3]

The second thing it gets right is that it gives rebellion a starting point, not a slogan. Beetee, the victor from District 3, secretly plans to bomb the arena's water tank and flood Sub-A, the computer brain that runs the Games;[7] Plutarch leaves a back door inside the propaganda machine. These people are not heroes. They are people who found a crack in the system. The fire Katniss lights 24 years later is primed here, in advance.

The third thing it gets right is the ending. Haymitch wins the Games and comes home to District 12 to find his mother and his little brother Sid burned to death in their house; soon afterwards Lenore Dove is killed by poisoned gumdrops sent by Snow.[7] He does not take revenge. He does not make a speech. He drinks alone and lives alone. Years later he tells this story to Katniss and Peeta, writing it into a memorial book so that the names of the dead will not be forgotten.[7] For a novel written for readers aged 12 and up, that is an ending built on an extraordinary trust in the reader.

Chapter FiveWhat It Misses

The first miss is suspense. From page one the reader knows Haymitch will win, will survive, and will lose everything. Collins replaces "will he win?" with "how does he win?", and most of the time the substitution works: the force-field kill, the water-tank bomb, the body double — every step is solidly built. But some passages cannot escape the prequel's built-in flaw: when you know the hero cannot die, the danger is only a performance.

The second miss is the line around fan service. The book is packed with echoes: where the pin came from, the youth of Katniss's parents, Plutarch's young days, Effie's first appearance. For old readers every one of them is a gift; for new readers every one of them is a reference that needs a footnote. Collins mostly gets the balance right, but the friendship between Maysilee and Asterid, and the brotherhood between Burdock and Haymitch, are in places written as if winking at old readers rather than serving the story.

The third miss is the tension between the violence and the message. The book condemns the Capitol for turning murder into entertainment, while writing the murders beautifully: poison flowers, poison fruit, bird mutts, a force-field kill — every death is carefully designed. Collins knows the contradiction, of course; she makes Haymitch pay for his victory with everything he has, which is her answer to it. But an answer is one thing, and the pleasure a reader takes in the arena scenes is another. This is not a problem unique to this book — the series has carried it since the first volume. The prequel only makes it bigger.

Set against the previous prequel, the difference is clear. The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes tells how Snow became a tyrant; its film opened in 2023, grossed about US$350 million worldwide, and divided the critics, with a 64% score on Rotten Tomatoes.[8][12] That book's problem was its hero: readers had to spend 500 pages falling in love alongside a future dictator. Sunrise on the Reaping has no such problem — Haymitch is on the reader's side from the first page. The reviews say so too: the New York Times called it great, USA Today said yes, the new prequel really is that good,[3] and the film's US$225 million opening weekend dwarfs its predecessor's sub-US$100 million debut.[11][12]

Questions Without Answers Yet

To be honest, a few things still have no reliable answer, and this piece will not pretend otherwise:

  • Actual sales of the Traditional Chinese edition in Taiwan: the publisher has not released sales figures, and no reliable total could be verified here, so none is estimated.
  • The film's final worldwide box office: the film opened on November 20, 2026, and at the time of writing only the opening-weekend figure of about US$225 million is available, so no final total is given.
  • The precise first-week sales of The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes: Scholastic said only that Sunrise on the Reaping sold twice as many copies in the US in its first week, without publishing the predecessor's exact first-week figure, so none is calculated here.
  • Whether Collins will write another Hunger Games prequel: no new book has been announced as of this writing, so none is speculated about.

EpilogueWhat the Dawn Reaps

The title, "Sunrise on the Reaping," works on two levels. Literally, it is the dawn of reaping day, the morning Haymitch turns 16 and the harvest begins. Metaphorically, dawn is the most hopeful hour of the day — and in this book the dawn reaps hope itself, because the Capitol has arranged everything before first light.

But Collins leaves one thing unharvested: memory. In the closing pages Haymitch writes the names down one by one — Maysilee, Wyatt, Louella, Ampert — refusing to let the Capitol decide who deserves to be remembered. Seventeen years ago Katniss survived the arena with poison berries; 24 years before that, Haymitch survived it with an invisible wall. They won in different ways, but what they did after winning was the same: remember the dead, and refuse to live by the script the Capitol wrote for them. That is why a story whose ending was written long ago was still worth writing — and still worth reading.

The Island's Time Machine: How Taiwan's High-Speed Rail Pressed an Island into a Single City

From a much-mocked money pit to 225,000 passengers a day, one railway rewrote Taiwan's sense of time, its sense of space, and the meaning of the word “far”.

PrologueBanqiao, the Morning of 5 January 2007

At 7 a.m. on 5 January 2007, the first high-speed train pulled out of Banqiao Station, heading south for Zuoying. The platform was crowded — some people had come to ride, but many more had come just to watch, to see with their own eyes what a train running at 300 kilometres per hour looked like on Taiwanese soil. From that day on, time in Taiwan has been measured in two ways. In the old way, Taipei to Kaohsiung was half a day's journey, a trip that swallowed a whole morning or afternoon. In the new way, it was 96 minutes — about two-thirds of a film, about the length of a short nap.[2][8]

No one could foresee what would follow that day. Once the applause of the opening ceremony faded, what remained were half-empty carriages, a mountain of debt, and a question everyone was asking: was this railway, built at a cost of NT$513.3 billion, Taiwan's pride — or a gamble that would never stop losing money?[6] Nearly 20 years later, the answer is written on the platforms every day: in 2025, 82.07 million passenger trips were made on the high-speed rail, an average of 225,000 people a day.[1] This essay is not an engineering history of a railway. It is about how one railway rewrote an island's sense of time and space — and what Taiwanese people mean by the word "far".

Chapter OneA Gamble Called BOT

The story of Taiwan's high-speed rail begins with a bet. On 11 May 1998, Taiwan High Speed Rail Corporation was founded; on 23 July that year, it signed a build-operate-transfer agreement with the Ministry of Transportation and Communications — a private company would finance and build the line, operate it for 35 years, and then hand it back to the government.[4] At the time, it was one of the largest BOT projects in the world, with a total project cost of NT$513.3 billion.[6] Five private shareholders — Continental Engineering, Evergreen, Fubon, Pacific Electric Wire and Cable, and TECO — won the concession with a "zero-dollar bid", proclaiming that the government would not have to spend a cent.[9]

"The government won't have to spend a cent" sounded clean and decisive. In reality, it was a cheque covered in fine print. The logic of BOT is to hand the risks of public infrastructure to the market: private money builds it, private money bears the risk, and the government merely supervises and takes over at the end. But a railway is not a factory. Its payback period is measured in decades, and a single percentage point of error in ridership forecasts can overturn the entire financial model. What is more, the technology itself was switched midway: the winning bid was based on a European system, but the line was ultimately built on Japanese Shinkansen technology — and settling with the European consortium alone cost US$65 million.[6] In 2000, civil construction began, and Taiwan's western corridor entered years of living beside building sites.[2][4]

The financial model of a railway never bets only on whether the engineering can be finished. It bets on whether people, decades later, will want — and need — to move that way.

The strangest thing about this gamble was what it was really betting on: not technology, but human nature. Engineers could guarantee that trains would run at 300 kilometres per hour — test runs in 2005 even reached 315[7] — but no one could guarantee that Taiwanese people would change how they moved. Would someone used to taking the night train south, used to treating Taipei–Kaohsiung as "a long trip away", pay twice the fare to save three hours? No blueprint could answer that question. Only the crowds on the platform, on opening day, could.

Chapter TwoThe Empty Platform

After opening, the answer arrived — colder than anyone had expected. In 2007, the high-speed rail carried only 15.556 million passengers, an average of 42,600 a day,[3] against a forecast in the original investment plan of 236,000 passengers a day for that year.[3] In other words, actual ridership was less than a fifth of the projection. Brand-new stations stood in the middle of farmland, wide platforms held only a handful of travellers, and trains glided in and out like a performance without an audience. The media began calling it a money pit, and the doubts spread from the Legislative Yuan to every street corner.

Behind the numbers lay a brutal financial chain: ridership below forecast meant ticket revenue could not cover interest and depreciation; in 2009, the company ran into a shortfall in repaying principal and interest, preferred-share dividends went unpaid, and lawsuits and default risks followed one after another.[3] The trains kept running on schedule while the finances quietly sank beneath the surface. By the end of 2014, the company had only NT$1.8 billion in usable liquid funds — one lost lawsuit away from bankruptcy.[9] That was the high-speed rail's most ironic moment: it was running more steadily than ever, carrying more people than ever — in June 2013, daily ridership passed 129,000 for the first time[7] — and yet it was closer than ever to collapse. The problem was never whether people rode it. The problem was that the original financial structure could not support the real pace at which a railway grows.

Those quiet early years proved something important: the value of infrastructure cannot be judged by its first year's balance sheet. People take time to change their habits, and a society takes time to relearn what "distance" means. In the first years after opening, Taiwanese people still thought with an old map: Kaohsiung was "the south", another world, a trip that demanded ceremony. No one puts "same-day return" into their life overnight. Habits turn far more slowly than trains do.

Chapter ThreeThe Map Rewritten in 96 Minutes

Change arrived quietly. No one ever announced the birth of the "one-day living circle"; it grew out of countless ordinary decisions. A salesperson living in Taichung takes the morning train to a meeting in Taipei and is back at the office by lunch. Parents in Kaohsiung send their child to a competition in Taipei on a Saturday morning and are home for dinner. An engineer in the Hsinchu Science Park starts seriously considering living in Taichung, because the commute is only 30 minutes. The fastest Taipei–Zuoying trip takes just 96 minutes,[8] and a non-stop train from Nangang to Zuoying takes about 105 minutes[7] — a journey that once consumed most of a day is now shorter than a film.

When travel time is compressed, the meaning of space changes with it. Taiwan's western corridor was once a string of scattered cities, each with its own sphere of life: Taipei was Taipei, Taichung was Taichung, Kaohsiung was Kaohsiung. The high-speed rail stitched them into a single line — 12 stations like 12 knots tying together 345 kilometres of coastline.[1][2] Miaoli, Changhua and Yunlin stations joined the network on 1 December 2015, and Nangang followed on 1 July 2016, completing the line.[2] For the first time, someone living in Yunlin could feel that Taipei was not "very far away" but "an hour away"; a young person in Kaohsiung could feel that Taipei was not "another place" but somewhere you could go — and come back from — in a single day.

The deepest part of this change is not on the timetable; it is in people's minds. A sense of distance is a psychological state, not a physical length. When Taipei–Kaohsiung shrinks from "half a day" to "96 minutes", the invisible border between north and south loosens. People in the south no longer feel they live on the "periphery"; people in the north no longer feel that heading south is an expedition. Every life decision that requires crossing cities — finding a job, going to school, seeing a doctor, visiting family, falling in love — gets recalculated because of one railway. The "one-day living circle" is, in the end, not a transport term but a new imagination of life: the whole west coast can be a single city.

Chapter FourWhose High-Speed Rail?

But before the high-speed rail could become part of everyday life, it had to survive. In 2015, the government and the company completed a financial restructuring: the original shareholders cut their capital by 60%, all preferred shares were redeemed, and public and quasi-public investors injected NT$30 billion, while the concession was extended from 35 years to 70.[4][9] After the restructuring, the public and quasi-public stake exceeded 60%, the Ministry of Transportation and Communications became the largest shareholder, and the company changed from a private enterprise into a government-owned, privately operated one.[9] On 27 October 2016, Taiwan High Speed Rail Corporation was listed on the Taiwan Stock Exchange, under the ticker 2633.[4]

The restructuring was fiercely controversial at the time. Some said the public was footing the bill; some said it was a gift to conglomerates; others said it was a painful but necessary rescue. Looked at differently, it answered the most fundamental question of the BOT model: whose railway is this, really? The promise that "the government won't have to spend a cent" had been built on wildly optimistic ridership forecasts. When that sandcastle collapsed, the government could not simply stand aside — because if the trains stopped, the cost would not be one company's bankruptcy but the paralysis of the entire western corridor. The 2015 restructuring amounted to an admission of a simple fact: some infrastructure is, in the end, a public good, and its finances cannot be judged by a single company's profit-and-loss statement alone.

If a railway is destined never to support itself on ticket fares alone, who should support it — the market, the government, or every person who rides it?

Notably, the restructuring did not turn the high-speed rail into a loss-making state enterprise. On the contrary, once freed from crushing depreciation charges and the preferred-share burden, its profits began to grow steadily. Operating revenue reached NT$53.19 billion in 2024 and NT$54.65 billion in 2025, with after-tax net profit of NT$6.58 billion in 2025.[1] The company once dismissed as a "money pit" is now a steadily dividend-paying listed company on Taiwan's stock market. The turnaround is a reminder: what was "losing money" was often not the operation, but the financial assumptions written at the start.

Chapter FiveAfter It Became Ordinary

In 2024, the high-speed rail carried 78.25 million passengers, an average of 214,000 a day, a record since opening;[5] in 2025 it went further — 82.07 million trips, 225,000 a day on average, with a punctuality rate of 99.31%.[1] Cumulative ridership has passed one billion trips, and the single-day record stands at 338,000 passengers, set on 1 October 2023.[7] On every holiday homecoming, every concert night, every New Year's Eve countdown, the high-speed rail station is the first place to fill up. The announcements most often heard on those once-empty platforms now warn that the non-reserved cars are full.

Beyond the numbers, the most telling change is how the high-speed rail has disappeared from people's attention. The best infrastructure is the kind you stop noticing: nobody praises tap water every day, and nobody thanks the high-speed rail for being on time every day. For the younger generation of Taiwanese, "taking the high-speed rail to Taipei" feels no different from "taking the metro to Ximending". They can hardly imagine that an earlier generation needed half a day to travel from Kaohsiung to Taipei. That sense of "of course" is the high-speed rail's greatest achievement — it turned a one-time miracle into the everyday.

Of course, the story is not finished. In 2023, the company ordered 12 new-generation trainsets based on Japan's N700S, expected to enter service in 2027;[10] extensions south to Pingtung and north to Yilan are still under discussion, and new towns around the stations are still growing. Record ridership has brought new troubles, too: seats are hard to come by at peak hours, and the queues for non-reserved cars grow longer. These are the problems of success — an entirely different order of problem from the question of the early years, which was whether the company would go bankrupt at all.

EpilogueAnother Way of Measuring Time

Looking back over nearly 20 years, what Taiwan's high-speed rail truly changed was never just speed. Speed is only the surface. What was rewritten was the unit by which Taiwanese people measure their lives. People used to measure the distance between north and south in days: a day to go to Kaohsiung, a day to come back to Taipei. Now they measure it in minutes: 96 minutes, 105 minutes, 30 minutes. When distance can be counted in minutes, the psychological borders between cities disappear, and people's choices multiply — you can live in Taichung and work in Taipei; you can attend a morning meeting in Kaohsiung and have dinner in Tainan; you can live in any city on the west coast without giving up the opportunities of another.

That is why the story of the high-speed rail is worth remembering. It is not a story about steel and concrete, but a story about confidence: whether a society dares to build a railway for a way of life that does not yet exist; whether it dares to admit that forecasts will be wrong and finances will run into trouble — and then keep the trains running while it repairs the damage, one fix at a time. The train that left Banqiao on the morning of 5 January 2007 carried more than its first passengers. It carried an island's wager on the future. Nearly 20 years later, the wager has paid off — not by making Taiwan perfect, but by making Taiwan smaller, and Taiwanese people's world larger.

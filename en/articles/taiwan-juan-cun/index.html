Taiwan's Military Villages: The Temporary Home That Lasted 75 Years

Around 1949, nearly two million soldiers and civilians crossed the strait to Taiwan. The government built rows of low houses beside the barracks, meant to last a few years. People ended up staying for three generations.

PrologueA Village That Thought It Would Only Stay a Few Years

At the foot of Taipei 101 stands a row of low, single-story houses — green doors, red doors, blue doors, weathered walls, black tile roofs. Tourists take photographs here: in the background, the tallest building in Taiwan; in the foreground, the shortest houses in Taipei. This is Four-Four South Village, the first military dependents' village in Taipei.[3]

The word juancun — military dependents' village — is rarely heard outside Taiwan. It refers to the settlements formed across the island by Nationalist soldiers and their families who came to Taiwan around 1949. At their peak, there were 886 government-listed villages in Taiwan.[2] They were meant to be temporary housing — everyone assumed they would go home in a few years. Instead, people stayed for three generations.

This article is not about one village. It is about how "temporary" becomes "forever". The juancun is a miniature of Taiwan's postwar history: a great migration, a forced adaptation, a homesickness that never got its farewell, and a group of people who kept living their lives in the cracks.

Chapter 1Crossing the Strait

Between 1945 and 1950, nearly two million soldiers and civilians moved from mainland China to Taiwan.[1] An island of about six million people suddenly gained a third more. There were not enough houses, not enough food, not enough of anything.

The first arrivals moved into barracks, warehouses and schools left behind by the Japanese. In November 1948, the 44th Arsenal of the Combined Service Forces moved from Qingdao to Taipei; its workers and families first lived in Japanese military warehouses at Sanzhangli, and the next year built their own houses south of the arsenal — Four-Four South Village, the first juancun in Taipei.[3] Which village was the first in all of Taiwan is not agreed: Huangpu New Village in Fengshan, Kaohsiung, housed military families as early as 1947 under the name Chengzheng New Village, and many sources call it the first in Taiwan; others give the title to Four-Four South Village, built in 1948. Both claims are half right — Huangpu came earlier, and Four-Four South was the first in Taipei.[3][9]

Three parties built the villages: the government provided land and money, the Women's Anti-Communist League (Fulianhui) raised donations to build houses, and families paid for their own houses on government-provided land.[1] The Fulianhui, led by Soong Mei-ling, funded a large number of the houses. They went up fast, and they were crude: bamboo walls plastered with mud, tiled or thatched roofs, one family to a room, shared kitchens and toilets. Everyone said it was temporary, so nobody renovated properly — after all, they would be going home soon.

Location followed the military: most villages were built near barracks or fortifications, grouped by service branch. Taipei had the most villages, packed the densest; Kaohsiung had the most village households in Taiwan — the navy at Zuoying, the air force at Gangshan, the army at Fengshan.[1][2] Even the names gave away their origins: some honoured a service branch, some named a donor, some carried the spirit of the age — Victory, Success, Nation-Building, Revival. A village name was a slogan.[9]

Chapter 2A Miniature China Behind Bamboo Fences

Walk into a juancun and you hear the accents first, then you smell the food.

In one alley, Shandong, Sichuan, Hunan and Cantonese accents mixed together, nobody understanding anybody else, everyone getting by on a patched-together "village Mandarin". Children grew up hearing four or five accents, and when they grew up and opened their mouths, people knew at once: this one came from a juancun. Outside the village was Taiwan; inside was a miniature China — not the China on a map, but the China of memory, the little bit of home each family had carried with them.

The most honest evidence was in the kitchen. Juancun cooking had no fixed recipes; it was old soldiers from every province recreating the tastes of home with Taiwanese ingredients, from memory. Shandong people made steamed buns, Shanxi people cut noodles, Sichuan people who missed spice stirred bean paste into the soup. Taiwanese beef noodle soup was born this way: postwar Taiwan had a custom against eating beef, but the mainland veterans had no such taboo, and in the villages they made noodles with American aid flour and stewed them with canned American aid beef, adding each province's touch, until a bowl of Taiwanese beef noodles emerged.[5] The red-braised style split into two schools — Shandong, the earliest traceable shop being Liu Shandong Beef Noodles, opened in 1951, and Sichuan style, the earliest traceable shop being the Gongyuan Pork Knuckle Noodles in Gangshan, Kaohsiung, in 1962. Gangshan was an air force village, and Sichuan had been the air force's base during the war against Japan.[5] One bowl of noodles is a history of migration.

Material life in the villages was makeshift. In the early years most cooking was done on coal-briquette stoves; a rice jar at home might be a converted artillery shell crate, an ashtray a spent shell casing.[2] Things from the army flowed into the home, and the home smelled of the army too. Children played in the alleys and listened to the radio. The first generation of villagers almost all believed they were only staying here for a while.

That sense of "temporary" shaped the architecture: houses could be extended and added to, but few people built them to last. Illegal additions grew one after another, the alleys narrowed, the villages grew crowded. Temporary houses became permanent homes — a single sentence that almost sums up the whole history of the juancun.

Chapter 3The Village Children Write the Village

The juancun truly became literature only after the second generation grew up.

The first generation was too busy surviving to write. In the 1970s, the mainland-born second generation began to write, and juancun themes truly appeared; by the 1980s they had formed a distinct current, known as "juancun literature".[6] The most famous of its writers is Chu Tien-hsin. In 1992 she published In Remembrance of My Buddies from the Military Compound, about the "buddies" who grew up in the villages and later scattered — film stars, politicians, writers, people whose upbringing was unusual but who could recognise one another instantly by instinct: "Oh — so you are here too."[6][8]

Juancun literature was never only nostalgia. It wrote about an awkwardness of identity: the second generation was born in Taiwan and grew up in Taiwan, yet their accents carried the mainland, and the gods their families worshipped, the food they ate, the words they spoke all pointed to a place they had never been. After martial law was lifted, that awkwardness came under a magnifying glass — are you Taiwanese, or are you Chinese? The villagers' answer was never either-or, but a state of being both and neither. What Chu Tien-hsin wrote was the weight of that state.

The same generation changed Taiwan. The second generation produced many writers, directors, musicians and politicians — Hou Hsiao-hsien grew up in a Fengshan village, and the juancun runs through his films; Teresa Teng and James Soong also grew up in the villages.[6] The villages were small, but the people they raised spread into every corner of Taiwanese society. The "temporary village" was in fact one of postwar Taiwan's most important cultural incubators.

Chapter 4Demolition and Reconstruction

The houses grew old, and so did the villages. In 1996, a law sped up their disappearance.

The National Military Old Dependents' Villages Reconstruction Act passed in 1996, and the government began large-scale rebuilding: tearing down the low, crowded old villages and building apartment blocks on the same land or elsewhere to house the original residents. By 2001, only 530 government-listed villages remained; by 2008, the programme had rehoused 71,074 households and carried out 433 villages.[1] The number 886 was the total of government-listed villages counted by the Ministry of National Defense in 2005 — and the origin of the name "Goodbye 886" for Kaohsiung's military village cultural park.[2]

Rebuilding had its reasons: the old houses were genuinely dilapidated, the pipes ageing, the alleys narrow, the public facilities inadequate, and residents wanted flats with lifts and balconies. But demolition had its price: once a village was torn down, the grocery shop at the alley entrance, the neighbours dropping in on each other, decades of accumulated goodwill — all of it scattered. The original residents moved into apartment blocks, where upstairs and downstairs became strangers. The juancun was not demolished; it was "improved" away.

The opening of family visits to the mainland in 1987 was another turning point. First-generation veterans could finally go back to visit, only to find that their hometowns were still there but no longer the hometowns of memory — and on returning, that Taiwan, where they had lived for 40 years, was home.[1] "We cannot go back" changed from a slogan into a fact. The "temporary" of the juancun formally expired that year.

Chapter 5How to Stay

Tearing down is easy. Staying needs a reason. Taiwan spent more than 20 years working out how.

The first way was to turn a village into a monument. Most of Four-Four South Village has been demolished, but in 2003 four of its houses were listed as historic buildings and rebuilt as the Xinyi Public Assembly Hall and a military village cultural park — the first juancun in Taipei to be preserved.[3] What you see there today is a carefully preserved "sample": the houses remain, but the people who lived in them have moved away.

The second way was to turn a village into a park. Mingde New Village in Zuoying, Kaohsiung, once housed many senior generals and was known as the "Generals' Village"; it is now open as the "Goodbye 886 — Taiwan Military Village Cultural Park", which will eventually comprise 52 houses, the largest military village preservation park in Taiwan.[2] Since 2014 the Kaohsiung city government has run a "live-in conservation" programme — people move in, repair the houses, and live in them. So far 133 houses have been restored and 91 households matched to move in.[2] A house with people living in it does not die.

The third way was the most unexpected: an old soldier picked up a paintbrush. Rainbow Village in Taichung was never a listed village at all — it was six houses built by nine veterans themselves. In August 2008, Huang Yung-fu, the last remaining resident — known as "Rainbow Grandpa" — began painting the walls with cheerful figures and animals. In 2010 university students launched the "Save Rainbow Village" campaign, and the Taichung city government preserved it as Rainbow Art Park, which opened on January 25, 2014.[4] At its peak, Rainbow Village drew 2 million visitors in 2017.[4] Huang died on January 23, 2024, at the age of 101.[4] His reason for painting was simple: the houses were about to be demolished, and once they were covered in paint, nobody could bear to tear them down.

EpilogueTemporary for 75 Years

From 1949 to 2024: 75 years. Most of the first generation of villagers are gone, the second generation is growing old, and some of the third generation no longer know what a juancun is. Of the 886 listed villages, most have been rebuilt as apartment blocks; the few that remain have become parks, assembly halls, art parks.

The juancun taught Taiwan one thing: "temporary" is the most deceptive word there is. They said they would stay a few years, and stayed a lifetime. But precisely because they thought it was temporary, people never built serious walls or defences — the alleys stayed open, the doors stayed open, the smell of a neighbour's cooking drifted over, you carried a bowl across, and they carried one back. The walls of a juancun were bamboo fences you could push over; the bonds between its people were stronger than in many gated communities.

There are no new juancun in Taiwan today. The few that remain exist no longer as housing, but as mirrors: they show how a great migration put down roots, how a homesickness was passed down three generations, and how a "temporary" decision came to live on an island forever. The low houses of Four-Four South Village still stand at the foot of Taipei 101 — low, weathered, unremarkable. But their very standing there is a sentence: we came, we stayed, we are part of Taiwan.

If the people who came to Taiwan in 1949 had known they would stay a lifetime, what would the villages have looked like? Would there still have been bamboo fences, still a grocery shop at the alley entrance? Did the word "temporary" wrong them — or did it, in the end, make them?

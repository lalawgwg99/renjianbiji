Taiwan Travelogue: A Novel Pretending to Be a Translation, and How the World Learned to Read Taiwan

In 1938, two women sharing a single name board a train across Taiwan and eat their way through twelve dishes; in 2026, the book won the International Booker Prize, the first Chinese-language work ever to do so.

PrologueA Translation That Never Existed

On the evening of May 19, 2026, at Tate Modern in London, Taiwanese writer Yang Shuangzi and Lin King, the translator of the English edition, lifted the International Booker Prize trophy together.[4] It was the first time a Taiwanese work had won the prize — and the first time for a work in Chinese.[5] The winning book had been published back in March 2020,[1] and took six years to travel from Taiwan to the world. And the strangest thing about it is something no prize can explain: the book pretends to be a translation.

The first edition's cover lists two names side by side: Chizuru Aoyama and Yang Shuangzi. The belly band reads "by Chizuru Aoyama, translated by Yang Shuangzi." According to that story, this is the autobiographical novel of a Japanese writer, first published in 1954, which Yang merely translated back into Chinese. But Chizuru Aoyama never existed. She is an author Yang invented, along with her 1938 journey across Taiwan.[2] A novel pretending to be a translation, which then won the world's highest prize for translated literature through a real translation — that fact alone is the book's best footnote.

Chapter One1938, Two Chizurus Board a Train

The story begins in May 1938. The Second Sino-Japanese War had broken out not long before, and Japan's government was pushing the kominka movement in colonial Taiwan, treating the island as a base for its southern advance. Chizuru Aoyama, an aristocratic writer from Nagasaki, was invited by the Government-General of Taiwan to give a lecture tour. She refused to lend her name to the empire's southern expansion, and wanted only to travel around Taiwan and taste its food. Settling in Taichung, she met a local Taiwanese woman, Chizuru Wang, who became her interpreter and guide. The two boarded trains and travelled the length of the island — Taipei, Taichung, Changhua, Chiayi, Kaohsiung, Tainan, Keelung, Fengyuan.[2]

The book's twelve chapters are each named for one of the twelve dishes the two women eat along the way: sunflower seeds, mi-sha-mu rice noodles, ma-yi soup, sashimi, braised minced pork, winter melon tea, curry, sukiyaki, leftover stew, dou-mian noodles, savoury sponge cake, and shaved ice with red beans.[2] Every dish is a station, and every station is a test of whether these two women can cross the distance between their identities. Aoyama gradually falls in love with Wang and urges her to give up her arranged marriage and move to Japan with her; Wang instead resigns as her interpreter and ends the relationship. In the final chapter, Aoyama meets Wang again beneath an arcade, and the two sit side by side and share a bowl of shaved ice, sweet and sour at once.[9]

Chapter TwoTwelve Dishes, One Language

The cleverest thing about this book is that it treats food as a language. The two women come from different worlds, speak different mother tongues, and are divided by the identities of coloniser and colonised, aristocrat and commoner, "mainlander" and "islander." Those things cannot be talked about directly; but sit down over a bowl of rice noodles, and the conversation begins. Food brings people closer — and shows them exactly how far apart they are.[9]

The twelve dishes are themselves an argument. Sunflower seeds and rice noodles are everyday food; sukiyaki and curry are flavours the colonisers brought with them; winter melon tea and leftover stew are the tastes of a Taiwanese home. With every dish Aoyama eats, she learns a little more about the island — but the more she learns, the clearer the wall between her and Wang becomes. Food can be shared; circumstances cannot. Two people can split a bowl of shaved ice, but when it is finished, one returns to Japan as an aristocratic writer while the other stays in Taiwan to accept an arranged marriage. That is the book's cruelty: it uses the gentlest things to write about the things that cannot be crossed.

Chapter ThreeThe Translator, the Translated Island

"Translation" is the book's real subject, more central than love. Ever since her earlier work, Yang has been using Chinese-language fiction to "translate" Japanese-colonial society — reviving the perspective of young women in the colonial era to explore where Taiwanese identity comes from.[3] Taiwan Travelogue pushes that method to its limit: the author pretends to be the translator, and the translator is the author.

The device is not a trick; it is the novel's metaphor. In Japanese-ruled Taiwan, the "national language" was Japanese, while the island's people spoke Taiwanese, Hakka, or Indigenous languages — a colourful, complicated mix.[3] Wang is that island in miniature: she makes her living by translating, building bridges between two languages, and for that reason she stands forever in the middle, belonging to neither side. Her name pays tribute to Yang Chien-ho, Taiwan's first female journalist, while Aoyama's model can be traced to the Japanese writer Fumiko Hayashi, who toured Taiwan giving lectures in the 1930s.[2]

The two women share a name, like mirror images: both women of low standing, both lovers of literature, yet always separated by an invisible wall. Aoyama wonders why Wang always seems to wear a "Noh mask" — sweet, capable, charming on the surface, yet distant inside.[3] In Noh theatre, the mask lets the performer switch body and mind. For Wang, the mask is not acting; it is survival. In the coloniser's world, a Taiwanese woman must first learn to make others comfortable before she can keep her own place. Only at the end does Aoyama understand that her well-meant offer — inviting Wang to return to Japan with her and pursue an independent career — looked, from the other side, like another kind of arrogance.[3]

Chapter FourWhy This Book Won the Booker

The road to the Booker took six years. Published in March 2020, the novel won Taiwan's Golden Tripod Award for literature in 2021; the Japanese edition came out in 2023 and won the Japan Translation Award in 2024; the English edition, published by Graywolf Press in 2024, won the National Book Award for Translated Literature the same year; and in May 2026 came the International Booker.[2][4] Every step was pushed along by translation — a book about translation, made famous by translation, finally crowned for translation.

Before the prize, it had already sold more than 40,000 copies in Taiwan, with rights sold in 24 countries and sales past 10,000 in Japan, the United States, and Britain alike.[4] On the night of the win, Chunshan Publishing ordered an overnight reprint of 70,000 copies; after three emergency reprints, total print runs passed 110,000, and the book topped the literary charts at every major retailer.[8] By the end of 2026 it had taken the top spot on Eslite's annual bestseller list, ranking first in 15 counties and cities.[11] A novel about Taiwan in 1938 became the bestselling book in Taiwan in 2026.

Beyond the prizes, the book matters because it answers a question Taiwanese people have asked for a long time: who, exactly, are the Taiwanese? Yang says she writes to answer that question.[6] President Lai Ching-te called the book "Taiwan's most beautiful love letter to the world."[6] The phrase is a little sentimental, but the meaning is right: through the eyes of a Japanese woman looking at Taiwan, the book lets Taiwanese readers see themselves — an island that has been colonised, translated, and is still learning how to tell its own story.

Chapter FiveWhat the Book Does Not Say

Success that fast brings criticism just as fast. Some critics say the novel romanticises Japanese colonial rule and departs from the historical record — that it makes the coloniser too gentle and the suffering of the colonised too light. The charge is not entirely baseless: kind as Aoyama is, she still arrives on an invitation from the Government-General; clever as Wang is, her choices are still framed by her era. A novel can let two women fall in love, but love does not cancel colonisation.

But judging fiction by the historical record is a blunt instrument. From its first page, this book admits it is invented — the author does not exist, the travelogue does not exist, even the 1954 first edition is made up. Its aim is not to restore 1938, but to ask a question nobody in 1938 dared to ask: if genuine feeling arises between coloniser and colonised, is that feeling real, or is it another form of colonisation? The book offers no answer. It has Wang resign, the two part, and leaves the reader to think.

One more thing worth knowing: the final chapter did not exist in the first draft; it was added later.[2] In other words, the reconciliation over shaved ice is a gift the author gave her readers afterwards. Without that chapter, the book would have ended in parting; with it, the parting gains an echo. The addition itself reveals Yang's position: she does not believe history offers reconciliation, but she believes literature can give people one more chance to sit down together.

Questions Without Answers Yet

To be honest, the following two things still have no reliable answers, and this article will not pretend otherwise:

  • Total sales of the Chinese edition in Taiwan: "more than 40,000 copies" before the prize is the publisher's figure, and "print runs past 110,000" after the win is a print run, not sales. The two must not be confused, and the publisher has not released complete sales figures.
  • How much of the final chapter is invented: the scholar who wrote the recommendation only notes that the chapter was absent from the first draft and that the plot is "partly fictional," without saying which details were added, so it cannot be checked line by line.

EpilogueAfter the Bowl of Shaved Ice

What makes this book remarkable is not the prizes it won, but the question it changes. Taiwanese literature has long asked "who are we," and the usual answers are history, politics, blood. Yang offers a different answer: a bowl of shaved ice. Identity is not a note on a document; it is what you eat, the roads you travel, the moment two people sit down and finally speak honestly.

Once the book goes back on the shelf, what remains is not the plot but a single image: two women sitting side by side, sharing one bowl of ice. The ice will melt, the train will leave, colonisation will end — but that moment was real. That is, perhaps, all literature can do: not rewrite history, but leave a place inside it where people can sit down.

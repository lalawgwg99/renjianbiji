The Secret History of Gold: A 6,700-Year-Old Piece of Gold — Why Is It Still Rising Today?

Published in August 2025, this history of gold covers 5,000 years of monetary history in 16 chapters, all to prove one claim: gold does not rise; everything else falls against it. This deep read asks whether that claim is true — or only a story a gold bull tells himself.

PrologueA Piece of Gold 6,700 Years Old, Still Shining

In 1972, a hoard of gold jewellery was unearthed at the Varna Necropolis in Bulgaria. It is 6,700 years old, buried for nearly 7,000 years, and when it was dug up it was still as lustrous as the day it was buried.[5] That is the opening image of The Secret History of Gold, and also the whole argument: gold does not decay, so humanity trusts it forever. The English original was published by Penguin on August 28, 2025, 288 pages;[4] the Traditional Chinese edition, How Gold Drives the World?, followed on March 31, 2026, from Business Weekly Publications, translated by Hsu Ko-hsin, 129,361 words, priced at NT$500.[2] The author, Dominic Frisby, is a British comedian and financial writer who has written MoneyWeek's gold column for 20 years.[3] The book reached number 1 among gold-investment books on Amazon UK.[3] The cover says it plainly: "Governments lie, banks fail, but gold is always honest!"[2] This deep read asks: is that true, or only a story a gold bull tells himself?

The book runs 16 chapters across 5,000 years of monetary history, from colliding neutron stars to bitcoin.[5] Frisby's claim is made without apology: gold is not an interesting antique or a speculative trade, but the monetary standard against which everything else — currencies, wages, house prices, the cost of a pint of beer — should properly be measured.[5] This is not a gentle book. It does not pretend to be balanced, and it does not leave the reader to decide. As one reviewer put it, the argument is built on evidence, but it knows where it is going from the first page.[5]

Chapter OneWhere Gold Comes From: Neutron Stars, Myths and the First Metal

Gold is the first metal human beings ever used — tens of thousands of years before the Bronze Age, before iron, tin, or even copper, we were already using gold.[9] It was born in colliding neutron stars, present in the dust that formed the solar system, and it has not changed since: it does not rust, it does not tarnish, it is inert, and it is as good as impossible to destroy.[9] The book notes that all the gold ever mined is estimated at 216,000 tonnes, and that all of it still exists somewhere on earth.[1] The World Gold Council's figure is about 220,000 tonnes, two-thirds of it mined only since 1950.[8]

After the Varna gold, gold walked into myth and kingship: Alexander the Great consolidated his empire with gold coins, Rome hollowed out its empire by debasing them, and Aztec gold funded the Spanish Empire.[5][7] Frisby's way of writing history is unusual: he does not write a chronicle, he writes "the moments when gold was in the room" — every rise or fall of an empire has a shipment of gold moving behind it.

The numbers that best explain gold's nature recur through the book: one ounce of gold bought 350 loaves of bread in the time of the kings of Babylon, and buys more than 1,000 today; one gold dinar (about an eighth of an ounce) bought one sheep in the seventh century, and buys three today; four or five gold aurei (about three-quarters of an ounce) bought one fine linen robe in ancient Rome, and would buy a whole wardrobe today.[3] In other words, what one ounce of gold buys in food, clothing and resources is roughly what it bought 10 years ago, 100 years ago, even 1,000 years ago. That is why gold is called "the most honest measure" — not because it rises, but because it does not change.

Chapter TwoNewton's Two Faces: Alchemist and Executioner

The best-written chapter in the book is about Newton. From 1696 to 1727, Isaac Newton was Master of the Royal Mint, directing Britain's Great Recoinage, tying money to a scarce, tangible asset and laying the foundations of Britain's economic rise.[6] At the same time he was a relentless enforcer, hunting counterfeiters without mercy and sending some to the gallows.[6] The same man who legislated for sound money by day wrote alchemy in a locked room by night — he left more than a million words on alchemy, far more than on physics or mathematics, secretly pursuing the Philosopher's Stone and experimenting with toxic substances like mercury.[6]

Frisby writes about Newton not for the gossip but for the lesson: humanity's obsession with gold was never only greed; there is something deeper — the wish to turn base metal into gold, to turn the uncertain into the certain. Alchemy failed, but its heirs succeeded: what modern central banks do is essentially the same thing — creating money from nothing, only without pretending it is gold any more.

One detail from this chapter is worth remembering: in 1912, J.P. Morgan testified before Congress, "Credit is evidence of banking, but it is not the money itself. Money is gold…"[5] A banker saying that in Congress still reads like a verdict more than a hundred years later.

Chapter ThreeThe Gold Standard, the Gold Rushes, and One Sunday Evening in 1971

The middle of the book is a history of the rise and fall of monetary systems. The Bank of England is founded, central banks are born, and the gold standard becomes the global standard almost by accident; the California Gold Rush runs from 1848 to 1855, and San Francisco's population soars from 1,000 to 25,000;[6] the Bretton Woods conference makes the dollar the world's reserve currency, on the condition that it stays redeemable in gold at US$35 an ounce.[5] Then, in August 1971, Richard Nixon goes on television on a Sunday evening to announce that the dollar is no longer convertible into gold.[5] The age of the international gold standard ends there.[3]

Frisby's judgement on 1971 is merciless: from that day, the supply of money could be decided by governments facing re-election, and the bill was paid by everyone holding the currency. His evidence is a set of British house prices: in 1970, the average British house cost £4,000; in 2025, £283,000 — seventy-one times more — while average salaries rose only twenty-two times.[5] But priced in gold, the average British house costs almost exactly what it cost in the 1950s.[5] In other words, houses did not get expensive; the pound got cheap.

The war chapters are written just as coldly. Nazi Germany systematically looted the central banks of occupied Europe, and the first stop on invading a new country was always the central bank vault;[7] in April 1940, the Norwegian government moved its gold to Lillehammer hours before the Nazis reached the capital;[7] the contents of the Hungarian gold train remain largely unaccounted for to this day.[5] Frisby writes that gold "was silent witness to the horrors and ambitions of the Third Reich. It was a means to finance Nazi aggression, but also to escape it."[7] A currency that belongs to no one helps good people and bad people alike — and that is exactly the point.

Chapter FourWhy It Went So Far: The Year Gold Hit US$5,000

The timing of this book was almost made to measure. In March 2025, gold first passed US$3,000 an ounce; on October 8, 2025, it first passed US$4,000;[10] over 2025 it rose about 65%, its strongest year since 1979;[10] the 2025 average of US$3,442 was a record;[10] and in January 2026, gold first passed US$5,000.[10] A history of gold published in August 2025 saw the price climb from just over US$3,000 to more than US$5,000 within five months — readers did not need persuading, only a book to explain what they were already living through.

The second reason is the central banks. From 2022 to 2024, central banks around the world bought more than 1,000 tonnes of gold every year, the strongest three-year streak since the 1950s;[11] China has been the world's largest importer of gold for 25 years and its largest producer for 20 years, and it exports not a single ounce.[9] Central banks print money with one hand and buy gold with the other — that contradiction is the book's best advertisement.

The third reason is the author. Frisby is no academic economist; he is a comedian turned financial writer, twenty years into MoneyWeek's gold column, a host of two financial comedy shows on BBC Radio 4, with more than 17,000 subscribers to his Substack, The Flying Frisby.[3][4] He can turn an eighteenth-century idea like the Cantillon Effect into one plain sentence: the people who get newly printed money first profit most.[5] Readers are not only buying a history of gold, but a man who has been telling gold's story for 20 years.

Chapter FiveA Gold Bull's Confession: What This Book Leaves Out

But the more popular the book, the fairer it is to ask what it leaves out. First, it never hides its position. Frisby describes himself as a "right libertarian" and is an open gold bull;[8] one reviewer notes that the book does not pretend to offer a balanced assessment of gold versus fiat money and leave the reader to decide — it knows where it is going from the first page.[5] That is not a flaw, but readers should know what they are reading: this is a defence brief, not a textbook.

Second, the title misleads. Another reviewer says the book's only error is a misleading title — because there is nothing secret about this history.[8] The Varna gold, Newton, Nixon: all of it is public history. "Secret history" sells mystery, not new evidence.

Third, the China reserve numbers. Frisby doubts China's officially reported reserves of 2,200 tonnes and argues the real figure is far higher — but the reviews do not agree on the range: one cites 30,000 to 40,000 tonnes,[6] another 13,000 to 20,000 tonnes.[8] The same author's same estimate differs by more than double between two reviews. That number is not evidence; it is a guess — and part of the book's argument about Chinese gold rests on it.

Fourth, the yardstick is the conclusion. Measure everything in gold, then prove that gold is the most stable — the argument is circular. Gold pays no dividend and yields nothing; holding it costs the return you give up. And since 1971, under fiat money, global wealth and living standards have risen enormously; blaming everything on the monetary system is an oversimplification. Frisby's advice is: "Keep 10% of your money in gold and hope it never goes up."[6] That sentence is honest — it admits gold is not for making money but for buying insurance. But when a 288-page book argues its way to "buy insurance" in the end, the reader is entitled to ask: what were the empires and the wars in the previous 287 pages for?

Questions Without Answers Yet

To be honest, the following three things still have no reliable answers, and this article will not pretend otherwise:

  • China's actual gold reserves: the official figure is 2,200 tonnes, while Frisby's estimates range from 13,000 to 40,000 tonnes — a range that differs by more than double and cannot be checked, so this article endorses none of the numbers.
  • Where the gold price goes after 2026: after first passing US$5,000 in January 2026, gold fell back sharply; whether it keeps rising or reverts to the mean, nobody can predict, and this article will not try.
  • Actual sales of the Traditional Chinese edition in Taiwan: the publisher has not disclosed them, and they cannot be checked.

EpilogueThe Most Honest Measure Can Also Measure the Wrong Thing

When the book is closed, three distinctions should remain. First, gold and fiat money must be separated: gold is indeed one of the most honest measures — it does not rust, it does not tarnish, it does not obey governments. But an honest yardstick is not the same as good money; a ruler can measure that house prices rose seventy-one times, but it cannot measure why some people can afford a house and others cannot.

Second, stories and evidence must be separated: the Varna gold, Newton's gallows, the Norwegian gold train — these stories are all true, and all beautifully told. But beautiful stories added together do not make a complete monetary theory. Frisby is at his strongest telling stories, and at his weakest when he mistakes stories for proof.

Third, insurance and investment must be separated: keep 10% of your money in gold and hope it never goes up — that sentence is right. But it is a sentence about fear, not about getting rich. Gold will not make you wealthy; it will only make you poor a little more slowly when the time comes.

This book is worth reading, not because it proves gold is the answer, but because it asks the right question: when money can be printed without limit, what is it that cannot be printed? The answer is not necessarily gold. But the question matters more than the answer.

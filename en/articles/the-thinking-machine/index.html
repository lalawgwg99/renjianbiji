The Thinking Machine: From a Denny's Booth to Five Trillion Dollars — and a Driver Change at the Top

Published in April 2025, Stephen Witt's Nvidia biography won the FT and Schroders Business Book of the Year. Nineteen months later, its subject resigned as chief executive. This deep read does not chase gossip; it asks what Jensen Huang got right — and which of the very things he got right came back to bite him in November 2026.

PrologueOne Biography, Two Endings

On April 8, 2025, Viking, an imprint of Penguin, published a 272-page biography: The Thinking Machine: Jensen Huang, Nvidia and the World's Most Coveted Microchip.[1] It is about Jensen Huang and Nvidia. Seven months later, on December 3, 2025, it won the FT and Schroders Business Book of the Year;[9] in the same year it was named a book of the year by The Economist and The Sunday Times.[10] The Traditional Chinese edition arrived even earlier: Commonwealth Publishing released The Jensen Huang Story on January 20, 2025, ahead of the English edition, and followed with an expanded bestseller edition on January 6, 2026.[2] One biography, two editions, two endings — because on November 12, 2026, the man in the book resigned as chief executive.[5] This deep read does not chase gossip. It asks one question: what did Jensen Huang get right — and which of the very things he got right came back to bite him in November 2026?

The author, Stephen Witt, is not new to this kind of book. His previous work, How Music Got Free, was shortlisted for the LA Times Book Prize, the J. Anthony Lukas Prize, and the FT/McKinsey Business Book of the Year.[4] For this book he interviewed Huang six times, along with his family, friends and colleagues.[4] The Economist called it the second corporate biography of Nvidia, and the more critical and more readable of the two.[10] The New York Times review warned that, having read it, you would still be unsure about Huang's vision for the future.[4] Read after November 2026, that sentence looks like a prophecy.

Chapter OneFrom Tainan to Denny's

Jensen Huang was born on February 17, 1963, in Tainan — though the birthplace is disputed: Encyclopaedia Britannica says Tainan, Wikipedia says Taipei, and this article follows the former.[4] At the age of nine, in 1973, he was sent to a boarding school in Kentucky — a school for troubled boys.[1] From the age of fifteen, he washed dishes, cleared tables and waited on customers at a Denny's in Oregon, from 1978 to 1983.[1] He was also a talented table-tennis player.

This is not decoration in the biography; it is the key to the whole book. A nine-year-old sent to a foreign school for troubled boys learns survival before English: reading faces, remembering rules, finding his place inside a system. A teenager who spent five years carrying plates at Denny's learns a second thing: service — understand what the customer wants before you move. Witt's point is plain: the way Huang later ran Nvidia — flat, direct, always on the front line — came from here.

On April 5, 1993, Huang founded Nvidia with Chris Malachowsky and Curtis Priem, in a Denny's in East San Jose, with about US$40,000 in starting capital.[1] The company's name comes from the Latin invidia, meaning envy.[1] Choosing Denny's was no accident: it was the place Huang knew best, and the place where he learned to read people.

Chapter TwoOne Chip, the Whole Company on the Table

Nvidia almost died. In 1997, the RIVA 128 saved the company — before it arrived, there was only one month of payroll left.[1] In January 1999, Nvidia listed on the Nasdaq, and in the same year it launched the GeForce 256, billed as the world's first GPU.[1] In 2006, Nvidia launched CUDA, made public in 2007 — a platform that let developers write general-purpose programs on graphics chips.[1]

CUDA was the biggest gamble in the book. At the time, nobody needed a graphics chip that could run general-purpose programs, yet Nvidia spent years and a great deal of money betting on a market that did not yet exist. The payoff: in 2012, the AlexNet team used two Nvidia graphics cards to do the work of 16,000 of Google's processors — the deep-learning era began, and Nvidia was the only company that had shovels ready.[1]

Witt writes this chapter cold: he does not celebrate foresight, he counts the cost. The money CUDA burned, the people who left, the board's doubts — all of it is in the book. Foresight looks like genius afterwards; at the time it looks like stubbornness. The most valuable thing about this biography is that it never confuses the two.

Chapter ThreeHow the Thinking Machine Was Built

In June 2024, Nvidia became the most valuable company in the world. In July 2025, it became the first company in the world worth US$4 trillion; on October 29, 2025, the first worth US$5 trillion.[4] Revenue for fiscal 2026 was US$215.9 billion, up 65%.[4] Huang himself held about 3.5% of the company, had about 60 direct reports, and had named no successor.[1]

Behind the numbers is the way a machine runs. Huang's management style is the most quoted part of the biography: no layers of reporting, no fixed one-on-one meetings, every senior executive receiving the same information at the same time, decisions made in open debate. The method has a name — "flat". It also has a price — "nobody can take his place". Witt never says it outright, but the whole book implies the same thing: when a company's fate rests on one person's judgement, that is itself the greatest risk.

This chapter should also answer what "the thinking machine" in the title means. The machine is not only the chip; it is the whole system — the chip, CUDA, the supply chain, the developer community, and a chief executive who always believed the company was about to die. One line of Huang's management philosophy runs through the book: the company could die at any moment. That sentence kept Nvidia alive — and kept it running like a machine at full speed forever. And machines overheat.

Chapter FourWhy It Went So Far: The Most Celebrated Business Book of 2025

The prizes first. On December 3, 2025, The Thinking Machine won the FT and Schroders Business Book of the Year;[9] in the same year it was named a book of the year by The Economist and The Sunday Times.[10] The New York Times review called it a "lively biography… fascinating"; The Guardian called it a "great story".[4] The Traditional Chinese edition appeared in January 2025, ahead of the English edition, with an exclusive preface by the author for Taiwanese readers; the expanded edition of January 2026 added a new preface analysing Huang's strategy since Donald Trump took office.[2]

There are three reasons it went so far. First, it is about a company that is rewriting the world, not one whose story is already closed. When readers opened the book, Nvidia's market value was setting new records daily, and every number in the book could be out of date the next morning — that sense of "happening now" is the rarest thing a biography can offer.

Second, the author's touch. Witt is a journalist who made his name writing about music piracy, and he knows how to turn technology into plain language. CUDA, GPUs, neural networks — in his hands these do not become instruction manuals; they become one gamble after another.

Third, timing. In 2025, the whole world was asking the same question: where does the AI money actually go? This book offered an answer: to the people selling the shovels.

Chapter FiveNovember 2026: Where the Biography Runs Out

The thing a biography fears most is its subject rewriting the ending after publication. This book met it.

On November 8, 2026, Nvidia's board announced an independent investigation; on November 10, the independent directors hired an outside legal team; on November 12, Huang resigned as president, chief executive and director, effective immediately.[5] Colette Kress, chief financial officer since September 2013, became interim chief executive; Harvey C. Jones, a director since 1993, became the company's first chairman in 33 years; the board engaged Heidrick & Struggles to begin a formal search for a permanent chief executive.[5] The statement gave no reason for the resignation. Reporting linked it to an investigation into an extramarital affair and concerns about foreign intelligence — the resignation came just four days after the investigation was announced.[5]

What the market did next shows both sides of this company. On November 17, Huang spoke for the first time on X, admitting he had made a "serious mistake" in his private life, promising to cooperate with the investigation, and saying he would return as chief executive if the board judged it in the company's best interests.[6] The same day, Nvidia reported third-quarter results: revenue of US$115.8 billion, up 103% year on year, the first quarter past US$100 billion; net income of US$65.3 billion; data-centre revenue of US$107.6 billion, up 110%, about 93% of total revenue; fourth-quarter guidance of US$130 billion; the fiscal 2028 revenue growth outlook raised from 70% to 75%; and the Vera Rubin platform contributing nearly US$20 billion in its first production quarter.[6] The shares rose more than 4% after hours. But in the month before, they had already fallen nearly 29%, and the market value had dropped from about US$5.78 trillion in October to US$4.51 trillion on November 6.[5][6]

On the earnings call, chairman Jones said the board had unanimously accepted the resignation and had no intention of bringing Huang back.[6] On December 1, Huang is scheduled to face questions from three committees of the United States House of Representatives.[6] Lisa Su, chief executive of AMD, has publicly spoken in his support.[6]

Here is a question no biographer can answer: was Huang's way of managing — flat, direct, everything resting on one person — the reason Nvidia succeeded, or its greatest weakness? When the company was riding high, the method looked like genius; when it turned, the same method meant no successor, no buffer, and nobody to take the bullet for him. Witt's book describes the first; November 2026 delivered the second. The biography's ending is not written yet, and no preface to an expanded edition can predict it.

Questions Without Answers Yet

To be honest, the following three things still have no reliable answers, and this article will not pretend otherwise:

  • Whether Jensen Huang will return as chief executive: on X he said he would return if the board judged it in the company's best interests; chairman Jones said the board has no intention of bringing him back. Both statements stand, the decision rests with the board, and this article will not predict it.
  • What the investigation covers: the board's statement gave no reason for the resignation, and reporting links it to an extramarital affair and concerns about foreign intelligence, but the investigation is still under way and the specific facts are unconfirmed, so this article will not pass judgement on them.
  • Actual sales of the Traditional Chinese edition in Taiwan: the publisher has not disclosed them, and they cannot be checked.

EpilogueThe Machine Still Runs; the Driver Has Changed

When the book is closed, three distinctions should remain. First, chips and people must be separated: Nvidia's chips, CUDA and supply chain are a real machine that will not stop because the driver changed. The earnings prove it — revenue of US$115.8 billion, data centres at 93%: the machine's momentum is greater than any one person.

Second, foresight and luck must be separated: CUDA was a correct gamble, but a gamble is a gamble precisely because it can lose. Turning one winning bet into ten acts of foresight is the easiest mistake a biography can make, and the easiest trap for a reader to fall into.

Third, founder and company must be separated: "Huang is Nvidia" was praise in 2025 and a question after November 2026. Can a company worth US$5 trillion bear the weight of "the founder is the company"? The board's answer is in the statement of November 12; the market's answer is in the after-hours rise of November 17; the real answer is still to come.

The thinking machine is still running. Only now, the person in the driver's seat is no longer the person who designed the machine.

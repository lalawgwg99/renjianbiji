Counselling for Toads: What Ten Sessions with a Depressed Toad Can Teach Us

A 1997 British book uses a fairy-tale toad to act out psychotherapy; it sold millions against the tide of a pandemic — because it turned "what is wrong with me" into a story you can talk about.

PrologueA Toad Who Did Not Want to Get Up

There was a toad who lived in a grand house on the riverbank, with money, standing, and friends — yet who did not want to get up in the morning, see anyone, or eat anything. His three good friends — Rat, Mole, and Badger — worried he might do something foolish, and after discussion, they urged him to see a therapist. Toad went, met a heron counsellor, and began ten sessions of counselling.[2] That is Counselling for Toads — a small British book published in 1997, in which author Robert de Board packed transactional analysis into the body of a fairy-tale toad and sold millions of copies.[4]

The Traditional Chinese edition was published by Sun Color Publishing on January 26, 2022, translated by Chang Mei-hui, priced at NT$400.[3] In 2026 it ranked number 5 on Eslite's annual bestseller list.[9] What makes a book nearly 30 years old sell better after a pandemic? The answer is not the theory but the toad — it made the words "what is wrong with me" sayable for the first time.

Chapter OneA Patient Arrives from the Riverbank

Every character in the book is borrowed from The Wind in the Willows — Kenneth Grahame's children's classic, published on October 8, 1908, originally bedtime stories told to his son Alastair, and counted among its readers J.K. Rowling and Theodore Roosevelt.[11] In the original, Mr. Toad is a rich, showy, accident-prone figure; de Board lifts him out of the comedy, places him in a consulting room, and asks a question the original never asked: what if Toad had depression?

The author said Toad's experiences were distilled from twenty years of counselling practice.[5] De Board himself graduated from Selwyn College, Cambridge, taught at Henley Business School for years, and worked as a clinical counsellor for two decades. The ten sessions in the book are not a transcript of a real case but a teaching demonstration: they let readers sit in and watch what counselling actually sounds like.

The design is clever. For most people, psychotherapy is a black box: they do not know what is discussed inside, how many sessions it takes, or whether talking helps at all. Put the black box inside a fairy tale and readers dare to walk in. Toad asks every question we dare not ask: Am I ill? Will I get better? Does talking really work?

Chapter TwoThree People Live Inside Us

The theory the book teaches is transactional analysis, founded by Eric Berne in the 1950s. Its core is simple: three ego states live inside us — Parent, Adult, and Child. The Parent state carries voices learned from elders, split into Critical Parent and Nurturing Parent; the Child state is the child we once were, split into Adapted Child and Free Child; the Adult state is the present, rational, matter-of-fact self.[6]

Toad's problem becomes obvious through this lens: his Parent state is far too strong. A critical voice in his head repeats that he is not good enough, that he always does the wrong thing, that he is an embarrassment; his Child state has learned to adapt — to obey, to please, to never say no. His Adult state almost never gets a turn. Counselling, session by session, is the heron pulling Toad back into the Adult state: Is that true? Or is it an idea you learned as a child?

The book also unpacks more than twenty psychological terms: collusion, emotional intelligence, life scripts, psychological games. Written as a textbook, none of this would be read; placed inside Toad's story, readers learn while laughing. That is the book's finest teaching design: theory hidden in story, story carrying theory forward.

Chapter ThreeWhat Actually Happens in Ten Sessions

Across ten sessions, the conversation is not about Toad's childhood trauma but about patterns he repeats every day. In the first, Toad weeps that he is worthless; the heron does not comfort him but asks: is "worthless" a fact, or a feeling? In the second, they discuss why he always tries to please people; in the third, his anger toward his father; and in the sessions that follow, they dig one layer deeper, until Toad discovers that he was not born depressed — he learned to be depressed.

That discovery is the turning point of the whole book. Depression is not an essence but a learned survival strategy — as a child, learning to trade self-blame for forgiveness, withdrawal for avoiding conflict, pleasing for keeping relationships. The strategy worked in childhood and became a cage in adulthood. Counselling does not smash the cage; it lets Toad see that he put the cage on himself, and that he can take it off himself.

In the final session, the heron asks Toad: if ten is fully recovered, where are you now? Toad says seven. The heron replies that seven is enough — the purpose of counselling is not to repair a person to perfection but to give them the ability to walk on alone. That ending matters, because it states the truth about psychotherapy: the therapist is not a magician; change always belongs to the client.

Chapter FourWhy It Sold Millions During a Pandemic

The numbers first. The English original was published by Routledge on November 20, 1997, 164 pages.[4] The book has been licensed in 10 languages, topped Amazon UK's counselling category and Dangdang's psychology chart;[2] during the pandemic, the simplified Chinese edition sold more than 2 million copies in under a year;[5] worldwide sales are claimed at 3 million copies in one publisher's copy and 5 million in another's — both are the publisher's figures.[8] The Traditional Chinese edition appeared in January 2022 and was still number 5 on Eslite's annual bestseller list in 2026.[9]

Why did it sell against the tide of a pandemic? The pandemic locked everyone at home, and home produced two things: loneliness, and time to face oneself. Emotions once escaped through busyness suddenly had nowhere to run. Toad's story offered a doorway: a person can be depressed, can ask for help, can slowly get better — and asking for help is not shameful. For many readers noticing their own mental health for the first time, this book was the first step before walking into a consulting room.

There is another reason: its lightness. Depression is a heavy subject, but told as a fairy tale, the book does not feel heavy. Readers need not first admit they are ill; they only need to care about a toad. Only after finishing do they realise that Toad's story was about them all along.

Chapter FiveThe Limits of the Story: Ten Conversations Do Not Cure Depression

But the more successful the book, the fairer it is to ask: does its theory hold up? Transactional analysis does not sit comfortably in academia. One scholarly review notes that research evidence for the effectiveness of transactional analysis therapy remains scarce, with much of the writing consisting of opinion and small-scale case pieces, and little that the academic community would recognise as scientific.[6] Critics add that reducing the complexity of human beings to three ego states is itself an oversimplification, and that clinical anecdotes cannot substitute for empirical research.

A deeper criticism is that the story simplifies counselling. Real counselling carries no "cured in ten sessions" guarantee; treating depression often takes months or years, and medication, psychotherapy, and lifestyle changes are all needed. Toad gets better in ten sessions in the book; readers who take that literally may develop unrealistic expectations of real treatment — if ten sessions did not fix me, is something wrong with me?

To be fair: the author never claimed the book was a treatment manual. A paper studying the book argues that judging it by the standards of fiction is unfair; the author's intent was to lift the veil of mystery from counselling so that more people would seek help.[5] Seen that way, the book's value is not its theory but its ice-breaking — it turned "seeing a therapist" from something shameful into something people can talk about.

Questions Without Answers Yet

To be honest, the following two things still have no reliable answers, and this article will not pretend otherwise:

  • Actual sales of the Traditional Chinese edition in Taiwan: "global sales past 3 million" and "past 5 million" are the publisher's figures for worldwide sales; how many copies sold in Taiwan alone has never been disclosed, and cannot be checked.
  • The model for Heron the counsellor: the author said Toad's experiences were distilled from twenty years of counselling, but never said whether Heron was modelled on himself or on a colleague, so it cannot be confirmed.

EpilogueAfter Toad Leaves the Consulting Room

At the end of the story, Toad does not become a hero, nor does he invent any grand philosophy of life. He learns just one thing: when the harsh voice inside starts judging him again, he can pause and ask — is this true? Or is it an idea I learned as a child? The question is small, but it is a switch: from "this is just who I am" to "let me think about it."

That switch is what the book leaves its readers. It promises no cure and sells no miracle; it only demonstrates a possibility — that a person can walk into a room, sit down, be properly heard ten times, and walk out to carry on with his life. For many readers, knowing that such a room exists is itself the beginning of change.

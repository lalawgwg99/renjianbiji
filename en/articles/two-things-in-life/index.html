Life Has Only Two Things: A 2019 Book, Retitled, Reaches Eslite's Top 10 in 2026

A book from 2019, given a new title and reissued in October 2025, reached No. 10 on Eslite's 2026 annual bestseller chart. Its whole argument fits in one sentence: life has only two things — none of your business, and none of my business. This deep read asks why that sentence lands so hard in 2026, whether it is the same idea as Adler's separation of tasks, and how a book with no method sells at all.

PrologueOne Swear Word, Two Things

On November 17, 2026, Eslite Bookstore announced its annual bestseller chart for 2026. At the top was Taiwan Travelogue, followed by The Almanack of Naval Ravikant and Rewire — all three already covered on this site. At No. 10 sat a book with a swear word in its title: Life Has Only Two Things: None of Your Business, and None of My Business.[5][6]

The book has an unusual past. It is not a new book. Its original title was Compete with Masters, Don't Argue with Fools, published by Gao Bao on January 9, 2019, in 273 pages.[4] On October 22, 2025, the publisher gave it a new title and a new cover and reissued it as a "bestseller collector's edition": 288 pages, 92,155 characters, priced at NT$380, with a black cat on a yellow cover and the tagline "Don't put other people's opinions on your life's to-do list".[1][2][3] Within a year of the reissue, it reached No. 10 on Eslite's annual chart.[5]

The author, Lao Yang's Owl, is a post-1985 columnist from mainland China who built his readership online — a self-described cat slave.[1] His method is blunt: no theory, only stories; no method, only attitude. The whole book fits in one sentence, which is its title: life has only two things — none of your business, and none of my business. In Chinese the phrase is profane; that is the point.

Why does one swear word deserve 288 pages? This deep read asks three questions: why does that sentence land so hard in 2026? Is it the same idea as Adler's separation of tasks? And how does a book with no method sell at all?

Chapter OneIt Sells Because It Swears Accurately

Start with the chart. More than half of Eslite's 2026 Top 10 is about burnout and letting go: The Let Them Theory at No. 7, Atomic Habits at No. 6, Counselling for Toads at No. 5, Diamond Sutra in Plain Words at No. 8, The Café on the Edge of the World at No. 9.[5] Readers in 2026 do not want more knowledge; they want less inner friction. This book's title is itself a ready-made remedy: give back what was never yours to carry.

The second reason is the honesty of the swear word. Self-help books usually soothe; this one swears. "None of your business" is crude, but precise. It says out loud a feeling most people cannot voice: why should I be responsible for your opinion of me? Readers are not buying the argument; they are buying the relief of hearing it said.

The third reason is the timing of the reissue. The October 2025 edition took a 2019 book and gave it a 2026 title. The old title, Compete with Masters, Don't Argue with Fools, was about posture; the new title is about boundaries. Posture is for other people to see; boundaries are for yourself. The new cover still prints the original title, which is an honest reissue — old readers can tell it is the same book, and new readers see a one-sentence promise.[1]

The publisher's copy calls the author a "million-copy bestselling author" and claims more than 400,000 positive reviews online.[1] Both figures are the publisher's own claims, with no independent verification, and this article does not vouch for them. But the chart position is real: Eslite's No. 10 is a full-year chart drawn from all 22 counties and cities, in stores and online.[5] When the original edition came out in 2019, a Vogue Taiwan book-news feature described the emotional profile of the modern young — not smooth enough, not worldly enough, low in emotional intelligence yet highly sensitive — exactly the reader this book is aimed at.[8]

Chapter TwoTwo Things That Are Really One Thing

The title says life has only two things. The first, "none of your business": other people's judgements, expectations, and feelings. The second, "none of my business": my choices, my feelings, my responsibilities. The line between them is the boundary of a person.

Psychology has a formal name for this: separation of tasks. Adler argued that telling whose task a problem is, is where human relationships begin; The Courage to Be Disliked brought the idea to Taiwan — Japanese original in 2013, Taiwan edition in 2014 — and it is still on the bestseller charts.[7] Lao Yang's book and Adler's book ask the same question in two different languages. Adler says "separate the tasks"; Lao Yang says "none of your business". The first is a diagnosis, the second is a swear word; the first asks you to think, the second lets you feel good first.

The book's 22 chapters are 22 scenes from ordinary life, and each chapter title is itself a sentence: "A Life Full of Taste — Why Do People Dislike You?", "I Like You Best When You Shut Up", "The Less You Know, the More Stubborn You Are", "Allow Others to Be Different from You — and to Be Whatever They Are", "Better Hurt by the Truth Than Comforted by a Lie".[2] Read together, the chapter titles are the book's whole method: don't fight, don't argue, don't explain — take your energy back.

But one distinction matters. Adler's separation of tasks comes with a psychological system — inferiority, teleology, community feeling — every concept traceable and debatable. Lao Yang's book has no system; it is a collection of essays. Each of its 22 pieces is one story plus one conclusion. How good the stories are decides how good the book is — and stories cannot be verified by calculation.

Two things, in the end, are one thing: moving your attention from other people back to yourself. "None of your business" is the means; "none of my business" is the end. After you finish swearing at others, what remains is the thing the book actually wants you to do.

Chapter ThreeFix the Feeling First, Then Fix the Problem

The book's most complete story is in its free sample: Ms. Zhu. Driving on a highway, she is rear-ended; the back of her car is smashed. She climbs out, sets up the warning triangle, helps the shaking driver who hit her to the roadside, calls the police, films and photographs the scene — all while calming the other driver and analysing the force and angle of the impact, as if it were a physics experiment. Another time, a flight is cancelled; her colleagues rush to argue with the staff and smash things, while she sits quietly drinking coffee and books everyone on the next flight.[2]

Ms. Zhu was not born calm. She says so herself: "You haven't seen how much I've lost." In high school she scored 29 out of 150 in maths — on purpose, to spite her teacher. Cooking at home, she cut her hand; her younger brother laughed at her, and she slapped him. He cried for half an hour and has never been close to her since. Her conclusion: "When something happens, fix your feelings first, then think about the problem. If your feelings aren't fixed, the problem will definitely go wrong."[2]

This is the most valuable chapter in the book, and its point is plain: feelings are not the enemy; losing control is. The book uses a folk saying: "A beaten dog bites the chicken — taking it out on someone else." Scolded at school, you snap at your parents at home; wronged at work, you shout at your family; fighting with your partner, you take it out on the children. Feelings look for the softest place to land, and the softest place is usually the people closest to you.[2]

The book also offers an image: a person with steady feelings is like an apple tree, arranging its branches by nature's rules, growing leaves and flowers, and honestly growing apples. It does not compare itself with the tree beside it, and it does not dream of growing sweeter oranges.[2] The image works because it promises nothing grand — only that you will grow into your own shape.

One caution: Ms. Zhu's story cannot be independently checked. The people in this book are the author's stories, and readers have no way to verify whether she exists. That does not weaken the point, but it decides how to read the book: read it as a fable, not as reportage.

Chapter FourThe Garbage-Truck Rule and the Art of Shutting Up

The book quotes an internet saying called the "garbage-truck rule": some people are like garbage trucks, driving around loaded with disappointment, anxiety, irritation, frustration, and anger; when the truck is full, it has to dump its load somewhere, quite possibly on a stranger. The book's advice for dealing with a garbage truck is: don't fight, don't argue, don't get tough — stay away as much as you can.[2]

Its companion piece is a chapter title: "I Like You Best When You Shut Up." Shutting up on purpose is not surrender here; it is arithmetic: I won't waste my time on you, so I'll wait for you to shut up. The book also writes about forgiveness: "To live two more years, learn to forgive. Forgiveness is really just letting yourself off the hook."[1]

The most honest sentence hides in another chapter: "If you gave it everything and still didn't get better, that doesn't mean you committed some unforgivable wrong. Most of the time, giving it everything only makes sure you don't fall too far behind."[1] Self-help books rarely write like this. They usually promise that effort pays off; this one says the payoff for effort may only be "not falling too far behind". The sentence is not encouraging, but it is honest — and honesty is the most valuable thing in this book.

But shutting up and forgiving both have boundaries, and the book never draws them. When should you shut up, and when should you speak? When should you forgive, and when should you hold someone to account? Unfairness at work and harm inside a family cannot be settled with "none of your business". Some things are both other people's business and yours. The book draws the line too cleanly — clean enough to be a slogan. A slogan can feel good for one evening, but the next morning, the things you have to face are still there.

The garbage-truck rule has the same problem: it is an internet saying, not a psychological concept. Calling someone a "garbage person" is itself a label. The book uses it to teach you to walk away, but before walking away, you should ask one question: is he really a garbage truck, or just having a bad day? The book never asks that question.

Chapter FiveWhat It Gets Right, and What It Misses

Three things deserve credit. First, the diagnosis is accurate. Inner friction really does come from treating other people's business as your own. Other people's judgements, expectations, and feelings were never yours to manage; managing them is the friction itself. Second, the language is ruthless. "None of your business" compresses into one swear word what Adler needed a whole book to say. The swear word here is not vulgarity; it is the removal of pretence — it stops you from pretending to be magnanimous. Third, it sells no quick fix. It never promises you will become strong after reading it; it only says that giving it everything may only keep you from falling too far behind.

Three things are missing. First, there is no method. Twenty-two essays, no steps, no exercises, no checklist. After reading, you know which direction to walk, but not where the first step lands. Second, the stories cannot be checked. Ms. Zhu, the garbage truck, every story that makes you nod — the author is the only source. You can be moved by a story, but you cannot use it as evidence. Third, the line is drawn too cleanly. In real life, many things cannot be sorted into whose task they are: a parent's expectations, a partner's feelings, a manager's demands are both other people's business and yours. The book's two-way split feels good until it meets these grey areas, where it has nothing to say.

One more honest point: this is a reissued book. The 2019 contents were repackaged under a new title in 2025, and the cover does note the original title, so the publisher is being honest. But for readers who bought the 2019 edition, there is nothing new here — the new thing is the title, not the book.[1][4]

Questions Without Answers Yet

To be honest, the following three things still have no reliable answers, and this article will not pretend otherwise:

  • The book's actual sales in Taiwan: the publisher has not released a Taiwan sales figure; this article found no reliable number and will not estimate one.
  • Whether the people in the book's stories are real: Ms. Zhu and the other figures are the author's stories and cannot be independently checked, so this article does not vouch for them.
  • How the "more than 400,000 positive reviews online" figure was counted: it is the publisher's copy, with no independent verification, and this article does not vouch for it.

EpilogueAllow Others to Be Others

Near the end, the author writes: may you make peace with the world, allow others to be others, and be yourself. "After all, you are not a washing machine; you don't have to take responsibility for every bad feeling."[1]

The sentence is worth keeping. Not because it is profound, but because it says a hard thing lightly: you are not responsible for other people's feelings. A washing machine has to wash every piece of clothing; you don't. You only have to look after your own.

So the book's real title is not Life Has Only Two Things but Life Has Only One Thing: take your energy back to yourself. "None of your business" is the first half of the sentence; "none of my business" is the second. The first half feels good; the second half is what makes you walk.

After finishing this book, there is no need to rush off and swear at anyone. Ask yourself one question first: today, was there something that was none of my business, but I took charge of anyway? Put that thing down. That is the whole use of this book.

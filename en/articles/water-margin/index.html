Water Margin: 108 Outlaws, and a Dream China Has Told for Six Hundred Years

Driven to Mount Liang, the outlaws became saints — and then became criminals again. What the book really tells us is how a system manufactures its own rebels.

SlowFolio Editorial Team · Reading time 13 min

  • 1517In 1517, Martin Luther posted his Ninety-five Theses at Wittenberg, challenging the sale of indulgences and opening the Protestant Reformation; seven years later, German peasants took up arms, and Water Margin was taking shape in China.

PrologueA Book Without an Author

One hundred and eight outlaws gather in a marsh in Shandong, raise a great banner reading 'Enforce Justice on Heaven's Behalf', win battles for a few years, accept the emperor's amnesty, and die almost to a man. China has been telling this story since the 14th century — six centuries now — and has not finished telling it.

Start with something strange: the book has no author — at least, no author anyone can name with confidence. The standard editions credit Shi Nai'an, but who Shi Nai'an was — when he lived, where he came from, what he did — not one of these can be established from reliable evidence. The more widely accepted view is that Shi was the principal author and Luo Guanzhong handled the later editing, but that is only 'the more widely accepted view', not a settled fact.[1]

The date of composition is just as murky. The mainstream 'Jiajing theory' holds that the book took shape around 1524, because that is when other texts first mention it, in the 1520s; an earlier date, at the end of the Yuan and the start of the Ming, has been proposed, but the evidence does not hold up and the theory has not won wide acceptance.[1] The earliest complete printed edition we can still see dates from 1589, and the story's prototypes circulated earlier still, around 1400.[2] In other words, the book was not written in a single year: storytellers, opera performers and men of letters added layer upon layer over more than a century.

Nor is there a settled text: there are 100-chapter, 120-chapter and 70-chapter versions, each its own Water Margin. A book with no author, no date of composition and no definitive text became one of China's Four Great Classical Novels and was counted among the Six Books of Genius.[1] That is itself the first question worth asking: how can a book with no author outlive books that have one? Perhaps the answer is that it never belonged to any one person. It belongs to everyone who has ever told it.

Chapter OneWho Drove Them to Mount Liang

The story does not begin on Mount Liang. It begins at a dead end.

The historical Song Jiang really existed. In 1119, the first year of the Xuanhe reign of the Northern Song, Song Jiang and 36 companions rose in arms around Liangshan Marsh.[6] According to later accounts, the immediate spark was the court's decision to take the marsh into state ownership and tax the fishermen who lived off the lake, cutting off their livelihood; the larger background was the 'flower and rock' levies of Emperor Huizong's reign, when the imperial procurement office scoured Jiangnan for rare stones and flowers while men like Zhu Mian squeezed the people layer upon layer, the taxes heavier every year.[6] In those same years, Fang La also rose in Jiangnan, at one point taking 6 prefectures and 52 counties.[6] Their names were written into the History of the Song, completed in 1345,[3] but everything the History says about Song Jiang amounts to only a few dozen characters — and a few dozen characters were enough for six hundred years of retelling.[4]

The novel turns those few dozen characters into the fates of 108 men. The most pitilessly written is Lin Chong. Lin Chong was an instructor of the imperial guards — 800,000 of them, as the book has it — a man with a family, a career and respectability, lacking nothing except a little luck. The son of the powerful minister Gao Qiu set his eyes on Lin's wife, and one frame-up followed another: a sword carried by mistake into the White Tiger Hall, exile to Cangzhou, assassins on the road at Wild Boar Forest, a fire set at the fodder depot. Each time, Lin Chong tried to endure, to submit, to step back. He stepped back until there was nowhere left to step, and on a snowy night he took up his sword, killed his enemies and fled to Mount Liang.[1] What makes Lin Chong's story so good is that he never set out to rebel. He was pushed down the mountain one incident at a time.

Wu Song is the same. The tiger-slaying hero returns to Yanggu county to find his elder brother Wu Dalang poisoned, his sister-in-law Pan Jinlian having conspired with Ximen Qing. Wu Song goes to the magistrate first, and the magistrate does nothing; he gathers the evidence, and still the magistrate does nothing. He kills Pan Jinlian and Ximen Qing, avenges his brother, and sends himself into a life on the run.[1] Wu Song's logic is simple: if the court will not uphold justice, he will uphold it himself. Readers cheer for him not because murder is right, but because in that situation there seemed to be no other road.

The word 'driven' — bi, to be forced — is the most precise word in the whole book. No one on Mount Liang was born a rebel. They all wanted, at first, to be law-abiding subjects: an instructor, a clerk, a hunter, a fisherman. A chain pushed them out: a word from a powerful man, an order from a magistrate, a petty clerk's obstruction, a trial that presumed guilt. Each link in the chain did only a little; together they made a dead end. That is how a system manufactures outlaws: it needs no conspiracy, only people following the rules — while the rules themselves are crooked.

Chapter TwoEquality in the Hall of Gathering

What truly draws people to Mount Liang is not robbery. It is the promise of a different world.

The promise is simple: big bowls of wine, big chunks of meat, and no questions about your birth. Down the mountain, everything turns on pedigree, connections and who your father was; on the mountain, only two things count: ability and yi — loyalty between brothers. A failed examination candidate, a fugitive officer, a framed instructor, a farmhand who has killed a man — on the mountain they are all good fellows.[1] In a society built on rank, the promise of 'no questions about your birth' is itself a temptation.

The promise also has a banner. Before the Hall of Gathering stands a great apricot-yellow flag bearing four characters: 'Enforce Justice on Heaven's Behalf.'[1] The wording is careful: it does not say 'avenge ourselves', nor 'overthrow the court'. It says 'on Heaven's behalf'. Heaven is the highest legitimacy there is. We are not bandits; we are Heaven's agents, sent to set things right. A man abandoned by the system needs exactly this story: I am not a bad man; the world is wrong, and I have come to correct it.

Everyone carries a Mount Liang inside — a place where you need not bow your head, beg a favour, or watch someone's face.

So Mount Liang is not a mountain. It is a dream. The dream is this: if there were a place where you need not pull strings or knock on back doors, where reason alone could keep you alive — how good that would be. The dream has lasted six hundred years because what it dreams of has never arrived outside the dream.

Chapter ThreeAfter the Seats Were Assigned

But the dream has an expiry date, and the date is the day the seats are assigned.

The turning point of the book is not the amnesty. It is the ranking. In Chapter 71, all 108 heroes gather in the Hall of Gathering and take their seats according to the stars of Heaven and Earth: 36 Heavenly Spirits, 72 Earthly Fiends, Song Jiang first, Lu Junyi second, and the rest in order down the hall.[1] From that chapter on, Mount Liang has an official seating chart — which is to say, a table of ranks.

The historical Song Jiang had only 36 men; 108 is a later invention.[4] But the novel insists on 108, because 36 Heavenly Spirits plus 72 Earthly Fiends add up to a heavenly number. Destiny makes the ranks sacred: the seats were not assigned by men, but by Heaven, and so no one may question them. Equality lasted only until the moment of the ranking; once the seats were set, the mountain had its chieftains, its junior officers and its foot soldiers — those at the table and those standing by it.

Mount Liang copied the very thing it rebelled against. Below the mountain, the court had its ranks; on the mountain, Liangshan had its ranks. Below, men pledged loyalty; above, men pledged loyalty too — only the object of loyalty changed, from the emperor to Song Jiang. Li Kui is the best evidence: killing the innocent without a qualm under the banner of yi, and no one on the mountain could restrain him, because his protector was Song Jiang himself.[1] Robbing the rich to feed the poor was selective too: whom to rob and whom to feed was the mountain's decision. This is not equality. It is a new set of men in charge.

This is the fate of every movement of rebellion: to fight a system, you must organise; and once you organise, you grow the same skeleton as the old system. Mount Liang was not defeated by the court. On the day the seats were assigned, it had already begun to become what it opposed.

Chapter FourAmnesty, or Going Home

Song Jiang never wanted to overthrow the court. He wanted the court to recognise him.

This is the most easily misread point in the book. In Song Jiang's 'loyalty and yi', loyalty comes first and yi second. He went up the mountain not to become emperor, but to wait for a letter of amnesty — for the court to take Liangshan into service and turn 108 outlaws into officials. For Song Jiang, rebellion was a means, not an end; the end was to go home, back inside the system, and be recognised by it as a good man.[1]

The amnesty came, and the price came with it. The Liangshan army campaigned in turn against the Liao, against Tian Hu, against Wang Qing and against Fang La, fighting one war after another on the court's behalf. Fang La was defeated, and the heroes died in droves; Song Jiang himself died at last from a cup of poisoned wine sent by Gao Qiu.[1] The most loyal man of all died at the hands of the very power he served. That is the whole fable of amnesty: the way the system takes you in is to use you up, and then dispose of you.

If Song Jiang had known the ending from the start, would he still have gone up the mountain? And knowing it, would he still have wanted that letter of amnesty?

The early-Qing critic Jin Shengtan made the choice for every reader: he cut away everything after the amnesty, leaving 70 chapters, and rewrote the ending as a nightmare — Lu Junyi dreams that all 108 heroes are beheaded, wakes with a start, and sees above the hall a plaque reading 'Peace Under Heaven'.[5] Lu Xun called it 'a dragonfly with its tail cut off.'[5] Yet the 70-chapter edition became the most widely read of all, because readers would rather stop inside the dream than watch what happens after waking. What Jin cut was not the plot, but the ending; he preserved the dream for his readers.

Chapter FiveOne Word — Yi — That Ruled for Six Hundred Years

In the end, the whole book comes down to a single word: yi.

In Water Margin, yi is not the law. It is a promise between brothers: when you are in trouble, I come; when I am in trouble, you come. The law sits in the hands of corrupt officials; yi sits in your own hands. When the law cannot be relied on, people fall back on yi. That is why this book has lived in China for six hundred years: it offers a spare moral system, one to fall back on when the official system fails.

But yi has its boundaries. The yi of Mount Liang faces inward, not outward: brothers will die for one another, and the same men can be ruthless to the people below the mountain. Yi is, at heart, the loyalty of a small circle, not universal justice. It warms the people inside the circle and endangers the people outside it. The easiest mistake in reading Water Margin is to take yi for justice. Yi is only a substitute for justice — something people make for themselves to hold the place while justice is absent.

More people have used this book as a textbook than have read it. Storytellers, opera troupes and secret societies all borrowed its language, its banners and its ways of organising. 'Oppose corrupt officials, not the emperor' became the standard template of Chinese rebellion: the emperor is always good; only the men around him are bad; sweep the court clean and all will be well. The template's virtue is safety; its flaw is that it can never touch the real problem. The problem is not one corrupt official. It is the system that produces corrupt officials.

In those same years, Europe was living through something similar. From 1524 to 1525, the German Peasants' War broke out, peasants taking up arms against their lords and the Church;[7] and 7 years earlier, Martin Luther had posted his Ninety-five Theses at Wittenberg, challenging the Church's authority.[8] At the two ends of the world, the same generation was asking the same question: when those in power no longer uphold justice, what should ordinary people do? Europe's answer led toward the Reformation and institutional change; China's answer was written into Water Margin — go back inside the system and be recognised by it. Two different imaginations of rebellion, and the Water Margin kind ruled China for six hundred years.

EpilogueThe Marsh Is Still There

The waters of Liangshan Marsh dried up long ago. The site is still there in Shandong, and visitors can still go and see it.

But the marsh inside each of us is still there. So long as a society can still drive good people to a dead end, so long as there are people who feel that reason is useless, that begging is useless, that only the mountain offers a way to live, this story will still be told. Song Jiang is dead, Lin Chong is dead, Wu Song is dead, and no one even knows who told the story — but the story is not dead, because it was never about them. It is about us.

We do not read Water Margin to learn how to slay a tiger, or how to rob the rich. We read it to see one thing clearly: when a society leaves good people no way to live, good people will find a way of their own — and the end of that way is very often the same magistrate's office. See that clearly, and the story of Mount Liang is truly finished — not in the book, but outside it.

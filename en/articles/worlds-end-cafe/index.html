The Why Cafe: Three Questions That Have Sold for 23 Years

A cafe with no address, a menu carrying three questions — how did a book of only 35,701 words take number 9 on Eslite’s 2026 annual bestseller list?

PrologueA Cafe With No Address

In November 2026, Eslite published its annual bestseller list, and The Cafe on the Edge of the World stood at number 9.[6] The book first appeared in 2003, under the title The Why Are You Here Café, self-published by its author, John Strelecky.[3][4] Twenty-three years later, new titles come and go on that list, and this one is still there. Of the top ten, it has lasted the longest.

The book is thin. The Readmoo ebook edition runs to 35,701 words,[2] readable in a single evening. It has no theory, no data, no step-by-step list — only a cafe, three questions, and a handful of parables. This deep read asks: how does a book this thin keep selling for 23 years? What did it get right, and where does it say too little?

Chapter OneA Lost Man Walks Into Three Questions on a Menu

The story begins with getting lost. John, an advertising executive, sets off on holiday, hits a traffic jam, takes a side road, and drives further and further off course until, nearly out of fuel, he finds a small cafe glowing in the middle of nowhere. All he wants is fuel, a bite to eat, and to get back on the road. Instead, the back of the menu carries no specials, only three questions: Why are you here? Do you fear death? Are you fulfilled?[3]

He spends the night talking with Casey, the waitress, Mike, the owner and cook, and another guest who happens to be there.[5][3] The whole book is that one night. No chase, no twist, only conversation. John asks a question, Casey asks one back; he thinks he is asking about life, and she shows him he has not yet understood the question itself. It is a risky way to build a book: one evening has to carry everything, and it works only if the questions are sharp enough, not if the plot is twisty enough.

Each of the three questions does a different job. The first asks about direction: why are you here? The second asks about time: do you fear death? Death is not there to frighten you; it is there to remind you that time is limited, and limited things have to be chosen. The third asks about the present: are you fulfilled? Together they form a complete check — direction, deadline, and present state — and if any one of the three is missing, the answer comes out wrong.

Chapter TwoPFE: Giving “the Meaning of Existence” a Name

Casey hands John his first tool, an abbreviation: PFE, Purpose for Existing.[9] It is not a job or a title, but the thing that makes you feel most alive — what you would still do even if nobody paid you. The book’s claim is that once a person has seriously asked “why am I here,” that door is very hard to close again.[10]

To make the abstraction concrete, the book tells parables. The best known is the businessman and the fisherman: the businessman urges the fisherman to expand, go public, retire in comfort, and the fisherman asks, and then what? The answer circles back to the life he already lives — fishing, time with family, an afternoon nap.[9] Then there is the green sea turtle: it does not fight every wave, but lets the ones it cannot beat roll past, saving its strength for the waves that carry it toward open water.[10] The book also offers “green arrows and yellow arrows”: no daily choice is neutral; each one pushes you toward your PFE or away from it. Finally, it sets a seven-day experiment, asking the reader to actually try it rather than merely nod along.[10]

This is the cleverest part of the book, and also its most fragile. Clever, because it turns a philosophical question into a tool you can carry in your pocket: three questions, one abbreviation, a few stories, usable the moment you finish reading. Fragile, because the simpler the tool, the easier it is to misuse — Chapter Five will come back to that.

Chapter ThreeWhere These Ideas Come From

Strelecky was born in 1969 in the suburbs of Chicago, spent five years as a management consultant to large companies, quit in 2002, backpacked around the world for a year, and then wrote this book.[4] The Hawaiian coastline and the green turtle in it carry the shadow of that journey;[3] five years inside corporations taught him exactly what “everything is fine, but something is missing” feels like. The cover line says it plainly: life is not bad, but something seems missing.

The questions in the book are not his invention. Existentialism asked them long ago: human beings have no ready-made essence; meaning has to be made. The psychologist Viktor Frankl turned that into a school, logotherapy: the most fundamental human drive is the will to meaning, and meaning can be found in doing a deed, in loving a person, or in the attitude taken toward suffering that cannot be changed.[8] Frankl added that meaning is not invented but discovered.[8] What Strelecky did was pack these heavy traditions into a light story that someone who has never read philosophy can finish in one evening.

The shadow of Stoicism is there too: separate what you can control from what you cannot, and spend your strength on the former. The turtle refusing to fight the waves is that teaching in animal form. The English edition’s jacket calls the book “a Jonathan Livingston Seagull for the 21st century” —[3] the same recipe, a thin parable about breaking away from the flock’s way of living. That is not an insult. A parable has one advantage a theory book lacks: it does not argue with you; it only needs you to recognize yourself in it.

Chapter FourWhy It Has Stayed Popular for 23 Years

First, the numbers. After self-publishing, the author sold more than ten thousand copies across twenty-four countries in under a year,[4] reached the bestseller lists in Singapore and then Taiwan,[4] saw the French edition hit number 1 in Canada in 2009, and watched the German edition hold the number 1 spot in Europe for nine straight years (2015–2023).[5] In Taiwan it was number 1 in the 2025 inspirational category and number 23 overall for the year, number 7 on Kobo’s 2025 annual list and number 10 on Readmoo’s,[5] and in 2026 it took number 9 on Eslite’s annual bestseller list.[6] American companies such as IBM and American Express have recommended the book.[5]

The sales figures need careful reading. The same book carries different numbers in different editions: the 2025 Traditional Chinese edition claims 5 million copies sold worldwide in 45 languages;[2] the 2026 English edition claims over 6 million copies in more than 45 languages;[7] the 2026 Traditional Chinese limited edition claims 12.5 million copies.[5] All of these are the publishers’ own claims, with no independently audited figure to check them against. What is certain is the order of magnitude — millions of copies, 45 languages — not the final digit.

It has lasted not because of aggressive marketing, but because it sits exactly where two needs meet. First, the question is big enough that everyone has to ask it sooner or later: why am I here? Second, the answer is light enough to finish in one evening and retell to a friend. A book that does both becomes a kind of social currency: you do not even need to have read it to talk about it. And in 2026, Taiwanese readers are collectively fighting burnout,[6] so a book that asks “are you fulfilled?” is being picked up all over again.

Chapter FiveThe Price of Thinness: What It Gets Right, What It Lacks

But thinness is its price. In 35,701 words covering a single night, there is almost no plot, and the characters exist to deliver ideas: Casey always asks exactly the right question, Mike always has a story, John always has his revelation at the last moment. Readers expecting the density of a novel will be disappointed; this is an extended parable, not a novel.

PFE is the same. As a reminder it is powerful; as a method it is almost impossible to test. How do you know the PFE you found is real, and not just the fantasy you like best right now? The book’s answer is the seven-day experiment,[10] but seven days can test a habit at most, not the meaning of a life. The turtle metaphor is equally moving and equally fragile under questioning: many waves in life are not yours to choose whether to fight — rent, illness, caring for family do not spare you just because you go with the flow. The fisherman parable, too, is a retelling of an old tale, and the fisherman’s contentment sounds beautiful only on the assumption that he already lives by the sea and owns a boat.

The author seems to know this himself. The sequel, Return to the Cafe on the Edge of the World, moves the scene to a tropical island and turns from discovering meaning to practising it: in the ten years after the first book, he found that many people had awakened but still lacked the courage and feared failure.[11] That is the author’s own admission that the first book only walked half the road.

One more thing needs saying plainly: this book never promises to change your circumstances, only the way you see them. For some people that is liberation; for others it is evasion. If you have worked overtime for three straight months and your pay does not cover the rent, the problem is still there after you finish the book. The book is not wrong; the mistake is reading it as a cure-all.

Questions Without Answers Yet

To be honest, the following two things still have no reliable answers, and this article will not pretend otherwise:

  • The year of the first Traditional Chinese print edition: the Readmoo ebook edition is dated February 27, 2025,[2] but no reliable record was found for the year Sun Color Culture first brought the print edition to Taiwan, so no guess is offered here.
  • Audited sales figures for Taiwan and worldwide: 5 million, over 6 million, and 12.5 million copies are all publishers’ own claims,[2][7][5] they disagree with one another, and none is independently audited; how many copies sold in Taiwan has never been disclosed, and cannot be checked.

EpilogueAfter Leaving the Cafe

When the book is closed, what should remain is not the abbreviation PFE but the three questions. The book’s most honest design is that it offers no standard answer: why are you here? Do you fear death? Are you fulfilled? Nobody can answer for you, not even the author. Its value is not in the answers but in forcing you to stop — somewhere on the highway of life, there comes an evening when you have to admit you are lost.

If the book deserves one practical use, it is its seven-day experiment:[10] pick something you suspect might be your PFE, do it for seven days, and see on the morning of the eighth day whether you want to continue or feel relieved. The answer will tell you whether that is your direction or a passing impulse. The cafe’s door is always open, but the people who walk in still have to walk out again, back onto their own road.

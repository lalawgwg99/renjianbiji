The Wright Brothers: Twelve Seconds That Taught Humanity to Fly

December 17, 1903, on a beach at Kitty Hawk, a machine of wood and fabric stayed in the air for 12 seconds — after four years, a wind tunnel, three gliders and a wager nobody believed

PrologueDecember 17, 1903, 10:35 a.m.

At 10:35 a.m. on December 17, 1903, on a beach near Kitty Hawk, North Carolina, a machine of wood and fabric left the ground, flew for 12 seconds and 120 feet, and landed back on the sand.[2][3] At the controls was Orville Wright; watching from the sand were his brother Wilbur and five witnesses.[3] One of them, John T. Daniels of the lifesaving station, pressed the shutter and took the first photograph of powered flight in human history.[2]

Four flights were made that day. The last, flown by Wilbur, lasted 59 seconds and covered 852 feet.[2] Then a gust of wind flipped the machine over and wrecked it — the Flyer never flew again.[3] It flew for a single day, four times, less than two minutes in total. But what humanity had wanted to do for thousands of years was done in those two minutes.

This is not a story about "who invented the airplane." It is about three better questions: why did two bicycle repairmen get there first? Why did the first thing they spent four years on turn out to be a wind tunnel? And when nobody in the world believed them, what good was believing?

Chapter OneThe Problem Was Never Flying. It Was Control.

The Wrights were not the first people who wanted to fly. Before them, Otto Lilienthal of Germany had already made thousands of glider flights and died in a crash in 1896; Samuel Langley, secretary of the Smithsonian Institution, spent government money on a 52-horsepower "Great Aerodrome," which plunged into the Potomac River on December 8, 1903 — nine days before the Wrights succeeded.[6] Everyone was asking the same question: how do you get a machine heavier than air off the ground?

The Wright brothers' answer was that the question was wrong. Getting off the ground was not hard; what was hard was what happened afterwards. In 1899, they tested an idea with a kite of five-foot wingspan: twist the wings like wringing a towel, so that one side generated more lift than the other and the machine banked left or right. They called it wing-warping.[5] They were not building an airplane. They were inventing a steering wheel.

They soon grasped the key point: flying meant controlling three directions at once — up and down, banking left and right, and turning left and right. Wing-warping handled roll, a forward elevator handled pitch, a rear rudder handled yaw. That three-axis control system was their real invention.[5][6] On March 23, 1903, they applied for a patent — not for "a flying machine," but for "a system of aerodynamic control."[6][8]

This is the most important sentence in the whole story: the Wright brothers did not invent the airplane. They invented control. Everyone wanted to build an airplane; only they thought of control.

Chapter TwoTwo Hundred Answers in a Wind Tunnel

Between 1900 and 1902, the brothers tested three full-size gliders at Kitty Hawk.[5] The 1901 glider had a problem: by the accepted lift formulas of the day, its wings should have produced far more lift than they actually did.[6] They had two choices: believe the books, or believe their own eyes.

They chose their eyes. In the autumn of 1901, behind their bicycle shop, they built a wind tunnel about six feet long, and over two months they systematically tested some 200 airfoil shapes.[6][8] They built their own instruments and recorded their own data — experiments later called the foundation of modern aeronautical engineering.[5]

The wind tunnel gave them two things: correct lift data, and a kind of confidence — that even the authoritative numbers could be wrong. The 1902 glider used the new wing shapes and a new control arrangement, linking wing-warping to a movable rudder so that turns no longer ended in stalls. In the autumn of 1902, they glided down the dunes hundreds of times, and flying became, for the first time, something repeatable.[5]

Only then came power. No manufacturer could supply an engine light enough, so they asked their shop mechanic, Charles Taylor, to build one: six weeks, a 12-horsepower gasoline engine.[6] Propellers could not be bought either, so they calculated and carved their own — and their propellers later proved more efficient than any the experts had produced.

Chapter ThreeTwelve Seconds

The first attempt came on December 14, 1903. A coin toss gave Wilbur the first try; surprised by the sensitivity of the elevator, he over-controlled on takeoff, and the Flyer stalled and crashed.[5] Three days of repairs, and on December 17 they tried again.

At 10:35 a.m., Orville lay prone on the lower wing while Wilbur steadied it from alongside. The engine fired, the Flyer ran down its wooden rail, and lifted off. Twelve seconds, 120 feet, no more than about 10 feet high, a ground speed of 6.8 miles per hour — into a headwind of about 27 miles per hour.[2][3] Daniels pressed the shutter. Five people saw it.[2]

Each of the four flights went further than the last. The final one, flown by Wilbur, lasted 59 seconds and covered 852 feet.[3] Then the wind flipped the machine over. Those twelve seconds were later recognised as the first controlled, sustained powered flight in history.[2] But in the days that followed, almost nobody believed it.

The news of the first flight appeared in just four newspapers the next morning.[7] An airplane had flown for twelve seconds, and the world did not even look up.

Chapter FourThe Years Nobody Believed

Over the next two years, the Wrights kept flying, and kept getting better. In 1904 and 1905, they flew more than a hundred times over a pasture near Dayton; the longest flight lasted nearly forty minutes and covered about 24 miles, and the 1905 Flyer III became the first truly practical airplane.[8] But the U.S. government was not interested, and neither was the Army — its order did not come until 1908.[10]

The turn came in 1908. On May 14, Wilbur took their mechanic Charles Furnas up for about 800 feet — the first time a human being had flown as a passenger; later the same day, Orville flew Furnas two miles.[9] For the first time, the airplane had a second seat — and a second meaning. It was no longer an inventor's toy. It could carry people.

On September 17 of the same year, tragedy arrived. During a demonstration flight for the Army at Fort Myer, Virginia, a propeller broke and the Flyer crashed; the passenger, Lieutenant Thomas Selfridge, died that evening, the first fatality in powered aviation, and Orville broke his leg and several ribs.[10] Flying now had a price. The Wrights did not stop — they repaired the machine and kept flying.

Wilbur died of typhoid fever on May 30, 1912, at forty-five; Orville lived until January 30, 1948, aged seventy-six, and watched the airplane change from wood and fabric into metal and jet engines.[8]

Chapter FiveSixty-Six Years

From 1903 to 1969 is sixty-six years. On July 20, 1969, Apollo 11 landed on the Moon, and the astronauts carried a small piece of wood and fabric from the Wright Flyer with them to the lunar surface.[2] From a twelve-second hop over a beach to another world 384,400 kilometres away took a single human generation.

What filled those sixty-six years? In July 1909, Louis Blériot crossed the English Channel; in June 1919, Alcock and Brown made the first nonstop crossing of the Atlantic; in May 1927, Lindbergh flew alone from New York to Paris, 3,600 miles in 33.5 hours.[13][14] Then came airliners, jet engines, supersonic flight, and finally the Moon.

The Wright brothers foresaw none of it. They only wanted to solve the control problem. But once control was solved, the rest was a matter of time. Twelve seconds was a beginning, not an end.

EpilogueAfter the First Twelve Seconds

The Wright brothers' story is often told as a story of genius. It is better told as a story of method: find the real problem first, then run the experiment, then trust your own data rather than the authoritative numbers.

They had no university degrees, no government funding, no laboratory. They had a bicycle shop, a wind tunnel they built themselves, and a stubbornness: when the 1901 glider did not lift enough, they blamed the formula, not the wind; when the Flyer crashed on December 14, 1903, they blamed nothing, repaired it, and flew again.

In June 1903, Orville wrote to a friend: "Isn't it astonishing that all these secrets have been preserved for so many years just so we could discover them!!"[5] He had not flown yet when he wrote that. But he already knew where the secrets were — not in the sky, but in the wind tunnel, in the data, in one crash after another.

Questions Without Answers Yet

To be honest, the following two things still have no reliable answer, and this article will not pretend otherwise:

  • Supporters of the German immigrant Gustave Whitehead claim he flew a powered machine in Connecticut as early as 1901; but there is no verifiable photograph or reliable record, and mainstream historians still date the first controlled powered flight to December 17, 1903.
  • The telegram the brothers sent their father on the day of the first flight reached the newspapers with "59 seconds" mis-transmitted as "57 seconds"; the discrepancy remains a small footnote in the verification of the flight records.

At 10:35 a.m. on December 17, 1903, the world did not change. The newspapers barely reported it, the government ignored it, and even their father, receiving the telegram, probably did not know what it meant. But twelve seconds is twelve seconds: a machine heavier than air left the ground under its own power, flew under control, and landed at a point as high as the one it started from.[3] From that moment, humanity's history of living on the ground began its countdown.

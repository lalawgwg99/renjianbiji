The Wushe Incident, 1930: An Uprising in the Mountains, and the Price of a People

On October 27, 1930, six Seediq villages rose up at the athletics meet of the Wushe Public School; within two months, about 640 of the roughly 1,200 people who took part were dead. The following spring, the survivors were attacked again and forced to relocate to Chuanzhongdao. This essay asks: why would a people pushed to the edge choose a road they knew led nowhere — and how did one uprising rewrite Taiwan's colonial history and the fate of its Indigenous peoples?

PrologueBefore the Mist Lifted

On the morning of October 27, 1930, the mist had not yet lifted over the mountains of Nantou. On the sports ground of the Wushe Public School, Japanese settlers were setting up the annual joint athletics meet: children in neat uniforms, parents carrying lunch boxes, officials and policemen from far away seated in the viewing stands. No one knew that on the other side of the forest, some 300 Seediq warriors, rifles seized from police substations in hand, were already walking toward that field.[7]

After that day, the name Wushe could never go back to what it was. It changed from the name of a mountain settlement into one of the heaviest words in Taiwan's history. This essay is not only about an uprising that happened 96 years ago. It is about how an entire people, after losing everything, chose the fiercest possible way to carve their name back into history.

Chapter OneThe Forest Taken Away

In 1895, the Treaty of Shimonoseki ceded Taiwan to Japan. For the Han Chinese on the plains, a change of rulers was a piece of paper; for the Indigenous peoples of the Central Mountain Range, it was the beginning of a whole new order. The Government-General declared the forests state property and placed the tribes under its "aboriginal management policy": policemen replaced chiefs, police substations replaced tribal councils, and people needed police permission to enter the forest, hunt, or move.[1][4]

The Seediq were among the last to bow to this order. They lived around Wushe and along the Zhuoshui and Meixi rivers, divided into three groups: Tgdaya, Toda, and Truku. In their tradition, a man earned his facial tattoo by headhunting; the tattoo was the mark of adulthood and the credential for crossing the rainbow bridge to join the ancestors after death. Women wove, men hunted, and the rules of the tribe were called Gaya, the law handed down from the ancestors, above all else.[4]

Japanese rule struck precisely at these things. Headhunting was banned, tattooing was banned, hunting rifles were confiscated and registered; tribespeople were drafted to build roads, carry loads, and perform corvee labor for meagre wages, whipped or jailed for the slightest disobedience. Policemen lived inside the villages and governed not only public order but weddings, funerals, festivals, and daily life. For the Seediq, this was not only the loss of land and freedom. It was the loss of the way to become a "real person" — in their belief, a person without tattoos could not cross the rainbow bridge after death.[4][9]

Mona Rudao was the chief of the Mahebo village, the most respected man among the Tgdaya. In his youth he had been selected to visit Japan with a delegation of tribal leaders, and the Government-General once regarded him as a model of cooperation. But the other side of being a model was that he understood the price of this rule better than anyone: his people had been made strangers in their own forests, and even the laws of their ancestors had to answer to the police.[7]

This is the part of the Wushe Incident most worth thinking about. It was not a sudden riot. It was the inevitable fruit of a system of rule that had been running for 35 years. A regime can take away land, rifles, and festivals, but it cannot take away a people's memory of who they are. When the gap between that memory and reality grows too wide to bear, conflict stops being a question of whether, and becomes a question of when.

Chapter TwoA Cup of Wine

On October 7, 1930, the Mahebo village was holding a wedding banquet for Tado Mona, the eldest son of Mona Rudao. By Seediq custom, for the host to offer a guest wine is the highest honour; to refuse a cup of wine is to refuse the person.[7]

A Japanese policeman happened to pass the banquet, and Tado Mona stepped forward to offer him wine. The policeman, disgusted that the young man's hands were stained with the blood of an animal just slaughtered, struck his hand with a cane. Tado Mona hit him back on the spot. The next day, Mona Rudao came to the policeman's house carrying wine to apologize, and was turned away at the door.[7][8]

Under the Seediq Gaya, an insult must be washed clean; under the Japanese police system, striking a policeman must be punished. Two laws collided in the same mountain, and neither would give way. Mona Rudao knew what was coming: removal as chief, confiscation of rifles, perhaps imprisonment. He summoned the chiefs of the six villages and made a decision: rather than wait to be picked off one by one, they would strike first.[8]

This was not a moment of impulse. Twenty days passed between October 7 and October 27. In those 20 days, the six villages linked up, hid rifles, and agreed on a time and signals. The day chosen was the athletics meet, and that was no accident: it was the one day of the year when the Japanese of Wushe were gathered together in one place, and also the day their guard was lowest. A humiliated father, a broken wedding banquet, a cup of wine no one would drink — sometimes history turns on a small thing, the last weight on 35 years of patience.

Chapter ThreeOctober 27

At dawn on October 27, 1930, the warriors first attacked the police substations around Wushe, seizing rifles and ammunition and cutting the telephone lines. Then they rushed into the athletics meet at the Wushe Public School. The Japanese in the viewing stands became the first targets, and many were cut down on the spot. In the chaos, two Taiwanese dressed in kimonos were mistaken for Japanese and killed.[7][8]

That day, 134 people were killed, most of them unarmed civilians, including women and children. The news shook Taiwan, and it shook Japan too. The Government-General censored the reports while dispatching troops. On October 29, Japanese forces retook Wushe; the warriors withdrew into the deep mountains and kept fighting with the terrain and the rifles they had captured.[7][8]

Behind the numbers was a war so uneven it bordered on hopeless. On one side, some 300 warriors with hunting rifles and the forest; on the other, colonial police and army troops with artillery, machine guns, and aircraft, reinforced by pro-Japanese Seediq and neighbouring tribes fighting as auxiliaries. In the fighting on November 1, Basao, the second son of Mona Rudao, was killed; by November 4, the villages of the uprising had been overrun one by one. On November 28, Mona Rudao shot himself in a mountain cave; on December 8, his eldest son Tado Mona, who had taken over command, also took his own life. By mid-December, the suppression was complete.[7][8]

Postwar counts put the toll at about 640 dead out of roughly 1,200 Seediq who took part — and many of them died by their own hand, choosing suicide over capture and humiliation. It was the Seediq people's last choice, and the most painful line in the story. A people who had spent a generation learning to bow finally, in a single morning, gave all their bowing back at once.[7][8]

Chapter FourAircraft and Poison Gas

The means of suppression remain the greatest controversy of the Wushe Incident. That Japanese aircraft bombed the mountain areas is beyond doubt; whether poison gas was used has been argued for nearly 100 years.[6]

On November 3, 1930, the commander of the Taiwan Army wired the Army Minister asking for the urgent delivery of blister-gas shells, on the grounds that the rebels were hiding in steep, forested cliffs that were hard to assault. On November 5, the Army Ministry replied that, for reasons of foreign relations, the request could hardly be considered. According to historical research, the army test-fired three special shells on November 8 and used several hundred tear-gas shells for mountain guns on November 18. Other accounts maintain that blister gas was actually used.[2][8]

The testimony of survivors makes the dispute harder to settle. Awi Hepah, who fought in the uprising at the age of 14, recalled that what the aircraft dropped smelled strange, made people vomit, and blurred their vision, while his companions died one after another, their faces twisted and their skin rotting. Chemical weapons experts note that the use of at least tear gas is accepted by most historians; whether a more lethal gas was used remains unsettled to this day.[6]

A final verdict may never come. But one thing is certain: Wushe in 1930 was among the earliest battlefields in Asia where chemical weapons were suspected to have been used. And the willingness of a colonial government to turn aircraft and gas against its own "subjects" says everything about the nature of this suppression — it was not the restoration of order, but a war to uproot a people from their mountains. The Wushe Incident thus became the last large-scale armed Indigenous uprising under Japanese rule; after it, no Taiwanese Indigenous tribe would again confront the Government-General with the strength of a village.[4][6]

Chapter FiveThe Second Spring

The uprising was over, but the revenge had only begun. On April 25, 1931, the survivors who had surrendered — now called the "protected tribes" — were attacked by the Toda group, old enemies of the rebelling villages. A total of 216 people were killed. This is known as the Second Wushe Incident. Later investigations found that the attack took place with the acquiescence, even the instigation, of the local police.[8]

On May 6, 1931, the 282 survivors were forcibly relocated to Chuanzhongdao, along the middle reaches of the Beigang River — today the Qingliu tribal village in Ren'ai Township, Nantou. From then on, they lived under police surveillance, and even their festivals and movements had to be reported. The mountains of Wushe were still there, but the people of the mountains were gone.[8]

Mona Rudao's remains were not found in the cave until years later. On October 27, 1973, the 43rd anniversary of the incident, they were brought back to Wushe and buried at the Wushe Incident Memorial Park, where his bronze statue still stands before the monument. It took a man 43 years after his death to finally come home to his own mountain.[10]

And the name Seediq waited even longer. Under Japanese rule, the Seediq were classified as Atayal; after the war, the classification stayed. It was not until April 23, 2008, that the Seediq were officially recognised as Taiwan's 14th Indigenous people. From 1930 to 2008 — a full 78 years — passed before a people took back their own name.[4][10]

EpilogueThe Other Side of the Rainbow Bridge

In 2011, Wei Te-sheng's Warriors of the Rainbow: Seediq Bale opened in cinemas in two parts. With a budget of NT$700–750 million, it was the most expensive Taiwanese film ever made at the time. It carried the Wushe Incident into theatres, and into the first serious history lesson many Taiwanese ever had about it.[9]

But a film is not history. Mona Rudao was not a born hero; he was a chief cornered between two laws. The Seediq were not born warriors; they took up arms only after losing their forests, their rifles, their tattoos, and their dignity. What makes the Wushe Incident matter is not how terrible it was, but the truth about colonial rule it laid bare: when a regime treats a people's culture as an obstacle to be cleared away, conflict stops being a question of whether, and becomes a question of when.[1][4]

Today, before the bronze statue at the Wushe Incident Memorial Park, people lay flowers every October 27. The legend of the rainbow bridge is still told among the Seediq: only a real person can cross the rainbow bridge and meet the ancestors. The people of 1930 crossed that bridge in their own way. What we can do is remember why they had to.

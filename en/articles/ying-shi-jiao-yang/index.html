Eagle-Style Parenting: A Former Pilot on Teaching Children to Fly on Their Own in the AI Era

Hiram Kang, published by Cite (Fangzhi) on May 1, 2026, 272 pages; number 6 on Kingstone's new-books bestseller chart for the first half of 2026. A former commercial pilot, born in New Zealand and raised by a single mother, turns his mother's 80/20 bet and the cockpit's golden rules into a parenting book. This deep read asks: why did it take off? And is its promise — a life AI cannot replace — a method, or a dose of relief?

PrologueWhen Answers Start to Inflate

On May 1, 2026, a 272-page parenting book was published in Taiwan. Its author was a former commercial pilot who had spent 10 years in the cockpit. The title was blunt: Eagle-Style Parenting: A Life Algorithm That AI Cannot Replace. It carried no academic title and no psychology doctorate behind it, yet within two months it reached number 6 on Kingstone's bestseller chart for new books in the first half of 2026.[1][4] This deep read does not ask what tricks the book teaches. It asks a more direct question: when generative AI, for 20 US dollars a month, can answer faster, more accurately and more completely than your child, what exactly are parents afraid of — and why did this book land precisely on that fear?

The book opens with a sharp line: when answers start to inflate, teaching children to keep chasing the correct answer is itself a risk.[3] For a long time, parenting worked like a question bank. Effort, discipline and early preparation usually bought a predictable result, and we grew used to believing that finding the "right method" would carry a child safely along. But once generative AI arrived, many abilities that used to take years to build could be rented for a monthly subscription. Imagination has been made concrete, creation democratised, and anyone can produce anything — while it grows harder to judge what is worth producing. Hiram Kang's answer is that the hard part is no longer doing things better. It is making judgments when there is no standard answer at all.[7]

Chapter OneHis Mother's Bet

Hiram Kang was born in New Zealand, raised by a single mother, flew commercial aircraft for 10 years, and now runs the "Eaglish Family" with his wife Queenie and three children, followed by more than a million people across platforms.[3]The whole book begins not in a cockpit but around a round table at a family dinner. In 1996, when Hiram was 6, his mother bought him a computer. The operating system was MS-DOS, driven by typed commands, and she also bought him the game The Legend of Sword and Fairy and taught him to go online through dial-up to look for information. For that era, it was parenting far ahead of its time.[7] But what truly set his mother apart was her attitude to grades. She never demanded 100 marks. She set one rule: "80 is enough."[7]

80 is responsibility — the floor that keeps you alive. The remaining 20 is for exploring life, for crashing into things and finding the ceiling of your own value. That was his mother's version of the 80/20 rule. Hiram lived by it: full marks in mathematics on the national exam, yet he chose the English department of a five-year junior college; after graduation he transferred to Tamkang University's international business programme; he volunteered for the special forces paratroopers; after discharge he worked his way into becoming a commercial pilot.[7] Had every hour of his youth been squeezed into "you must score 100," he would probably not have this life of many detours that still adds up to a whole. He would have been an exam machine, good at standard answers and unsure of who he was.

His mother placed an even bigger bet on something else. Just before Hiram turned 20, he fell in love with the stock market and began studying futures and options. Under the law at the time, anyone under 20 needed a guardian's consent to open a derivatives account. His mother not only agreed to open it — she wired him 40,000 New Taiwan dollars and told him to go and experience it for himself, adding plainly: "I will support every decision you make, because this is your life. You have to experience it and fail for yourself before you learn anything. If you succeed, of course I will be glad — but I actually know you will fail, and I look forward to the growth you will find after failing."[7] He did lose the 40,000 dollars in futures and options, as tuition. Then, by luck, he drew two shares in a new listing at a wildly inflated premium and sold them at the top on listing day, winning the 40,000 back. On paper the round broke even. But for the first time he understood clearly that someone was willing to spend real money to let him act on an impulse to try something wild, just so he could fall down and stand up again by himself. That bet became the starting point of the book's "positive-sum thinking": his mother traded her own security for a little more possibility in his future, and those 20 points of self-exploration might return more than 100 points of value to a life.[7]

Chapter TwoThe Golden Rules of the Cockpit

If his mother's bet is the emotional starting point of the book, the cockpit is its methodological one. Aviation has a set of golden rules: Aviate — keep the aircraft stable, so it neither stalls nor hits the ground; Navigate — know where you are, where you are going, and what terrain to avoid; Communicate — only then talk to air traffic control or the cabin and ask for help.[7] The order cannot change: steady yourself first, decide direction second, ask for help last. Hiram carries these three steps, unchanged, into parenting. When a child is in trouble, a parent's first move is not to step in at once or lecture immediately, but to steady their own emotions first.

The most convincing demonstration in the book is a true story about a smashed car window. On his first trip back to New Zealand, his birthplace, with the children, the family went up Mount Eden on the morning of their flight home to Taiwan to look over Auckland. In about 20 minutes, someone smashed the parked car's window and stole every bag — a camera holding 10 days of memories, a work laptop, and most importantly, their passports.[7] Queenie broke down in panic, completely at a loss. He stayed calm and started calling the police, contacting his company, and comforting his wife, asking her to help find the office that could help. Afterwards he broke the episode into three steps: steady the situation — confirm everyone was safe and take stock of the losses; decide the direction — go to the office for temporary passports, then ask the company for leave; communicate — calm his family's emotions and build a connection with the outside world.[7] If the captain loses his head, he cannot bring his family safely home.

The book applies the same rules to children. Failing an exam, quarrelling with a classmate, being criticised by a teacher — to a child, these are real small crises. If parents only rush to comfort or solve the problem, the child never learns to stand up alone. Instead, the book says, practise resetting: first guide the child to calm down, breathe deeply, and allow the feeling to exist without letting it take the controls; then help the child sort out what happened, what the choices are, and what they want to do; finally, encourage the child to say what they need — do they want help from mum and dad, or do they want to try alone?[7] This passage works because it has a source. Resilience comes from the Latin resilire, meaning "to spring back"; and the book defines resilience plainly — not a talent but an algorithm, the ability to keep order in chaos and judgment inside fear.[7] It adds one crucial line: resilience is raised by love. Research in psychology shows that children who grow highly resilient almost always have one steady, caring supporter behind them, giving them a deep assurance — no matter how well or badly you do, you are loved.[7]

Chapter ThreeThree Parts of the Life Algorithm

The "life algorithm" in the title has three parts. The first is the "value triangle": creation, awareness, love. Creation gives birth to value, awareness gives rise to meaning, love gives rise to connection; value is not one-off but fluid — once what is created is seen, it is exchanged, and the exchange flows back into new creation. This is the "value reflux" the book keeps returning to. What decides how far a person travels is not height but the speed of circulation.[3] The second part is "cold computing power" versus "hot computing power": cold power is what machines do best — optimisation and calculation, with answers getting cheaper by the day; hot power is what belongs to humans alone, and its engine has three layers — intention, cost, and attachment. Knowing why you act, being willing to pay for your choices, and keeping a real connection with people and the world — machines cannot do these three things.[3]

The third part is "positive-sum thinking," the choice of which game to play. Many parents see the world as zero-sum: time is limited, places are limited, resources are limited — one more minute of play costs one mark. So children are packed with cram school, talent classes and exams, always feeling that half a step slow means losing at some invisible starting line. But Hiram's mother left him those 20 points to try things wildly, get lost, and fall in love with things that did not look useful — and those seemingly wasted hours became the starting points of many turns in his later life.[7] Positive-sum thinking means not grabbing the same slice of pie from someone else, but making the pie bigger, so that every choice can flow back into new value.

Applied to the AI era, the algorithm turns into the book's sharpest chapter, Chapter 12: "Are You a Farmer or a Cow?" A farmer knows how to drive the tool; a cow is driven by the tool. In the AI era, the hard skill is becoming a farmer. Chapter 13 then lists "reverse parenting that raises cows," in five steps: make the child obedient; fill the child's schedule; make the child afraid of mistakes; make the child believe the world has only one standard answer; and blindly cling to tradition.[3] The five steps sting because each one names a Taiwanese family routine: obedience as a virtue, a packed timetable as security, mistakes as deductions, the standard answer as the destination. The book's warning is that children raised this way are exactly the ones most easily replaced by AI — because they only execute and never ask questions. Chapter 15 therefore proposes the "three-language era": the mother tongue is "who I am," a foreign language is "my connection with the world," and the language of questions is "what I can do with AI." Asking questions has become a third language.[3]

Chapter FourWhy It Took Off

First, the order of events: this book did not become famous and then find its author. The author was already known, and the book followed. Before publication, the "Eaglish Family" was already a family creator with more than a million followers across platforms, posting about family life, travel with children, married life and parenting on YouTube, Instagram and Facebook.[3][6] Readers first came to know a couple growing more relaxed with each of their three children, and a camper van, and only then walked into a bookshop to buy their book. Published on May 1, 2026, it reached number 6 on Kingstone's new-books bestseller chart for the first half of the year, on the same list as the film-tie-in edition of Project Hail Mary at number 5. A parenting book squeezing in among novels and exam guides did not get there on bookshop display alone. It brought its own traffic.[4]

The second reason is that the title itself is a promise. "A life algorithm that AI cannot replace" — six words aimed directly at parents' most concrete anxiety in 2026: what will my child live on? The book's answer is equally direct: for 20 US dollars a month, generative AI already answers faster, more accurately and more completely than your child; when answers start to inflate, teaching children to keep chasing the correct answer is itself the greatest risk of the AI era.[3] The opening works because it first admits the fear is real, then turns it into something actionable: instead of chasing correct answers, practise asking questions, making judgments, and taking responsibility. What buyers purchase is not only a method but a dose of relief — the anxiety finally has a name, and behind the name there is a road.

The third reason is language and story. His mother's bet, the smashed car in New Zealand, the 80 points, the farmer and the cow — every one of them is a story or a metaphor a reader remembers after hearing it once. The book has no academic tone; pilots' jargon, a Taiwanese saying about breaking a bone and growing braver, and small family stories take turns on the page. Add endorsements from paediatrician Huang Ying-ning, counselling psychologist Chen Chih-heng, parenting lecturer Wei Wei-chih (Zeh-ba), Hua Cheng-chun, Liu An-ting and clinical psychologist Wang Yi-chung, plus a feature by Parenting magazine, and a debut book by a first-time author arrived with a complete trust package.[3][5] Get the order right, and the amplifier works: first a family with a million followers, then a title that names the anxiety, then memorable stories and endorsements. Number 6 on the chart is the result, not the starting point.

Chapter FiveWhat It Gets Right, What It Misses

Three things are right. First, replacing "eliminating risk" with "managing risk" is the real thing. The most common trap in Chinese-language parenting, the book says in one line, is that we are used to eliminating risk and rarely learn to manage it. Since the Wright brothers flew in 1903, flying has always been tied to high risk; the risk never vanished — humanity used technology, institutions, standard procedures and a training culture to manage it, little by little, into a range we can accept.[7] Commercial aviation is now among the safest ways to travel, not because it is risk-free but because it learned to live with risk. Carrying that logic into parenting is the book's most defensible move. Second, the golden rules carry the weight of real professional training; they are not slogans the author invented out of thin air. Third, the 80/20 logic of delegation has evidence from one family — the author himself is the finished product, raised exactly this way by his mother.

The misses begin with evidence. Nearly every case in the book is a story from the author's own home: Zion and Zenith testing boundaries every day, making mistakes, standing up again; Zosia is still small, and her story has not begun. The sample is a single family, with no control group and no long-term follow-up, so readers cannot know how the method would work in another home, under different finances, or with a child of a different temperament. The book also cites almost no outside research for its central claims. The value triangle, cold and hot computing power, and value reflux are the author's own terms, and the book never explains how they relate to existing psychology or education research. What the reader gets is a self-consistent system of language, not a verified theory.

More concrete problems appear in the quotations and the numbers. The book quotes a rumoured remark by oil magnate John D. Rockefeller, supposedly made when he founded the General Education Board in 1902: "I don't want a nation of thinkers, I want a nation of workers." Yet the book itself admits, "whatever the true origin of this quote may be" — using a quotation of uncertain origin as evidence that industrial education was designed to serve the factory floor does not hold up as argument.[7] The same problem appears in the line that "driving is a million times more dangerous than flying": the preview gives no source or method of calculation, so readers can only take it as rhetoric, not as fact.[7] A book that teaches parents not to chase standard answers is not rigorous enough with its own numbers, and that is the most regrettable crack in the whole book.

Finally there are two deeper gaps. First, the promise "that AI cannot replace" cannot itself be verified. Nobody can guarantee what abilities will still be irreplaceable in 10 or 20 years; the language of questions is a third language today, and tomorrow generative AI may lower the threshold of asking itself. The book offers a direction, not an insurance policy, and readers who buy it as one will be disappointed. Second, the book never squarely calculates the cost of education outside the system. Travel, self-learning in a camper van, a mother's full-time support for a child's exploration — all of these cost money and time. In the foreword, Queenie asks the most practical question on readers' behalf: "Does education outside the system require a lot of money?" The book answers with an attitude, not a ledger.[3] Whether a family can copy eagle-style parenting depends not only on ideas but on the wallet and the calendar, and the book says too little about the latter.

To be honest, a few things still have no reliable answer, and this piece will not pretend otherwise:

  • Actual sales of the book in Taiwan: the publisher has not released sales figures, and no reliable total could be verified here, so none is estimated.
  • Precise follower counts for the "Eaglish Family" on each platform: public sources only say "more than a million followers across platforms," with no per-platform breakdown, so none is supplied here.
  • Long-term results of the family cases in the book: Zion, Zenith and Zosia are still growing up, and whether the method survives adolescence has no answer yet.
  • The source and calculation behind "driving is a million times more dangerous than flying": the book gives none, and no reliable source could be verified here, so it is not quoted as fact.

EpilogueTeaching Them to Fly

An eagle flies high not because it flaps harder, but because it knows how to borrow the wind. The book ends back in the cockpit: parents are not the captain — the child is the captain of their own life. What parents can do, while they are still present, is stay beside the child as they learn to face risk, make decisions, and bear the consequences.[7] Chapter 18, "Slow Strength," puts it most clearly: slow, because there is intention; steady, because one is willing to pay the cost; real, because there is connection.[3] When the world speeds up, slow is truly fast. It sounds like a motto, but inside the whole book it is really an echo of his mother's bet: keep the 80 points in order, and spend the remaining 20 on the books you want to read and the things you love.

The most honest line in the book is actually in its preface: it offers no quick fix and does not try to answer what you should or should not do.[7] Readers hoping for an instruction manual will be disappointed; readers hoping for a new place from which to look at their child will find it gives enough. The parenting anxiety of the AI era will not vanish because of one book. But one book can do one thing: help parents steady themselves first, decide direction second, and only then open their mouths to communicate. Steady, navigate, communicate — the pilot's order in turbulence turns out to be the parent's order inside anxiety too.

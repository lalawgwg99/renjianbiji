Rebirth: Press "Play," and the Dead Start Talking

Liu Tzu-chieh's first story collection in 12 years, published by Crown on May 4, 2026: 224 pages, 9 stories, from an antique remote control in a hot-spring inn in Atami to temples, funerals, and city life. On November 2, 2026, it won the Golden Tripod Grand Prize at the Taiwan Literature Awards, taking home NT$1 million; by late November it reached No. 10 on Kingstone's modern Chinese fiction chart. This deep read asks: why does a book about death make you laugh — and why, after the laughter, can't you laugh anymore?

ProloguePress "Play," and the Dead Start Talking

It is 4:50 in the afternoon, and the rain will not stop. Zhan Tian-ming has checked into a hot-spring inn halfway up a hill in Atami, alone, in a sea-view room booked for two, with dinner booked for two as well — but his travel companion, Yu Bo-heng, stopped replying to messages 30 days ago. Zhan picks up the remote control and discovers the television is a 14-inch CRT antique; the remote has one big round dial, a hollow triangle on top marked "Play" and a solid square below marked "Stop." He presses "Play" a few times, out of nostalgia, into thin air — and Yu Bo-heng's voice answers from inside the room.[2]

This is the opening of the first story in Liu Tzu-chieh's Rebirth. On May 4, 2026, Crown published this collection of 9 stories in 224 pages, priced at NT$300; six months later, on November 2, 2026, it beat 276 entries to win the Golden Tripod Grand Prize at the Taiwan Literature Awards, taking home NT$1 million; by late November it had climbed to No. 10 on Kingstone's modern Chinese fiction bestseller chart.[1][3][6] This deep read asks one question: why does a book about death make you want to laugh — and why, after the laughter, can't you laugh anymore?

Chapter OneIt Went Viral Because Readers Waited 12 Years — and Then It Won the Grand Prize

The numbers first. Liu's last story collection, Encounter, came out in 2014; Rebirth arrived 12 years later. In between she wrote novels, essays, and television drama — everything except a story collection.[9] The book was originally part of Crown's 70th anniversary programme, scheduled for 2024, but the manuscript kept slipping until the publisher's 72nd anniversary; its working title was Young Master, until one of its stories won the Yeh Shih-tao Short Story Prize and Liu had a flash of inspiration — she would let "rebirth" run through all 9 stories instead.[7]

The second reason it caught on is the prize. On November 2, 2026, the Taiwan Literature Awards announced that Rebirth had beaten 276 entries and 30 shortlisted works to take the Golden Tripod Grand Prize — her first nomination, and a win on the first try, worth NT$1 million; the 7 Golden Tripod winners and 3 Budding Talent winners each received NT$150,000.[3][13] Asked for a reaction, Liu laughed and said she did not know whether to feel ashamed or lucky, and called the judges "very brave" for letting the craft and the weight of the short story be seen again.[5] On November 15, the awards ceremony was held at Huashan Creative Park, where Minister of Culture Li Yuan presented the prize in person and Jan Hung-tze chaired the final jury.[4]

The moment the prize was announced, the book hit the charts. Kingstone named Rebirth one of its November 2026 staff picks, and when checked in late November it stood at No. 10 on the modern Chinese fiction chart — on the same chart, Huang Li-chun's Jie Jin sat at No. 16 and Yang Shuang-zi's Taiwan Travelogue at No. 1.[2][6]

But honesty first: the chart positions are real; the sales are not. The publisher has not released actual sales figures, and Kingstone's chart publishes ranks, not numbers. How many copies this book has actually sold has no reliable answer yet.

Chapter TwoWhat "Rebirth" Means

The title deserves a careful look, because the whole way this book is written hides inside those two characters. In Japanese, saisei is the play button on a video recorder — press it, and the picture moves again; dead time starts flowing. That is the button Zhan Tian-ming presses. He and Yu Bo-heng had agreed to travel together twice a year, once in spring or summer and once in autumn or winter, taking turns to decide while the other raised no objections; over ten years, from their first meeting at Takamatsu Station in Shikoku to the small islands of the Seto Inland Sea and Kiyomizu-dera in Kyoto, they had built a whole book of shared memories.[2] Yu married at 35 — his wife, Ryoko, had been a classmate in his university drama department; Zhan, 40, unmarried, an assistant professor of science and engineering, had never once made it through a family-arranged blind date. The two men had agreed to keep things a "clean travel-companion relationship," though their teasing constantly crossed the line — they had said everything shameless and perverted to each other, just never done any of it.[2]

Then Yu Bo-heng died. "Died in an accident, taken to hospital, could not be saved" — eight characters, and that was all. Zhan learned it from a Facebook post by Ryoko; counting from the day Yu stopped replying, that day was the 30th; counting from Ryoko's post, the 21st.[2] He went on the trip anyway — the journey had gone off plan from the very start, and going anyway, without cancelling or changing anything, was itself an act of stubbornness. In Atami he discovered that pressing "Play" really does make a dead travel companion speak, and pressing "Stop" silences him. Hasegawa at the front desk can see Yu too, and tells him: "Your friend is in the hot spring. He's waiting for you." Yu himself explains that after dying he arrived at a front desk, where someone asked him: "Would you like to check in, or check out?" He figured check out must mean logging out, and check in must mean reincarnation — so he said he would stay and play a while.[2]

The second meaning of "rebirth" hides in that check in, check out. The publisher's copy calls the 9 stories "check-outs and check-ins of a life" — someone has checked out, someone is still checking in; someone is dead, someone is alive, but the living have not really moved in yet.[2] Zhan is exactly that: he thought he had come to forget Yu Bo-heng, and only after pressing the remote a few times did he realise he had come because he felt Yu was "here." The third meaning is simpler: rebirth is not resurrection. It is a second chance — to meet again, to say it again, to live it again. The book was first called Young Master; it became Rebirth because the author realised all 9 stories were writing the same thing: when a person dies, the story does not end. The living have to find a way to finish saying what was left unsaid.

Chapter ThreeNine Stories, Nine Ways of Dying

The collection holds 9 stories, and from the publisher's copy, the trial read, and related discussions, 5 titles can be confirmed so far: "Rebirth, Ryoko, and a Haunted Hot Spring in Atami," "Good Night, Goodbye," "Young Master," "No Master," and "This Is Not Mine."[2][12] The Liberty Times laid the subjects out plainly: from a middle-aged man's gay travel companion passing away, to a student's affair with his teacher's wife, to temples, funerals, and city life — death changing every relationship it touches.[5]

"Good Night, Goodbye" is about an affair. Pin-yi sometimes comes to see "me" after Sheng-ming falls asleep, and returns home before Sheng-ming wakes. "I never felt guilty. News of illicit teacher-student affairs never seemed to have anything to do with me. But when I heard that Sheng-ming had died in her sleep, I felt an enormous guilt toward her."[2] The story also appeared in Novelist VOL.2 at the end of 2024, introduced as a piece that "uses finely observed everyday detail to explore the subtle relationship between love, loyalty, and betrayal."[11] One person's death finally lets another person see what they have done — Liu's favourite turn: death is not the ending. It is a mirror that shows the truth.

"Young Master" is about a temple. Grandpa translates the oracles of the gods; the father he never met was a great spirit medium. The young master, Chiu Peng-yu, is the god Emperor Ye's godson, yet senses nothing at all. Grown up, he learns translation himself, and finds that language feels like flowing water, like breath; but when he returns home for Grandpa's funeral, he still cannot translate to his mother what he and that William really are to each other.[2] A family that translates the words of the gods cannot translate the one sentence that matters most at home — that is Liu's humour: the gods know everything, except how to speak first.

"No Master" is about a countdown. On the day Chiao-yi receives a luxury apartment from her ex-husband, she also learns she has 6 months left to live; Sandra, the top-floor resident, is both an emissary of Ksitigarbha Bodhisattva and the mistress of the council speaker, and she invites Chiao-yi to a 100-day chanting of the Ksitigarbha Sutra — if you give your last 100 days to the Bodhisattva, surely you will reach heaven when you die, right?[2] Liu wrote this story in 2025, while she was in the United States for the Iowa International Writing Program.[13]

The writer Chen Hui put it best: "Rebirth is a meaningful, knowing smile. Every story has a dead person in it. It has nothing to do with horror. It is very much Liu."[2]

To be honest about the limits: apart from the first story, which has a published excerpt, and the other 4, which have only the publisher's quoted lines, the titles and plots of the remaining 4 stories have no public information to check against. This deep read will not invent stories for the pieces it has not read.

Chapter FourWho Is Liu Tzu-chieh?

Liu Tzu-chieh was born on October 20, 1980, in Tianwei, Changhua. She graduated from the journalism programme at National Taiwan Normal University and left a master's in Taiwan literature at National Tsing Hua University unfinished.[9] In 2003 she won the Unitas New Writer Award for her short story "Blindness," inspired by her own severe myopia; in 2006 her essay "Seven Days in Heaven" won first prize in the Lin Rong-san Literary Award, writing about the seven days of mourning after her father's death.[8][9] She has said that it was in November, 20 years ago, that this Lin Rong-san Award opened an important chapter in her creative life.[13]

"Seven Days in Heaven" later became a film, released on August 27, 2010, which she co-directed with Wang Yu-lin; it won Best Adapted Screenplay at the 47th Golden Horse Awards and Best Screenplay at the Taipei Film Awards.[10] From then on, readers knew her in only one way: she was "the Liu Tzu-chieh of Seven Days in Heaven." On the day she won the Grand Prize, she said she hoped that from now on she could become "the Liu Tzu-chieh of Rebirth."[7]

The 16 years in between were not idle: the story collections Dear Child (2013) and Encounter (2014), the novels For Real (2016), The Outside World, Free Game, and Hope You Are Here Too, the essay collections Here and Now, Transformation City, and Love to Write; in television she was showrunner for Date to Marry and Rock Records in Love, and wrote the film Salli.[2][9] She now lives in Taichung and teaches screenwriting at a university; in 2025 she was invited to the Iowa International Writing Program, where she finished "No Master," while "Good Night, Goodbye" was translated into English through a translation workshop.[2][13]

The starting point for Rebirth was an instinct, 20 years after her father's death: she must never write an essay like "Seven Days After Grandpa" and simply "use up" this material. How could she write it without betraying it, without falling short of it, without failing what she had heard with her own ears? Her answer: no essays. Nine stories, instead — letting death live a few more lives inside fiction.[2]

Chapter FiveWhat It Gets Right, What It Misses

Three things are right. First, restraint. The jury praised its "narrative that moves freely, mature and restrained, its structure never chasing a tidy ending, so that the story keeps fermenting after it ends" — at a time when contemporary fiction chases the scale and weight of the long novel, Rebirth shows another kind of depth and power in the short form.[3] The relationship between Zhan and Yu is written with a single remote control, a single pudding glass, and a single Facebook post — not one accusation, not one confession — and yet the reader understands everything. Second, the humour is genuinely funny. The Central News Agency wrote that she writes "with a Taiwanese sense of humour, shifting freely between the playful and the heavy"; Chen Pai-ching put it more sharply: "The stories in Rebirth are wildly unhinged and unafraid of the vulgar — Wong Jing's body, with Hou Hsiao-hsien's head."[2][3] A ghost film can play like a Lunar New Year comedy, and a comedy can turn out to hold a tragedy — a switch not every writer can make. Third, the title is exactly right. "Rebirth" is at once a play button, a reincarnation, and a check-in — one word threading all 9 stories together; as Chu Yu-hsun wrote, her fiction "keeps the craft for herself, and leaves the pure pleasure of reading to the reader."[2]

Three things should also be said plainly. First, it is too thin. 224 pages, 9 stories — just as the reader starts to feel the cut, the book ends, and anyone who likes the slow immersion of a long novel will find it leaves them wanting more. Second, and most practically: apart from the first story, the others have no published excerpts, so critics can only guess at the whole from the publisher's copy and a few quoted lines — this deep read included. Third, humour is a double-edged sword. After the laughter, does the grief get laughed away too? The Emperor Ye of "Young Master" and the Ksitigarbha Bodhisattva of "No Master" are gods every Taiwanese reader knows; written lightly, the reader smiles and moves on; written heavily, it risks blasphemy. Liu walks this line, and walks it well most of the time — but not every time. Fourth, death is everywhere. All 9 stories have a dead person in them, and a reader who is in mourning, or has just farewelled a loved one, will read this book very differently — that is not the book's fault, but it is worth knowing before picking it up.

EpilogueOnly After Goodbye Comes Rebirth

Liu herself has explained how the title came about: the book was first called Young Master, until "Rebirth, Ryoko, and a Haunted Hot Spring in Atami" won first prize at the 5th Yeh Shih-tao Short Story Prize in 2025, and the judge Kan Yao-ming praised it for "faithfully simulating reality, with a touch of the magical, re-creating the protagonist's survival and his summoning of the dead — a fascinating piece, worth chewing over again and again." In a flash, she decided to let "rebirth" run through the whole book.[2][7] On the day she won, she thanked the Emperor Ye who has long watched over her, and the jury's "guts" — the nerve to hand the grand prize to a short-story collection.[4]

The cover designer, Wu Chia-lin, said the cover's idea was that "memories, like silk threads, wind into circles — one circle not yet finished before the next begins, becoming a whirlpool of time, and a cocoon in the heart."[2] That sentence could stand as a footnote to the whole book: death is not a point. It is a thread wound round and round, and with every circle the living come a little closer to the dead — and a little further from themselves.

Zhan Tian-ming never pressed "Stop" in the end. He threw the remote onto a lounge chair and sank into the hot spring, thinking: counting from the day Yu Bo-heng stopped replying, today is the 30th day. Perhaps only when he can switch freely between play and stop will he be able to start again.[2] That is probably what this book is trying to say: only after goodbye comes rebirth. And goodbye is never said just once — you press "Play," say it again; press it again, say it again. Until one day, you can finally put the remote down.

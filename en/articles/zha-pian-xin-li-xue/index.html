The Psychology of Fraud: The More Certain You Are That You Won't Be Fooled, the Easier You Are to Fool

A fraud-prevention book by a hypnotist-magician from Kobe, published in Japan in 2017 and in Taiwan in 2018. Its core claim is one sentence: con artists don't steal your money; they steal your belief. This deep read asks how far a magician's word can be trusted — and why Taiwan lost NT$50.2 billion to fraud in 2024.

PrologueOne Sentence That Has Sold for Eight Years

On May 4, 2018, Cite Publishing released a 256-page book priced at NT$320. Its title is blunt: The Psychology of Fraud: Cracking the Psychological Techniques Used by Cults and Con Artists.[1] The author, Birdie, is a hypnotist-magician living in Kobe.[3] The Japanese original came out in June 2017 from Forest Publishing, with an even blunter title: Psychological Techniques Being Misused in the World.[6] A fraud-prevention book whose author is not a psychology professor, not a police officer, not a lawyer, but a magician who makes his living bending spoons. The first question this deep read must ask is: how far can a magician's word be trusted?

The book has sold in Taiwan for eight years and is still on the shelves. Its core claim is a single sentence: how can someone so educated still be defrauded? The truth is, the more certain you are that you won't be fooled, the easier you are to fool.[3] In 2024, Taiwan lost NT$50.2 billion to fraud, in more than 120,000 cases;[8] fraud losses grew from NT$8.9 billion in 2020 to NT$50.2 billion in 2024, more than fivefold in five years.[9] A book from 2018 reads more urgently in Taiwan in 2024 than in the year it was published. This deep read will not repeat the fraud-prevention rules; it asks what con artists actually steal — and why the people who say "I won't be fooled" are the easiest to fool.

Chapter OneCon Artists Don't Steal Money; They Steal Belief

The book's first sentence is really on the back cover: cults, fraud, fortune-telling, spirit possession, pyramid schemes, brainwashing — events that sound suspicious can be folded into everyday life simply by changing their names and descriptions.[3] Then, through media with social credibility, they spread and invade our lives as if it were only natural.[3] Birdie's point is plain: fraud does not begin when you hand over the money; it begins when you believe. The money is only the last step.

Chapter 1 states the book's position in its title: is it the supernatural, or is it a con? Are psychics really authorities on psychology?[3] Birdie's answer: everything in the world is hypnosis.[3] That sounds wild, but he does not mean that psychic powers are real; he means that psychic powers, séances and fortune-telling all use the same thing: suggestion. You don't believe in "séances", but call it "psychology" and you believe — change the name, and the same thing turns from superstition into science.[3]

There is a crucial distinction here. Birdie is not teaching you how to deceive people; he is taking deception apart: first find what the other person believes and get them to accept a suggestion; then dress the suggestion up as evidence with words like "statistics", "medicine", "authority" and "academia"; finally, once they start believing, they are hypnotised one after another.[3] Nothing in the whole process is false — every sentence is true, only the true parts have been selected and arranged in an order that walks you in by yourself. That is why victims, looking back, often cannot say which sentence was a lie: the most effective lies are mixed with truth.[3]

Chapter TwoThe Magician's Toolbox: Suggestion, Authority and Trance

Birdie's toolbox holds three tools. The first is suggestion. There are two kinds: direct suggestion — "you will feel very relaxed" — and indirect suggestion, hidden inside numbers and brand names. "Beware the indirect suggestion of numbers" and "Beware the halo effect of brands" are the titles of the last two sections of Chapter 1.[3] A number, a big company's name, is itself a suggestion: it decides for you before you have spoken.

The second is authority. Building rapport is the first step of a con:[3] first make you feel "this person understands me", then make you feel "what this person says can be trusted". The fortune-teller's patter is the best example: "Soon, you will meet someone good." "Soon, you will come into unexpected money."[3] The word "soon" never says whether it means tomorrow or months from now; because the wording is vague, it creates the bias that "this fortune-teller is really accurate."[3] That is not fortune-telling; it is linguistics. Vague words are always right.

The third is trance. Con artists all understand the "uncanny valley" — first surprise you, then put you in a trance, then never let the trance cool down.[3] A skilled magician knows how to gauge the uncanny valley, how to deepen it, and how to apply "deepening the trance" in business.[3] Birdie even uses a thermometer to explain how to deepen a trance.[3] A trance is not sleep; it is your attention narrowed down to a single thing — and that thing is whatever the con artist wants you to look at.

The three tools add up to one sentence: the most dangerous thing is subjective conviction.[3] Once you believe something from the bottom of your heart, suggestion no longer needs any force — you will finish the con artist's sentences for him.

Chapter ThreeThe Fraud Playbook: Four Steps, One Hypnosis

Chapter 3 is the most controversial chapter in the book, and its heading says "strictly forbidden to misuse": the fraud playbook — the techniques of deception being misused.[3] Birdie breaks a con into four steps: step one, find what the other person believes and get them to accept a suggestion; step two, sprinkle the words "statistics", "medicine", "authority" and "academia" into your sentences; step three, the more certain you are that "I don't believe anything without scientific evidence", the easier you are to fool; step four, once you believe, you will be hypnotised again and again.[3]

Step three is the most counterintuitive, and the most famous line in the book: the more certain you are that "I don't believe anything without scientific evidence", the easier you are to fool.[3] Why? Because such a person has a fixed defence: anything that sounds scientific lowers their guard. The con artist does not need to break the defence; they only need to make their words sound scientific — sprinkle in "statistics", "medicine", "authority" and "academia", and the defence opens by itself. A 2018 article in Business Weekly carried this very line as its headline: "So educated — and still defrauded?"[10]

Chapter 3 also covers the mechanism of "mind control" and "the automatic self-hypnosis system of religion":[3] a cult does not need to hypnotise you every day; it only needs to build a system in which you hypnotise yourself every day. Unpaid acts are all built on self-interest; never believe "the boss is selling at a loss" — these two lines sit in the same chapter for a reason: the most expensive things are free, because what they want is not your money but your belief.

Chapter FourWhy It Went So Far: In 2024, Taiwan Lost NT$50.2 Billion

The numbers first. According to the National Police Agency's 165 Anti-Fraud Dashboard, Taiwan recorded more than 120,000 fraud cases in 2024, with losses of NT$50.2 billion;[8] fraud losses climbed from NT$8.9 billion in 2020 to NT$50.2 billion in 2024, more than fivefold in five years;[9] by mid-August 2025, cases had already passed 100,000 and losses reached NT$56.5 billion, breaking the previous year's record before the year was out;[9] in the second half of 2024 alone, fake-investment fraud cost NT$41.6 billion.[9] A survey by the Global Anti-Scam Alliance and Whoscall found that more than half of Taiwanese encounter scam messages every week, and nearly 30% of victims pay or hand over personal data within an hour of contact.[9] In July 2024, the Fraud Crime Hazard Prevention Act took effect, requiring platforms to verify advertisers' identities;[8] but the numbers kept rising.

There are three reasons this book is popular in Taiwan. First, it is not about distant Japanese cults, but about what happens to Taiwanese people every day: fake investments, fake police officers, fake romances, one-page shopping scams. Readers open the book and see not case studies but their own text-message inbox.

Second, its author is not an expert but "someone who understands con artists". Birdie is a household name in the magic and hypnosis world, and many magicians learn from his lectures and teaching DVDs;[3] he is invited by major television, radio and magazine programmes, the most sought-after practitioner in the field, inside and outside the industry.[3] A man who earns his living by performing, teaching you how not to be fooled — that identity is itself the best mark of authenticity.

Third, timing. When the book was published in 2018, fraud in Taiwan was not yet as rampant as it is today; by 2024, every chapter of it had become the news. That is the fate of a fraud-prevention book: the more accurate it is, the longer it sells — not because the book is well written, but because the world has grown to resemble what the book describes.

Chapter FiveHow Far Can a Magician's Word Be Trusted?

But the more useful the book, the fairer it is to ask how much evidence it actually has. First, Birdie is not a psychologist; he is a hypnotist-magician. The claim that "everything in the world is hypnosis" is a magician's worldview, not the consensus of psychology. Modern psychology's account of hypnosis is far more complicated than that sentence, and the idea that "hypnosis controls the subconscious" is itself described in Chapter 5 as a fallacy.[3] A book that claims both "everything is hypnosis" and "hypnosis is not control of the subconscious" leaves a gap between the two sentences that it never closes.

Second, the techniques in the book come from the stage, not the laboratory. Terms like the "mirror effect", the "suspension bridge effect" and the "low-ball technique" genuinely exist in social psychology, but Birdie's use of them is a magician's use: he cares whether they work on stage, not whether they replicate in experiments. Readers should be clear: this book offers self-defence, not a textbook.

Third, and most worth watching: Chapter 4, "The Positive Use of Fraud Techniques: Unbeatable, Follow Your Heart", teaches how to use deception in romance and sales — "Strictly forbidden to misuse! How to hypnotise women like this", "Strictly forbidden to misuse! How to hypnotise men like this", "Just for a while, please fall in love with me."[3] Every section is marked "strictly forbidden to misuse", yet every section also spells out the method in full. Between self-defence and attack there is only the reader's single thought. Birdie himself writes in Chapter 5, "Hypnosis Should Be Destroyed", saying hypnosis has not changed in 130 years and that its "qualifications" can be bought with money.[3] A man who earns his living from hypnosis writing "hypnosis should be destroyed" — that is the most honest sentence in the book, and also the most contradictory one.

To be fair, the book also gets something right. It turns "being defrauded" from a moral problem into a technical one: you were not fooled because you are stupid, but because your brain has a mechanism that can be exploited. That shift is itself a liberation — it frees victims from blaming themselves, and it turns fraud prevention from "be careful" into "understand the mechanism".

Questions Without Answers Yet

To be honest, the following three things still have no reliable answers, and this article will not pretend otherwise:

  • The true total cost of fraud in Taiwan: the 165 Anti-Fraud Dashboard puts 2024 losses at NT$50.2 billion, but including underground money flows and unreported cases, annual losses are estimated at nearly NT$300 billion; the two figures use different scopes, so this article uses the dashboard's NT$50.2 billion and does not estimate a total of its own.
  • Whether the cases in the book are real: the cults, fortune-tellers and con artists in the book are mostly unnamed and cannot be independently checked, so this article does not vouch for them.
  • The scientific standing of "hypnosis": the mechanism of hypnosis is still debated in psychology, and Birdie's claim that "everything is hypnosis" is a magician's position, not an academic consensus, so this article will not rule on it.

EpilogueWhat Is the Ultimate Hypnotic Technique?

At the end of the book, Birdie asks a question: what is the ultimate hypnotic technique?[3] His answer is not in the table of contents; readers must turn to the last page themselves. This deep read will not spoil it. It offers only three distinctions.

First, belief and verification must be separated: what a con artist wants is not your money but your "belief"; the first step of fraud prevention is not to doubt everything, but to separate "believing" from "verifying" — trusting a person first does not mean handing them your money first.

Second, the language of science and science itself must be separated: "statistics", "medicine", "authority" and "academia" are a con artist's four favourite words, not because they are false, but because they sound true. Real science is not afraid of verification; what fears verification is always language pretending to be science.

Third, self-defence and attack must be separated: every move in this book can defend you, and can also be used to deceive. Birdie wrote both sides down, and the choice is the reader's. A magician's ultimate technique was never to make you unable to see the method, but to make you still willing to believe after you have seen it.

In 2024, Taiwan lost NT$50.2 billion to fraud.[8] That number will not shrink because of a book. But everyone who finishes this book gains at least one ability: before pressing the transfer button, to stop for one second and ask — what I believe right now, is it evidence, or is it a feeling? Stopping for one second is sometimes the whole difference.
